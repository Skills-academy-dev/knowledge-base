7 Study Tips

1.  Break Down Big Topics into Small Parts

HRM and organisational concepts can feel like a lot — from organisational structures

and workplace culture to recruitment, motivation, and performance management.

Don’t try to swallow the whole textbook in one go. Tackle one topic at a time, like

understanding how organisational hierarchies work or exploring Maslow’s hierarchy

of needs. Make sure each concept is crystal clear before moving on.

2.  Practise Applying Concepts to Real Workplace Scenarios

Many assessment questions will ask you to apply theory to practical workplace

situations — for example, choosing the best recruitment method for a vacancy,

identifying motivational challenges in a team, or analysing a manager’s leadership

style. Use the case studies and activities in your study guide to practise interpreting

workplace behaviour and suggesting effective HR solutions.

3.  Make Flashcards for Key Theories, Terms, and HR Functions

There are several important theorists and concepts to remember — like Herzberg,

Maslow, organisational culture, job design, and employee relations. Create

flashcards or hop onto apps like Quizlet. They’ll help you quickly recall definitions,

models, advantages, and processes — perfect for fast revision.

4.  Link Learning to Real-Life Workplace Practice

Think about how what you’re learning shows up in everyday work environments. For

instance, when studying motivation theories, consider what actually motivates

employees in organisations you know. When exploring recruitment, think about how

job adverts, interviews, and onboarding work around you.

5.  Test Yourself Often

After each module, try answering the reflection questions or activities without peeking

at your notes. This small-but-mighty habit helps you check what you truly understand

— and what needs another review before assessments come knocking.

6.  Use Your Study Guide for Quick, Focused Reviews

Before diving into a new chapter in your textbook, scan the study guide summary to

get the big picture first. It’s like getting the trailer before the movie — you’ll know what

to expect, and the learning will make more sense as you go.

7.  Stick to a Study Schedule

Time management is your superpower, especially as a distance learner. Create a

weekly study plan that feels realistic — whether that’s finishing one unit per week or

dedicating certain hours to reading, revising, or doing activities. Staying organised

helps you stay motivated, consistent, and totally in control of your learning journey.

2.1 Talent Management Models

●  Talent management models are frameworks that guide how organisations attract,

develop, and retain talent.

●  Each model supports different strategic needs, depending on the organisation’s size,

culture, and pace of change.

2.1.1 The Traditional Talent Management Model

Follows five stages: planning, attraction, onboarding, retention, and transition.

●  Strength: Simple and easy to apply.

●  Limitation: Not suited for organisations needing ongoing learning and innovation.

2.1.2 The High-Performance Talent Management Model

Focuses on high-potential employees through leadership development and succession

planning.

●  Strength: Builds strong future leaders.

●  Limitation: May overlook average performers.

11

2.1.3 The Competency-Based Talent Management Model

Aligns employee skills to organisational goals using defined competencies and

assessments.

●  Strength: Strong strategic alignment.

●  Limitation: Competency mapping can be complex.

2.1.4 The Integrated Talent Management Model

Links recruitment, learning, performance, and HR technology into one system.

●  Strength: Creates a unified talent strategy.

●  Limitation: Requires advanced HR systems and leadership support.

2.1.5 The Agile Talent Management Model

Enables rapid decision-making, continuous learning, and flexible career movement.

●  Strength: Highly adaptable in changing environments.

●  Limitation: Needs an open, agile organisational culture.

2.2 Evolving Models

●  Modern talent models emphasise flexibility, continuous learning, and diverse career

paths.

●  Employee-driven development replaces old hierarchical approaches.

7.1 HR-TMIS

An HR-TMIS serves as the organisation’s central database for all talent information used in

workforce planning.

It stores demographics, recruitment, performance, learning, compensation, and succession

data to support accurate, strategic decisions.

7.2 People Analytics

People analytics uses HR-TMIS data to uncover patterns, answer talent questions, and

guide evidence-based HR decision-making.

It transforms raw workforce data into insights that improve recruitment, development, and

retention outcomes.

23

onboarding, leadership, or cultural issues.

7.3 Predicting Future Workforce Needs

People analytics forecasts upcoming skill shortages, identifies future leaders, and models

the impact of HR initiatives.

Analytics evaluates training ROI, engagement levels, and diversity outcomes by comparing

trends over time.

It highlights whether development programmes, culture initiatives, or D&I efforts are

7.5 Empowering Informed Decision-Making

Data-driven insights replace intuition, helping HR align talent strategies with business needs.

Introduction

organisation’s current and future talent needs. In a world shaped by automation,

globalisation, shifting skill demands, and rapid technological change, workforce planning has

become a critical organisational capability. This module introduces the foundational concepts

UNIT 1: KT0201 — Definitions, Terminology and Concepts Related

to Workforce Planning

Workforce planning is the strategic process of ensuring an organisation has the right people,

with the right skills, in the right place, at the right time to achieve its goals. It is a proactive

approach built on key terminology and concepts that help organisations anticipate workforce

needs and develop a future-ready talent pool.

1.1 Workforce Supply

Workforce supply refers to all current employees and the skills, experience, and

demographics they bring.

It helps organisations understand what talent already exists and where strengths or

shortages may lie.

1.2 Workforce Demand

Workforce demand forecasts future talent needs based on strategy, growth, technology,

trends, and leadership changes.

It determines what skills and roles will be required to meet organisational goals.

27

1.3 Skills Gap

A skills gap is the difference between what skills the organisation has now and what it will

need in the future.

Hard skills relate to technical ability, while soft skills include communication, leadership, and

teamwork.

1.4 Succession Planning

Succession planning identifies high-potential staff and prepares them for future leadership

roles.

It reduces risk and ensures smooth transitions in critical positions.

Figure 19: Succession Planning Pipeline

Source: Research gate

1.5 Talent Management Strategies

Workforce planning informs strategies for hiring, developing, managing, engaging, and

retaining employees.

These strategies ensure talent is aligned with long-term organisational needs.

28

1.6 Human Capital Management (HCM) & ROI

HCM covers all processes for maximising workforce value, from hiring to development and

performance.

ROI measures how effectively HR initiatives deliver value, such as improved productivity or

reduced turnover.

1.7 Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation

Workforce planning must be regularly updated to stay aligned with changing business

conditions and employee expectations.

Annual updates significantly improve accuracy and effectiveness.

1.8 Strategic Workforce Planning Models

Two main models guide structured workforce planning: the HCI Model (customised and

detailed) and the OPM Model (broad and general).

Both help organisations analyse current talent, identify gaps, and build action plans for the

future.

UNIT 2: KT0202 – Evolving Models, Processes, and Application

Principles for Enhancing Workforce Planning

Workforce planning has shifted dramatically due to globalisation, technology, and

demographic change. Traditional planning methods based purely on historical data are no

longer enough. Organisations now rely on evolving models, dynamic processes, and flexible

application principles to stay competitive.

UNIT 3: KT0203 – Professional Practice Standards Relating to

Workforce Planning

Professional practice standards guide organisations in developing workforce plans that are

strategic, ethical, compliant, and effective. These standards ensure that workforce planning

is aligned with organisational goals, based on evidence, continuously improved, and

supported by ethical decision-making.

3.1 Strategic Alignment

Workforce planning must directly support long-term organisational goals.

This includes aligning talent needs with future strategy, setting measurable workforce goals,

and directing resources to priority areas.

3.2 Comprehensive Analysis

Standards require a full picture of current and future workforce needs.

This includes analysing supply (skills, demographics), forecasting demand (industry,

technology, growth), and identifying resulting skills or role gaps.

3.3 Data-Driven Decision-Making

Decisions must be guided by accurate workforce data.

Organisations use predictive analytics, KPIs like retention and time-to-hire, and HRIS

systems to forecast trends and improve planning accuracy.

3.4 Stakeholder Engagement

Effective workforce planning requires collaboration across the business.

HR, finance, operations, leadership, and employees all contribute insights to align plans

with real organisational needs.

3.5 Continuous Improvement

Workforce plans must evolve as conditions change.

Regular reviews, feedback loops, and updated data ensure the plan stays relevant and

responsive to new challenges.

3.6 Ethical and Legal Compliance

All planning must follow labour laws and uphold fairness.

This includes preventing discrimination, maintaining transparency, and ensuring HR teams

receive compliance training.

3.7 Risk Management

Standards require identifying workforce risks such as shortages, turnover, or obsolete skills.

Organisations use risk assessments and contingency plans (e.g., talent pools or reskilling) to

stay prepared.

3.8 Diversity and Inclusion (D&I)

Workforce plans must advance D&I priorities.

This includes tracking D&I metrics, ensuring unbiased hiring and promotions, and fostering

inclusive organisational culture.
