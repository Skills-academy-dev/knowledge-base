Data Management and Interpretation Are All Around You
It’s in every spreadsheet guiding a business decision, every database keeping customer information
safe, and every chart that helps teams understand what’s really going on. In simple terms, Data

● Understand how data flows in organisations
You’ll explore how information is gathered, organised, and maintained to keep a business

persuasively.

information responsibly. You’ll learn how to maintain integrity and transparency while ensuring
data is used appropriately and respectfully.

tools, communication, and decision-making — preparing you for careers in administration,
business analysis, data support, or information management.

decisions relating to recruitment, payroll, performance management, training, and compliance
all rely on accurate and well-managed information. This module introduces you to the
foundational concepts of data and data management, helping you understand what data is, how
it is used, and why organisations depend on effective data handling practices.
You will explore different types of data, such as qualitative and quantitative information, and
discover how data is collected, stored, processed, analysed, and reported within an HR context.
The module also highlights the importance of data governance — including the policies,

support organisational processes, comply with legislation such as POPIA, and contribute to

covered later in the programme.
Unit 1- KT0101 Definitions of Terms and Concepts Associated with
Data and Data Management
To work effectively with information, it is important to understand what data is, how it is used,
and why organisations rely on proper data management practices. In Human Resources (HR),
data helps the organisation make decisions, comply with legal requirements, and monitor
workforce performance. This unit explains the core concepts and terminology related to data
and data management, forming the foundation for the modules that follow.
1.1 Understanding Data
Data refers to raw facts, numbers, symbols, or observations that have been collected but not yet
organised or interpreted. Data on its own has limited meaning until it is processed or analysed.
Examples of raw data include:
● An employee’s clock-in time
● A list of leave days taken
● Dates of training attendance
● A job applicant’s qualifications
9

Types of Data
Organisations use different types of data depending on their needs.
Table: Types of Data
Type of Data Description Example
Quantitative Data Numerical information Hours worked, salary, age
Qualitative Data Descriptive information Comments on performance
Structured Data Organised in fixed formats Spreadsheets, databases
Unstructured Data Not arranged in a pre-defined way Emails, interview notes
Primary Data Collected directly from the source Employee survey responses
Secondary Data Previously collected by others Industry reports, statistics
1.2 Information
Information is processed or organised data that has meaning and can assist in decision-making.
When data is analysed, interpreted, and presented in a useful format, it becomes information.
Examples:
● Absenteeism rate for the month
● Staff turnover percentage
● Training success rate
● Recruitment cost-per-hire
Information supports planning, compliance, reporting, and general HR operations.
10

1.3 Data Management
Data management refers to the policies, processes, and practices that ensure data is collected,
stored, maintained, and used effectively. Good data management helps maintain data accuracy,
reliability, security, and accessibility.
Key elements of data management include:
● Collecting the right data
● Ensuring data quality
● Storing data securely
● Processing and analysing data
● Maintaining privacy and confidentiality
● Disposing of data properly after its retention period
Data management ensures the organisation has the information it needs to operate efficiently
and lawfully.
1.4 Data Lifecycle
The data lifecycle refers to the stages through which data moves from the moment it is created
to the moment it is disposed of.
Table: Stages of the Data Lifecycle
Stage Description
Data Creation Data is generated or collected for the first time
Data Storage Data is saved in a secure location (digital or manual)
Data Processing Data is organised, cleaned, updated, or categorised
Data Use Data is used for reporting, decision-making, or analysis
Data Sharing Information is shared with authorised users if required
Data Retention Data is kept for a specific period based on legislation
Data Disposal Data is securely destroyed, deleted, or archived
11

Example:
Employee attendance data is created by a biometric system, stored in an HRIS, processed for
payroll, used for absenteeism reports, retained for audit purposes, and eventually disposed of
according to legal requirements.
1.5 HR Data
HR data refers to information that relates specifically to employees and HR processes. This
includes personal details, employment history, leave records, performance ratings, training
results, disciplinary outcomes, and recruitment information.
Examples of HR data categories:
● Biographical data (name, ID number, address)
● Employment data (job title, department, start date)
● Payroll data (salary, tax details, benefits)
● Performance data (ratings, competencies)
● Training data (courses completed, skills acquired)
● Compliance data (EEA categories, medical certificates)
HR data must be handled ethically and legally, especially due to its sensitive nature.
1.6 Data Quality
Data quality refers to the condition or accuracy of data. High-quality data is essential for
effective decision-making.
Characteristics of good data quality include:
● Accuracy: Correct and error-free
● Completeness: No missing information
● Consistency: Same formats and definitions used throughout
● Timeliness: Up-to-date and available when needed
● Relevance: Useful for the purpose collected
● Reliability: Trustworthy over time
Poor-quality data leads to incorrect decisions, compliance issues, and operational inefficiencies.
12

1.7 Data Security
Data security involves protecting data from unauthorised access, loss, theft, or damage.
Organisations must ensure data is stored safely and accessed only by authorised individuals.
Security measures include:
● Passwords and authentication
● Encryption
● User access controls
● Firewalls and antivirus software
● Secure filing systems
● Confidentiality agreements
In South Africa, the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) sets out specific
requirements for protecting personal information.
1.8 Data Privacy
Data privacy refers to an individual’s right to control how their personal information is collected,
used, stored, and shared. HR must respect employee privacy by collecting only necessary
information and using it only for legitimate purposes.
Examples of privacy practices include:
● Not sharing medical information with unauthorised staff
● Collecting only relevant information during recruitment
● Keeping disciplinary records confidential
● Informing employees of how their data will be used
13

1.9 Data Governance
Data governance defines the rules, policies, roles, and responsibilities that ensure data is
managed properly. It ensures data is accurate, consistent, secure, and used ethically.
Data governance includes:
● Policies and procedures
● Quality control measures
● Privacy standards
● Security frameworks
● Responsibilities of HR, IT, and management
● Monitoring and auditing of data practices
Good data governance supports compliance, trust, and efficient operations.
This unit introduced the core concepts and terminology related to data and data management.
You learned the difference between data and information, the types of data organisations use,
the data lifecycle, and the roles of data quality, security, privacy, and governance. These

are essential for organisational efficiency and compliance.

Unit 2 – KT0102 The Data Management Process
The data management process provides a structured approach for handling information from
the moment it is collected until it is archived or safely disposed of. For HR professionals,
understanding this process is essential because HR data is sensitive, confidential, and heavily
regulated. Each step in the data management cycle ensures that information remains accurate,
secure, and useful for organisational decision-making.
In this unit, you will explore each stage of the data management process and learn how HR
departments apply these steps in daily operations.
2.1. Overview of the Data Management Process
The data management process consists of a series of steps that ensure data is properly
captured, maintained, used, and secured throughout its lifecycle.
The main stages include:
1. Data Collection
2. Data Storage
3. Data Processing
4. Data Analysis
5. Data Reporting / Presentation
6. Data Archiving
7. Data Disposal
Each stage contributes to the overall goal of producing reliable, usable information.
15

2.2. Data Collection
Data collection is the first step and involves gathering information from various internal and
external sources.
Common HR Data Collection Methods:
● Employee application forms
● Attendance systems (biometrics, swipe cards)
● Employee surveys or feedback tools
● Performance appraisal forms
● Payroll inputs
● Training registers
Example (South African context):
A call centre uses a biometric system to record clock-in and clock-out times. These
time-stamped records form part of HR’s attendance data.
2.3. Data Storage
Once collected, data must be stored safely to ensure it remains accessible and protected.
Table: Data Storage Options:
Storage Method Examples
Electronic HRIS systems, cloud platforms, encrypted databases
Manual Filing cabinets, lever-arch files, printed employee records
Hybrid Combination of digital and paper systems
Good storage practices include:
● Backing up electronic data regularly
● Restricting access to authorised users
● Securing physical storage areas
● Using passwords and encryption for sensitive files
16

2.4. Data Processing
Data processing involves organising, cleaning, and converting raw data into a usable format.
HR Data Processing Activities:
● Capturing employee information into the HRIS
● Verifying accuracy of payroll inputs
● Removing duplicate entries
● Updating employee records
● Standardising formats (e.g., date formats, name fields)
Why does this step matter?
Incorrect processing can lead to payroll errors, compliance issues, and inaccurate reporting.
2.5. Data Analysis
Data analysis allows organisations to interpret data in a meaningful way.
Examples of HR Data Analysis:
● Identifying absenteeism trends
● Calculating employee turnover rates
● Measuring training effectiveness
● Comparing departmental performance indicators
Purpose of analysis:
To assist HR and management in making informed decisions based on evidence rather than
assumptions.
17

2.6.Data Reporting/Presentation
After analysis, findings must be communicated clearly.
Common HR Reporting Tools:
● Dashboards (Power BI, Excel, HRIS dashboards)
● Monthly or quarterly HR reports
● Graphs, charts, and visual summaries
● Compliance reports for EE, WSP/ATR, etc.
Characteristics of good reporting:
● Clear
● Accurate
● Relevant
● Easy to interpret
2.7.Data Archiving
Archiving involves storing old or inactive data for long-term reference.
Examples of Archived HR Data:
● Records of former employees
● Historical payroll information
● Prior performance documentation
Why organisations archive data:
● Legal retention requirements
● Audit purposes
● Organisational memory and trend analysis
Example:
POPIA and SARS regulations require certain employee data to be kept for specific periods
(e.g., payroll records must be kept for five years).
18

2.8. Data Disposal
Data disposal is the secure destruction of data that is no longer needed.
Safe Disposal Methods:
● Shredding physical documents
● Secure deletion software
● Wiping or destroying hard drives
● Following organisational retention schedules
Why is disposal important?
To prevent identity theft, protect confidentiality, reduce risk of legal penalties, and maintain
POPIA compliance.
2.9 The Data Management Cycle
The data management process is cyclical. After disposal, new data is collected, and the cycle
continues. Each stage influences the next, making accuracy, consistency, and proper
management essential.
Table: Summary of the Data Management Process
Stage Purpose
Collection Gathering accurate and relevant data
Storage Keeping data secure and organised
Processing Cleaning and preparing data for use
Analysis Identifying trends and insights
Reporting Communicating information clearly
Archiving Storing data for long-term use
Disposal Destroying data securely when no longer needed
19

The data management process provides a clear framework for handling HR information. By
following each step — from collection to disposal — HR professionals ensure that data remains
accurate, secure, compliant, and valuable for organisational decision-making. Understanding
this process prepares you to manage information responsibly throughout its lifecycle.

Unit 3 - KT0103 General Data Management Governance
Data governance provides the framework, rules, and responsibilities that guide how an
organisation manages its data. In HR, this includes ensuring personal information is collected
legally, stored securely, used appropriately, and protected from misuse. Governance ensures
that HR data is accurate, reliable, ethical, and compliant with legislation such as POPIA.
This unit explains the principles of data governance, the roles involved, the policies required,
and the importance of maintaining high standards when managing employee information.
3.1 Understanding Data Governance
Data governance refers to the overall management of data availability, usability, integrity, and
security. It is a system of decision-making authority, accountability, and control over data and its
handling throughout the organisation.
Key components of data governance include:
● Policies and procedures
● Data quality standards
● Security and privacy measures
● Defined roles and responsibilities
● Compliance with legislation
● Monitoring and auditing practices
Effective governance ensures that HR data supports organisational strategy and complies with
legal obligations.
21

3.2 Principles of Data Governance
Data governance is built on several core principles that guide how data should be handled
ethically and securely.
● Accountability
Specific individuals or departments are responsible for ensuring data is managed
correctly.
● Integrity
Data must be accurate, complete, and reliable. Any updates or changes must be
legitimate and recorded correctly.
● Security
Data must be protected from unauthorised access, theft, and misuse through technical
and organisational measures.
● Compliance
Data management must comply with laws such as POPIA, BCEA, LRA, EEA, and
SARS requirements.
● Transparency
Employees should know why their information is being collected, how it will be used,
and who will have access to it.
● Ethical Use
Data should be used fairly, without discrimination or misuse, and only for legitimate
organisational purposes.
● Accessibility
Authorised users should have the right level of access to perform their functions
effectively.
22

Table : Principles of Data Governance
Principle Description
Accountability Assigning responsibility for data decisions
Integrity Ensuring data accuracy and reliability
Security Protecting data from threats
Compliance Meeting legal and policy requirements
Transparency Being open about data usage
Ethical Use Using information responsibly
Accessibility Allowing appropriate levels of access
3.3 Policies and Procedures for Data Governance
Organisations develop policies to guide employees on how to handle data correctly. These
policies form part of the data governance framework and ensure consistency across the
organisation.
Common data-related policies include:
● Data Protection Policy
● Information Security Policy
● Access Control Policy
● Data Retention and Disposal Policy
● Confidentiality Agreements
● POPIA Compliance Procedures
● Incident Reporting and Data Breach Protocols
23

These policies specify:
● What data can be collected
● How data must be stored
● Who can access or update information
● How long data should be kept
● How data must be disposed of
3.4 Data Quality Standards
High-quality data is essential for effective HR decision-making. Governance includes
maintaining standards for data accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness.
Examples of data quality practices:
● Routine audits of records
● Validating employee information
● Standardising formats (e.g., date formats, ID numbers)
● Ensuring accurate capturing of attendance and leave data
● Updating information regularly
Poor data quality results in compliance issues, incorrect payroll, inaccurate reports, and poor
decision-making.
3.5 Data Security and Privacy
Data security protects information from threats such as unauthorised access, hacking, loss, or
corruption. Privacy protects employees’ rights regarding how their personal information is used.
Security measures include:
● Passwords and authentication systems
● Encryption of digital records
● Firewalls and antivirus tools
● Locked filing cabinets for physical documents
● Access control lists (ACLs)
● Regular backups of HR data
24

Privacy requirements include:
● Collecting only necessary information
● Informing employees why data is being collected
● Restricting access to sensitive information
● Ensuring confidentiality when dealing with personal records
Privacy is governed by POPIA, which sets conditions for the lawful processing of personal
information.
3.6 Roles and Responsibilities in Data Governance
Effective governance involves several people with different roles.
HR Department
● Ensures HR data is accurate, complete, and secure
● Enforces privacy and confidentiality
● Maintains employee records
● Monitors compliance with organisational policies
IT Department
● Manages system security and technical controls
● Supports access permissions
● Protects systems from cyber threats
● Performs system backups and maintenance
Data Governance Officer / Information Officer
● Oversees POPIA compliance
● Develops policies and standards
● Handles data breach incidents
● Ensures organisational adherence to legal requirements
25

Managers and Supervisors
● Ensure data related to their teams is accurate
● Submit documentation on time
● Follow correct information-handling procedures
Employees
● Provide accurate information
● Follow organisational rules on data usage
● Maintain confidentiality where required
3.7 Monitoring and Auditing
Data governance requires continuous oversight. Monitoring and auditing activities help identify
errors, risks, and areas that need improvement.
Examples of monitoring activities:
● Reviewing system access logs
● Checking data accuracy regularly
● Conducting compliance audits
● Reporting and investigating data breaches
● Ensuring proper disposal of outdated data
These practices help the organisation maintain trust, protect data, and improve efficiency.
26

3.8 Risks of Poor Governance
If governance processes are weak, the organisation may face:
● Legal Risks
POPIA violations may result in fines, lawsuits, or criminal charges.
● Operational Risks
Incorrect data may lead to payroll errors, poor decision-making, and inaccurate
reporting.
● Reputational Risks
Data breaches can damage the organisation’s public image.
● Security Risks
Hackers or unauthorised users may access or misuse sensitive information.
● Compliance Risks
Inaccurate data may lead to failed audits or incorrect statutory reporting.
Table: Risks Associated with Poor Data Governance
Risk Type Consequence
Legal Risk POPIA non-compliance, fines
Operational Risk Incorrect HR reports, payroll errors
Reputational Risk Loss of trust, negative publicity
Security Risk Data breaches, identity theft
Compliance Risk Failed audits, penalties

Introduction
Human Resources relies on accurate and reliable data to make decisions, meet legal
requirements, and support employees throughout the employment lifecycle. To achieve this, HR
must collect information from a variety of internal and external sources. Understanding these
sources — and knowing how to collect data correctly — ensures that HR information remains
relevant, complete, and compliant with organisational policies and legislation.
This module explores the different types of data sources available to HR, the methods used to
collect data, and the factors that influence the selection of appropriate data collection
processes. By the end of this module, you will understand where HR data comes from, how it is
gathered, and why proper collection practices are essential for effective HR functioning.
