Introduction
HRM Recordkeeping Is All Around You
Every organisation depends on accurate information — from attendance registers and
payroll data to performance reviews, contracts, health and safety reports, and digital files.
HRM recordkeeping is the system that keeps all this information organised, secure, and
accessible. It ensures that employees are paid correctly, legal requirements are met,
workplace disputes are managed fairly, and organisational decisions are based on
trustworthy data.
In simple terms, HRM recordkeeping is about capturing, protecting, and managing the
information that tells the story of every employee’s journey — from the moment they
are hired to the day they leave the organisation.
By learning about HRM recordkeeping, you will:
● Understand the purpose of HRM records – You’ll explore why organisations
document employment information and how these records support fairness,
accuracy, and compliance.
● Learn how to manage and store information – You’ll discover the standards and
practices used to organise, file, and protect both paper-based and digital records.
● Develop strong administrative and data-handling skills – You’ll learn how to
maintain accurate files, protect sensitive information, and manage records ethically
and professionally.
● Ensure compliance with legislation – You’ll understand the legal requirements that
govern HR records in South Africa, including the BCEA, LRA, EEA, OHSA, POPIA,
and SARS regulations.
● Build your technological confidence – You’ll gain practical knowledge of digital
systems, cloud storage, data security, and backup procedures used in modern HR
environments.
● Support effective HR service delivery – With strong recordkeeping skills, you’ll
help organisations operate smoothly, reduce risk, and ensure accountability.

UNIT 1 KT0101 Definition and types of HRM records.
Human Resource Management (HRM) records form the foundation of effective HR service
delivery. These records contain essential information about employees and their
employment history, enabling HR practitioners to manage people-related processes
accurately, consistently, and in compliance with legislation. In a modern organisation, HRM
records serve not only as administrative tools but also as strategic resources that support
planning, decision-making, and organisational effectiveness.
1.1 Definition of HRM Records
HRM records refer to the systematic collection, storage, management, and protection of
employee-related information within an organisation. These records include
documentation related to recruitment, employment contracts, performance management,
training, compensation, disciplinary processes, medical information, and termination
procedures. The primary purpose of HRM records is to ensure reliable documentation
throughout the employee lifecycle, from initial application to final separation.
Maintaining accurate HRM records is essential for:
● Ensuring legal and regulatory compliance
● Supporting fair and consistent HR practices
● Facilitating accurate payroll and benefits administration
● Assisting in performance evaluation and development
● Enabling informed HR decision-making
● Enhancing organisational transparency and accountability
Did You Know?
Some HR records, especially medical and payroll records, must be kept for several years
after an employee leaves. This ensures compliance with labour audits, tax regulations, and
safety investigations.
9

1.2 Importance of Maintaining HRM Records
HRM records play a vital role in organisational functioning. They contribute to:
● Informed Decision-Making
By analysing trends in performance, attendance, training, or turnover, HR
practitioners can recommend appropriate interventions and support strategic
workforce planning.
● Effective Performance Management
Detailed performance and disciplinary records provide a reliable basis for evaluating
employee strengths, identifying skills gaps, and planning development initiatives.
● Legal Compliance
Labour laws require organisations to maintain specific records such as contracts,
payslips, injury reports, and termination documentation. Accurate recordkeeping
protects the organisation during audits or disputes.
● Dispute Resolution
Documented evidence assists HR in resolving grievances, addressing misconduct,
and defending organisational decisions fairly and transparently.
● Succession Planning
Records of employees’ skills, qualifications, and achievements help identify potential
candidates for promotion and leadership roles.
Fun Fact
Before HRIS systems existed, HR records were kept in physical filing cabinets — sometimes
entire rooms filled wall-to-wall with paper files!
1.3 Categories of HRM Records
Job performance records capture all information related to how an employee performs in
their role over time. These records help HR and managers monitor productivity, identify
development needs, and support fair performance evaluations. They also provide a
documented history of achievements, behavioural concerns, and growth opportunities, which
is essential for accurate decision-making.
Clear and detailed performance records ensure that employees understand expectations
and receive appropriate support. They also promote transparency and fairness, especially
when decisions relate to promotions, rewards, or disciplinary action. In addition, reliable
performance data strengthens organisational planning by identifying skills gaps, training
needs, and potential future leaders

UNIT 2 KT0102 Importance of the availability, integrity, and security
of HRM records.
Human Resource Management (HRM) records form the backbone of all employee-related
processes within an organisation. These records document every stage of the employee
lifecycle—from recruitment and selection to performance management, compensation, and
eventual separation. Because HRM records contain sensitive personal, financial, medical,
and employment information, their proper management is essential for organisational
effectiveness, legal compliance, and employee trust.
To ensure that HRM records continue to support sound HR practices, three key principles
must be upheld: availability, integrity, and security. These principles work together to
create a reliable and trustworthy HR information environment.

2.1. Availability of HRM Records
Availability refers to the ability of authorised HR professionals to access employee records
whenever they are required to support decisions and processes. If records are missing,
corrupted, inaccessible, or stored in disorganised systems, HR operations can slow down
significantly.
Consequences of Poor Availability
● Delays in Decision-Making: HR processes—such as promotions, leave approvals,
disciplinary procedures, and benefits administration—cannot proceed without
complete and timely information.
● Workflow Disruptions: HR staff may waste valuable time locating, retrieving, or
reconstructing missing documents.
● Reduced Employee Confidence: Employees may lose trust in the HR department if
their records are unavailable or if processes are repeatedly delayed.
Ensuring Record Availability
Organisations can strengthen availability through:
● Cloud-Based HRM Systems: Allow secure, centralised access to employee
information from authorised devices.
● Digital Document Management: Provides structured storage, improved retrieval,
and faster indexing of HR files.
● Standardised Filing Procedures: Ensures that records are stored logically and
consistently, minimising the risk of misplacement or duplication.
Availability ensures that HR professionals have the right information at the right time,
enabling smooth HR service delivery.
Did You Know?
In many countries, including South Africa, organisations are required by law to retain certain
HR records — such as payroll data and medical records — for several years after
employment ends. Failure to comply can result in legal penalties.
13

2.2. Integrity of HRM Records
Integrity refers to the accuracy, consistency, and completeness of HRM records. When
records contain errors, outdated information, or missing data, HR decisions can become
unreliable or even legally problematic.
Implications of Poor Integrity
● Incorrect HR Decisions: Faulty performance ratings, inaccurate leave balances, or
incomplete training histories can undermine fairness.
● Payroll and Benefits Errors: Incorrect working hours or deduction records may
result in overpayments or underpayments.
● Compliance Risks: Incomplete or inaccurate records may violate labour laws,
exposing the organisation to penalties during audits.
Maintaining Record Integrity
Integrity can be upheld through:
● Data Entry Validation: Automated system checks that prevent incorrect inputs.
● Regular Data Audits: Frequent reviews to identify missing or inconsistent
information.
● Employee Self-Service Portals: Allow employees to update personal details,
reducing errors and improving data accuracy.
High data integrity strengthens organisational credibility and supports fair and transparent
HR decision-making.
2.3. Security of HRM Records
Security refers to the protection of HRM records from unauthorised access, theft,
manipulation, or loss. Given the sensitivity of employee information, maintaining strong
security controls is a legal and ethical obligation.
Risks of Poor Security
● Data Breaches: Confidential employee information may be exposed or stolen
through hacking or physical theft.
● Privacy Violations: Mishandling personal information can damage trust and lead to
legal repercussions.
14

● Discriminatory Misuse: Unauthorised access to sensitive data could be used
unfairly in employment decisions.
Strengthening Record Security
Effective security measures include:
● Access Controls: Restricting access to HRM records based on job roles and
responsibilities.
● Data Encryption: Protects information by making it unreadable without proper
authorisation.
● Data Loss Prevention (DLP) Systems: Monitors and restricts the movement of
sensitive information.
● Security Awareness Training: Educates staff about cybersecurity risks, social
engineering, and safe data handling practices.
Security ensures confidentiality, protects employees’ rights, and reduces organisational risk.
2.4. The Interdependence of Availability, Integrity, and Security
Although these three principles can be viewed individually, they operate as a connected,
interdependent system:
● Security enhances availability by protecting records from unauthorised deletion or
corruption.
● Availability supports integrity by ensuring data can be regularly reviewed,
updated, and verified.
● Integrity strengthens security by reducing vulnerabilities created by incomplete or
inaccurate data.
Together, availability, integrity, and security create a robust HRM recordkeeping framework
that ensures accurate information, protects privacy, and supports efficient HR service
delivery.
Fun Fact
The first “HR records system” used in factories during the Industrial Revolution was a
handwritten ledger book — today, artificial intelligence (AI) helps HR professionals analyse
thousands of HR records in seconds!

UNIT 3 KT0103 Principles for effective digital records
In today’s digital workplace, organisations rely heavily on electronic records to manage
employee information, document decisions, and support HR operations. Effective digital
records management ensures that organisational information remains accurate, accessible,
protected, and useful throughout its lifecycle. To achieve this, eight core principles must be
applied consistently: accountability, transparency, integrity, accessibility, retention and
disposition, authentication, usability, and interoperability.
These principles help organisations maintain compliance, safeguard sensitive information,
and support efficient HR service delivery.

3.1 Principles for Effective Digital Recordkeeping
Accountability
Accountability refers to assigning clear responsibility for creating, maintaining, classifying,
securing, and ultimately disposing of digital records. When accountability is well established,
organisations can ensure consistency, compliance with policies, and proper handling of
sensitive information.
Key Requirements:
● Assign ownership for each category of digital records.
● Develop documented procedures for record creation, storage, retention, and access.
● Train employees to follow recordkeeping protocols consistently.
Did You Know?
Many organisations experience “digital decay” — where records become unreadable over
time due to outdated formats or unsupported software. Proper planning prevents this.
3.1.1 Transparency
Transparency ensures that digital records accurately represent the events, activities, and
transactions they document. It also ensures users can track how records were created,
modified, and accessed.
Key Requirements:
● Ensure accurate and consistent data entry.
● Maintain audit trails of all actions on records.
● Use version control to preserve historical changes.
Fun Fact
The average organisation stores 80% of its information digitally—and most of it is created
by employees during everyday tasks without them realising they’re generating records.
18

3.1.2 Integrity
Integrity refers to the accuracy, completeness, and reliability of digital records. A record with
compromised integrity cannot be trusted for decision-making or compliance.
Key Requirements:
● Implement strong security controls to prevent unauthorised changes.
● Conduct regular data backups.
● Establish a disaster recovery plan to restore lost or corrupted data.
3.1.3 Accessibility
Accessibility ensures that authorised individuals can retrieve and use digital records easily. It
also includes making systems accessible to people with disabilities.
Key Requirements:
● Store records in open, widely supported formats.
● Use metadata tagging to improve searchability.
● Ensure compatibility with assistive technologies such as screen readers.
3.1.4 Retention and Disposition
Organisations must manage records through their full lifecycle—retaining them for the legally
required period and disposing of them securely when no longer needed.
Key Requirements:
● Implement a clear retention schedule.
● Use secure digital destruction or wiping methods.
● Comply with privacy legislation during record disposal.
3.1.5 Authentication
Authentication ensures that digital records come from verified creators and have not been
tampered with.
Key Requirements:
● Use identity verification processes such as secure login or multi-factor authentication.
● Implement digital signatures for important documents.
● Use timestamping to track when records were created or altered.
19

3.1.6 Usability
Digital recordkeeping systems must be easy to use and well-designed to encourage correct
usage and minimise errors.
Key Requirements:
● Provide user-friendly interfaces with clear navigation.
● Include robust search and filtering tools.
● Offer ongoing training and technical support.
3.1.7 Interoperability
Interoperability ensures that digital records can be accessed and transferred across different
systems and software platforms.
Key Requirements:
● Use open standards for data formats and metadata.
● Choose long-term accessible file formats.
● Develop data migration strategies to support future technologies.
Real-World Example
A South African healthcare organisation implemented metadata tagging and strict access
control after failing an internal audit. Within six months, retrieval time improved by 60%, and
compliance scores increased significantly.

UNIT 4 KT0104 Principles for effective manual/paper-based records
Even in the digital age, paper-based records remain essential for legal compliance, secure
backups, and long-term organisational documentation. To ensure that manual records
remain accurate, accessible, and protected, organisations must apply the core principles of
effective paper recordkeeping. These principles support HR service delivery by reducing
errors, strengthening compliance, and improving record accessibility.
4.1 Principles for Effective Paper Recordkeeping
4.1.1 Standardisation
A standardised filing system ensures consistency and prevents misplacement of important
documents. It allows authorised staff to locate records quickly and reduces confusion across
departments.
This includes:
● Using alphabetical, numerical, or subject filing systems
● Applying uniform folder and label formats
● Maintaining consistent naming and classification rules

4.1.2 Accessibility and Retrieval
Paper records must be easy for authorised users to find when needed. Clear organisation
supports timely HR processes and reduces administrative delays.
This includes:
● Clearly labelled shelves and folders
● An index or database matching file numbers to locations
● Regular checks to ensure files remain in proper order
Did You Know?
Many organisations still maintain paper copies of employment contracts because physical

4.1.3 Security and Preservation
Paper records often contain sensitive personal and organisational information and must be
protected from theft, damage, and environmental harm.
This includes:
● Locked cabinets or restricted-access filing rooms
● Fireproof or waterproof storage for critical documents
Controlled temperature and humidity to prevent deterioration
Retention and Disposition
Records must be stored only for as long as necessary. Once the required retention period
ends, documents must be destroyed securely to protect confidential information.
This includes:
● Following legal retention schedules
● Shredding outdated records
● Maintaining logs of disposed documents for compliance
Fun Fact
Colour-coded filing systems reduce misfiled documents by up to 30% because the human
eye recognises colour patterns faster than text.
Integration with Digital Systems
Manual and digital systems can work together to improve efficiency and accessibility. Many
organisations now use hybrid systems that combine the strengths of both.
This includes:
● Scanning important paper documents for digital backup
● Linking physical files to digital indexes
● Keeping paper only when legally necessary

Introduction
Accurate and secure HRM recordkeeping is essential for managing employees and
complying with South African law. Organisations must store information in ways that protect
privacy, support fair labour practices, and ensure records are available when needed. This
module introduces the principles of effective recordkeeping, including standards for
paper-based and digital systems, security measures for backups and recovery, and the
challenges of cloud-based storage. It also outlines key legislative requirements that guide
how HR records must be created, stored, and maintained. By the end of this module,

UNIT 1 KT0201 Professional recordkeeping standards
Professional recordkeeping standards ensure that information is created, maintained, and
preserved in a way that supports transparency, accountability, legal compliance, and
organisational efficiency. These standards guide HR professionals and administrators in
creating records that are accurate, authentic, secure, and accessible throughout their
lifecycle. Whether records are stored on paper or in digital systems, adhering to professional
standards is essential for trustworthy and effective HR service delivery.
1.1. Core Principles of Professional Recordkeeping
Professional recordkeeping is built on several universal principles that apply across
industries and professions.

1.1.1 Accuracy and Completeness
Records must provide a faithful and complete account of the activities or decisions they
document. Accurate records reduce errors, support fair decision-making, and ensure
compliance with organisational and legal requirements.
This includes recording:
● Dates and times
● Individuals involved
● Details of actions taken
● Supporting documents or evidence
Complete and accurate records strengthen organisational credibility and improve the
reliability of HR processes.

1.1.2 Reliability and Authenticity
A reliable record is one that can be trusted as a true representation of events. Authenticity
ensures the record has not been altered without authorisation.
This is achieved through:
● Audit trails showing creation, edits, and access
● Tamper-evident methods such as timestamps or digital signatures
● Consistent creation procedures that standardise how records are documented
Authentic records are essential during audits, investigations, or legal disputes.
1.1.3 Accessibility and Usability
Records must be easy for authorised users to find, understand, and use. Poor organisation
or inconsistent formatting reduces efficiency and increases the risk of errors.
Accessibility depends on:
● Clear filing systems
● Logical folder structures
● Consistent naming conventions
● Indexing with keywords or metadata
● Use of standard file formats
Accessible records support accurate reporting and reduce delays in HR processes.
