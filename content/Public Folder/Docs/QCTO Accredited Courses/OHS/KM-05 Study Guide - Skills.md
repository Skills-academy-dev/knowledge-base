Introduction to the Occupational Health and Safety

Applications Study Guide

Welcome to Your Introduction to the Occupational Health and Safety Applications Study

Guide\! This study guide has been developed to support you as a distance learning student. It is

designed to help you understand your textbook and prepare for your assessments — even if

you are studying from home.

Studying Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) Applications will equip you with the knowledge,

values, and practical skills to apply health and safety legislation, monitor compliance, and

promote a safe and healthy work environment.

By completing this course, you will learn how to:

● Understand and apply OHS legislation such as the Occupational Health and Safety Act

(OHSA) and related regulations.

● Conduct workplace inspections and identify potential hazards.

● Implement safety systems and monitor compliance in line with OHS standards.

● Promote cooperation between employees and management to build a culture of safety

and accountability.

This guide is your learning companion throughout the course. It is designed to:

● Explain key OHS concepts, roles, and procedures in simple, structured language.

● Summarise each module’s main topics to make study and revision easier.

● Help you prepare for assignments, tests, and final assessments.

● Strengthen your ability to apply OHS principles and procedures in real workplace

contexts.

Each module follows your prescribed Learner Manual and is divided into manageable units to

support steady progress and confidence in your studies.

Remember: You are not alone on this journey. Stay focused, plan your study time carefully, and

reach out to your tutor or support team whenever you need help or clarification.

Let’s begin your journey into the world of Occupational Health and Safety Applications — where

every action you take helps protect lives and build safer, fairer workplaces for all.

How to Use the Occupational Health and Safety

Applications Study Guide

This study guide is designed to make your learning journey easier, especially as a distance

learning student. It will help you understand your Learner Manual, stay organised, and prepare

effectively for your assessments. Here’s how to use it successfully:

1\. Start with the Module Overview

Each module begins with a short introduction that explains the key focus areas and learning

outcomes. Read this section first to understand what you will be learning and why it matters in

the context of workplace safety, hazard management, and legal compliance.

2\. Read the Unit Summaries

Every module is divided into smaller units. These unit summaries simplify important ideas and

concepts — such as hazard identification, risk assessment, control measures, and

documentation — to make them easier to understand. You can read them before studying your

Learner Manual to get an overview, or afterward to reinforce what you’ve learned.

3\. Highlight Key Concepts

As you read, use a highlighter or notes to mark key terms, definitions, and examples — for

instance, sections of the Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA), the Compensation for

Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act (COIDA), or the hierarchy of hazard controls.

Highlighting helps you focus on what’s most important and makes revision much easier later.

4\. Take Notes and Reflect

Leave space in this guide for your own notes, examples, or personal reflections. Writing in your

own words helps you remember information more effectively and apply what you’ve learned in

real workplace situations — such as safety inspections, risk assessments, or accident

investigations.

5\. Prepare for Assessments

Use this guide to revise before your tests, assignments, and final assessments. Each module

includes summaries, reflection questions, and case studies to help you practise critical thinking

and apply OHS principles to realistic workplace scenarios.

6\. Refer to Your Learner Manual for More Detail

This study guide supports your prescribed Learner Manual — it does not replace it. Always refer

back to your manual for detailed explanations, legislative extracts, technical procedures, and

examples that deepen your understanding of OHS applications and workplace safety systems.

7\. Manage Your Time Wisely

Plan your studies carefully by setting weekly goals. Work through one module at a time, review

your progress regularly, and stay consistent. Remember — steady progress leads to success

and helps you retain important information about risk control, documentation standards, and

emergency response procedures.

8\. Ask for Help When Needed

If you find something unclear, make a note of your question and reach out to your tutor or

academic support team. Asking questions shows curiosity and commitment — two essential

qualities of an effective Occupational Health and Safety Practitioner.

7 Study Tips

1\. Break Down Big Topics into Small Parts: Occupational Health and Safety (OHS)

Applications covers many important concepts — from understanding legislation and

hazard identification to risk assessment, control measures, and documentation. Don’t try

to learn everything at once.

2\. Practise Applying Knowledge to Real Workplace Scenarios: Many assessment

questions will ask you to apply what you’ve learned to realistic workplace examples. Use

the case studies and exercises in your study guide to practise analysing situations,

identifying hazards, and suggesting appropriate solutions.

3\. Make Flashcards for Key Terms and Concepts: There are many key OHS terms to

remember — such as hazard identification, risk assessment, hierarchy of control,

own workplace or community environment. Linking theory to practice makes your

learning more meaningful and easier to remember.

help you identify which areas you’ve mastered and which need more revision — whether

it’s OHS legislation, measurement instruments, or accident investigation techniques.

6\. Use the Study Guide for Quick Reviews: Before starting a new module in your

Learner Manual, read through the study guide’s introduction and summaries to get a

clear overview of what you’ll be learning.

7\. Stick to a Study Schedule: Time management is key for success as a distance learner.

Create a weekly study plan with realistic goals — for example, completing one unit per

week or setting aside one hour each day for focused study. Staying organised and

consistent will help you stay motivated, confident, and on track throughout your OHS

Applications course.

Introduction

Hazard identification and risk assessment form the foundation of every workplace safety

system. This module explains how to recognise potential hazards, assess the level of risk they

pose, and apply effective control measures to prevent accidents. You will also learn about the

legal requirements that guide hazard identification and risk management in South Africa.

Read page 10 of your learner manual. for more information.

Unit 1: (KT0101) The Process of Hazard Identification and Risk

Assessment

This unit introduces the steps involved in identifying and assessing hazards in different

workplaces. It explains how to evaluate potential dangers, classify them correctly, and take

practical action to reduce risks.

1.1 Definition of Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment

Hazard identification means finding anything in the workplace that could cause harm.

Risk assessment involves analysing how likely it is that the hazard will cause injury or damage

and how severe the outcome could be.

Together, they help employers and employees plan safe working methods.

The process begins by inspecting the workplace, reviewing tasks, and consulting workers.

Common steps include observing work areas, checking equipment, and examining past incident

Each hazard is then listed, described, and evaluated for potential impact.

1.3 Different Processes of Classifying Hazards

Hazards are classified according to their source or nature.

They may be physical (machinery, noise, heat), chemical (fumes, cleaning agents), biological

(bacteria, viruses), ergonomic (poor posture, repetitive movement), or psychosocial (stress,

fatigue).

Classification helps in choosing the most suitable control measures.

Unit 2: (KT0102) The Difference and Interrelationship Between

Hazards and Risks

This unit explains how hazards and risks are connected, and why both must be understood to

maintain a safe working environment.

2.1 Definition and Examples of Hazards

A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm or injury.

Examples include wet floors, unguarded machinery, faulty wiring, or toxic substances.

Identifying hazards early prevents accidents and illnesses.

2.2 Definition and Examples of Risks

A risk is the chance that a hazard will actually cause harm.

For example, a chemical spill is a hazard, but the risk depends on how much exposure occurs

and whether workers wear protective gear.

Assessing risk helps prioritise which hazards to address first.

2.3 Interrelationship Between Hazards and Risks

Hazards and risks are closely linked — a hazard exists at all times, but risk increases when

exposure or unsafe behaviour occurs.

Effective safety management reduces the level of risk, even if the hazard cannot be completely

removed.

Understanding this relationship allows for balanced, realistic safety planning.

Read pages 20—26 of your learner manual for more information on this unit.

Unit 3: (KT0103) Controls and Examples of Controls to Prevent

Hazards from Causing Incidents

This unit describes the different types of controls used to prevent hazards from causing harm

and to reduce the impact when incidents occur. It also explains the hierarchy of control used in

South African health and safety regulations.

3.1 Controls to Prevent Hazards from Causing Incidents

Prevention begins with eliminating hazards where possible.

If elimination is not feasible, controls such as substitution, engineering solutions, safe work

procedures, and personal protective equipment (PPE) are used.

These controls aim to stop accidents before they happen.

3.2 Controls to Mitigate the Consequences of Hazards

Some hazards cannot be fully prevented, so mitigation controls reduce the damage if an

incident occurs.

Examples include fire alarms, spill containment kits, first-aid equipment, and emergency

evacuation procedures.

Mitigation focuses on minimising harm and restoring safety quickly.

3.3 The Hierarchy of Control of Hazards and Risks

The hierarchy of control ranks safety measures from most to least effective.

It includes elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and PPE.

Employers must always aim for the highest level of protection practicable.

Unit 4: (KT0104) The Legal Requirements Regarding Hazard

Identification and Risk Assessment

This unit outlines the key legal duties of employers and employees regarding hazard

identification and risk assessment, as well as common sources of hazards in the workplace.

4.1 Legal Requirements for Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment

According to the Occupational Health and Safety Act (Act 85 of 1993), employers must identify

hazards, assess risks, and implement appropriate controls.

Regular reviews and record-keeping are required to ensure compliance and continuous

improvement.

Failure to comply may result in legal penalties or closure of unsafe operations.

4.2 Sources of Hazards in the Workplace

Hazards can come from equipment, work processes, materials, the physical environment, or

human behaviour.

Poor maintenance, lack of training, and unsafe work habits often increase risk.

Identifying these sources early helps prevent injuries and maintain productivity.

Introduction

Accident and incident investigation is essential for understanding how and why workplace

events occur. This module explains how to identify the causes of accidents and incidents, apply

legal requirements for reporting, and follow a structured process to prevent future occurrences.

It also explores how effective investigations contribute to continuous improvement in workplace

safety.

Read page 53 of your learner manual for more information.

Unit 1: (KT0201) The Difference Between Accidents and Incidents

This unit explains what distinguishes an accident from an incident and why both are important

for effective safety management.

1.1 Definition of an Accident

An accident is an unplanned event that results in injury, illness, damage, or loss.

It usually involves harm to people, equipment, or property.

Accidents indicate that control measures have failed or hazards were not properly managed.

1.2 Definition of an Incident

An incident is an unplanned event that does not cause injury or damage but has the potential to

do so.

It is often referred to as a “near miss.”

Incidents serve as early warnings that a hazard exists and needs attention.

1.3 Implications for Safety Management

Investigating both accidents and incidents helps identify weaknesses in safety systems.

Treating incidents seriously prevents them from developing into future accidents.

Effective reporting ensures that lessons are learned and risks are reduced.

Unit 2: (KT0202) The Interrelationship of Accidents and Incidents

This unit shows how accidents and incidents are connected and explains the statistical model

that demonstrates their relationship.

2.1 Relationship Between Accidents and Incidents

Research shows that for every serious accident, there are many minor incidents and near

misses.

The Heinrich Safety Pyramid illustrates this — frequent small incidents often lead to fewer but

more serious accidents.

By investigating and addressing minor incidents early, workplaces can prevent major injuries or

losses.

Unit 3: (KT0203) The Legal Requirements Regarding the Need to

Investigate and Report on All Accidents and Incidents

This unit outlines the main legislation governing accident and incident investigation and

reporting in South Africa.

3.1 The Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act (COIDA)

COIDA provides compensation for employees injured or made ill while performing their duties.

It requires employers to report all workplace accidents to the Compensation Commissioner

within seven days.

Failure to report may result in penalties and loss of benefits for affected workers.

3.2 The Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA)

The OHSA requires employers to investigate and report all workplace accidents and dangerous

occurrences.

Reports must include details of the event, causes, injuries, and corrective actions taken.

The Department of Employment and Labour monitors compliance with these requirements.

3.3 Investigation Requirements

Employers must appoint a competent person to conduct an investigation.

All findings must be recorded and corrective measures implemented.

Accurate reporting ensures compliance, accountability, and continuous improvement in safety

performance.

Unit 4: (KT0204) The Typical Process for Investigating Accidents and

Incidents

This unit describes the step-by-step process used to investigate workplace accidents and

incidents.

4.1 Steps in the Investigation Process

The process begins with securing the scene and gathering initial information.

Interviews are conducted with witnesses, evidence is collected, and causes are analysed.

The final report should identify root causes and recommend corrective and preventive measures

to stop recurrence.

Unit 5: (KT0205) The Criteria for Effective Accident and Incident

Investigation

This unit explains what makes an investigation effective and how to ensure accurate, useful

results.

5.1 Characteristics of Effective Investigations

Effective investigations are prompt, thorough, and objective.

They focus on identifying root causes rather than placing blame.

A good investigation results in clear recommendations that improve systems and prevent future

incidents.

Introduction

Occupational hygiene focuses on anticipating, recognising, evaluating, and controlling

workplace conditions that may cause illness or injury. This module introduces the key types of

occupational health hazards, how they enter the body, and how measurements are taken and

interpreted. You will also learn about the instruments used in occupational hygiene and how

threshold limits are applied to ensure compliance with health and safety standards.

Read page 87 of your learner manual for more information.

Unit 1: (KT0301) Potential Occupational Health Hazards and Routes of

Entry into the Body

This unit explains the main types of occupational health hazards and how harmful substances or

agents can enter the human body in the workplace.

1.1 Types of Occupational Health Hazards

Occupational health hazards include physical, chemical, biological, ergonomic, and

psychosocial hazards.

Physical hazards come from noise, vibration, or extreme temperatures.

Chemical hazards include gases, vapours, and dusts that may irritate or poison workers.

Biological hazards arise from exposure to bacteria, fungi, or viruses, while ergonomic hazards

result from poor posture or repetitive strain.

Psychosocial hazards relate to stress, fatigue, or workplace harassment.

1.2 Routes of Entry of Hazardous Substances into the Body

Hazardous substances can enter the body through inhalation, skin contact, ingestion, or

injection. Inhalation of fumes, vapours, or dust is the most common route.

Regular monitoring and use of protective equipment reduce exposure and health risks.

Read pages 87—92 of your textbook for more information on this unit.

Unit 2: (KT0302) Typical Occupational Hygiene Measurements That

Are Taken Within Industry

This unit outlines the common types of occupational hygiene measurements performed in

various industries to evaluate workplace conditions.

2.1 Common Measurements in Different Industries

Measurements may include noise levels, air quality, lighting intensity, temperature, and humidity.

In chemical industries, air sampling detects harmful vapours or dust concentrations.

In manufacturing, noise and vibration measurements protect workers from hearing loss.

Each measurement provides data to determine whether exposure levels are within safe limits.

Read pages 93—98 of your learner manual for more information on this unit.

Unit 3: (KT0303) Instruments That Are Used to Take Typical

Occupational Hygiene Measurements

This unit describes the main instruments used in occupational hygiene and explains how they

are operated and maintained to ensure accurate readings.

3.1 Common Measuring Instruments and Proper Handling/Calibration of

Instruments

Common instruments include sound level meters, gas detectors, dust samplers, thermometers,

and light meters.

These instruments must be calibrated regularly to maintain accuracy.

Proper use and handling of equipment ensure reliable results that support compliance with

health and safety standards.

28

Read pages 99—105 of your learner manual for more information on this unit.

Unit 4: (KT0304) The Threshold Limits for the Various Occupational

Hygiene Measurements

This unit explains how threshold limits are determined, applied, and monitored in workplace

health and safety management.

4.1 Legal Requirements for Threshold Limits

Threshold limits are regulated under the Occupational Health and Safety Act (Act 85 of 1993)

and related regulations.

Employers must ensure that exposure to hazardous agents remains below prescribed limits.

Regular monitoring and documentation prove compliance and protect worker health.

4.2 Roles of Stakeholders in Determining Threshold Limits

Stakeholders such as the Department of Employment and Labour, occupational hygienists, and

employer representatives establish and review threshold limits.

They rely on medical research and international standards to ensure worker protection.

Consultation ensures that limits remain relevant to changing industrial conditions.

4.3 Determining and Applying Threshold Limits

Threshold limits indicate the maximum safe level of exposure to a hazardous substance.

When readings exceed the limit, corrective actions must be taken immediately.

Regular measurement, record-keeping, and review help maintain a safe and healthy workplace.
