Introduction
Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) is at the heart of every workplace — from busy
construction sites and factory floors to modern offices and retail spaces. It’s reflected not
only in safety checklists and compliance reports but also in how we communicate — how
we listen, share information, and work together to keep everyone safe.
Effective communication is the cornerstone of OHS success. It ensures that vital information
about hazards, procedures, and responsibilities is shared clearly, accurately, and
respectfully. In this way, communication becomes more than just talking — it becomes a tool
for building trust, promoting understanding, and strengthening a culture of safety across all
levels of an organization.
This manual will guide you through the essential concepts, skills, and ethical principles that
define effective communication within the OHS discipline. You’ll learn how to express
yourself confidently, listen actively, resolve conflicts constructively, and represent both
employee and employer interests with professionalism and integrity.
By studying Fundamentals of Communication within the OHS Discipline, you will:
● Master the art of clear and ethical communication – Learn how to convey safety
messages accurately, avoid misunderstandings, and foster open dialogue that
supports workplace safety and compliance.
● Build trust and collaboration – Develop interpersonal and listening skills that
strengthen relationships between employees, management, and OHS
representatives.
● Promote ethical and responsible conduct – Understand how honesty, respect,
and integrity form the foundation of all professional communication in safety
management.
● Manage conflict effectively – Gain practical strategies for addressing
disagreements and turning difficult conversations into opportunities for growth and
understanding.
● Lead by example – Use your communication skills to motivate others, advocate for
safe practices, and contribute to South Africa’s goal of achieving safe, fair, and
productive workplaces.
Through every conversation, report, and representation, you have the power to influence
positive change. Effective communication doesn’t just share information — it saves lives,
builds trust, and shapes safer futures for everyone at work.

Unit 1: KT0101 – Basic Communication Model and Describe
Workplace Strategies to Overcome Typical Barriers to
Communication
Communication is central to every organisation and learning environment. For development
practitioners, educators, and ECD professionals, it is a vital skill for collaboration,
empowerment, and social transformation. This unit explores communication models, the
elements of the communication process, and common barriers that can prevent effective
understanding in the workplace.
By understanding how communication works, practitioners can deliver messages clearly,
interpret meaning accurately, and build stronger professional and community relationships.
1.1. Understanding the Communication Model
A communication model provides a structured way of representing how messages
move between people. It simplifies the complex process of human interaction and helps
practitioners identify where communication succeeds or breaks down.
Communication models are grouped into three broad categories:
● Linear models – one-way communication.
● Interactive models – two-way communication with feedback.
● Transactional models – continuous exchange between sender and receiver.

1.2. The Communication Process
The communication process follows a sequence of steps that make up how messages are
sent and received:
1. Generation of Ideas – The sender develops an idea to communicate.
2. Encoding the Message – The sender converts the idea into symbols, words, or
actions.
3. Selecting the Channel – The best medium is chosen to reach the audience.
4. Decoding the Message – The receiver interprets or understands the message.
5. Providing Feedback – The receiver responds, confirming understanding or
requesting clarification.

Unit 2: KT0102 – Coaching, Influencing People, Assertiveness and
Conflict
In any workplace — and especially in the Early Childhood Development (ECD) environment
— success depends not only on what you know but also on how you work with people.
Coaching, influencing, assertiveness, and conflict management are key skills that help
create a healthy, productive, and respectful working environment.
These skills shape how we communicate, collaborate, and support others. In this unit, you’ll
explore how to coach others toward success, how to influence people positively, how to
communicate assertively, and how to resolve conflicts constructively. Each of these abilities
contributes to stronger teamwork and a more harmonious centre.
2.1. Coaching
Coaching is a developmental process that helps a person move from where they are to
where they want to be. It’s about empowering others to take responsibility for their own
growth — not controlling them.
Did You Know?
Good coaches ask open-ended questions like “What would success look like for you?” —
instead of giving ready-made answers.
Benefits of Coaching
● Improves job performance and motivation.
● Builds confidence and self-awareness.
● Enhances teamwork and collaboration.
● Promotes continuous learning and growth.
Real Example:
A senior ECD teacher coaches a new assistant by helping her plan daily routines. Together
they reflect, adapt, and celebrate progress — strengthening both performance and trust.

respect.

UNIT 3: KT0103 – Basic Models for Coaching
Coaching is more than just telling someone what to do — it’s about helping them discover
their potential, set goals, and take action to achieve them.
In Early Childhood Development (ECD) settings, coaching helps practitioners reflect on their
practice, strengthen their confidence, and improve the quality of care and learning provided
to young children.
This unit introduces several coaching models — structured approaches that guide the
coaching process. Each model provides a different pathway to help people grow, overcome
challenges, and reach their goals. By the end of this unit, you’ll understand how to choose
and apply these models effectively in your professional environment.
Did You Know?
The term “coach” originally described a carriage that transported people from one place to
another. Likewise, a coach “carries” individuals from where they are now to where they want
to be.
3.1. What Are Coaching Models?
A coaching model is a framework that helps coaches guide others through a process of
reflection, decision-making, and growth. These models simplify complex interactions and
provide structure, ensuring that coaching conversations are purposeful and productive.
They are especially useful in professional environments like ECD centres, where structured
guidance supports teachers and practitioners to build their skills and confidence.
Using coaching models helps:
● Provide a clear structure for discussions.
● Keep goals realistic and measurable.
● Strengthen communication and trust between coach and coachee.
● Encourage consistent professional growth.

3.3. Key Coaching Models
Different coaching models work in different contexts. Below are some of the most effective
and widely used ones, adapted for the ECD and educational environment.
● The GROW Model
Developed by Sir John Whitmore, Graham Alexander, and Alan Fine in the 1980s, the
GROW model is one of the most popular frameworks for personal and professional
development.
The word GROW stands for:
● Goal: Identify what the coachee wants to achieve.
● Reality: Explore the current situation, strengths, and challenges.
● Options: Brainstorm possible strategies and solutions.
● Will: Commit to specific actions and next steps.
This model helps individuals move from intention to action and is especially useful when you
need clear, measurable progress.
● The OSKAR Model
Developed by Mark McKergow and Paul Z. Jackson in 2002, the OSKAR model builds on
solution-focused principles. It encourages reflection and positive reinforcement.
The process follows these stages:
● Outcome: Define what success looks like.
● Scale: Measure current progress (for example, rating progress on a scale of 1–10).
● Know-how: Identify skills, resources, and strengths already available.
● Affirm & Action: Recognise what’s working and decide what to continue doing.
● Review: Reflect on progress and plan the next steps.
This model is great for encouraging confidence and motivation, especially when learners
need to see how far they’ve come.
Did You Know?
The OSKAR model focuses on what’s working rather than what’s broken — it’s built on the
belief that people learn best through encouragement and recognition.
24

● The STEPPPA Model
Created by Angus McLeod in 2003, the STEPPPA model recognises the role of emotions in
personal and professional growth.
The model includes these key elements:
● Subject: Identify the main issue or topic of discussion.
● Target: Set a realistic goal to achieve.
● Emotions: Understand the emotional impact of the situation.
● Perception: Explore how the goal affects others and the bigger picture.
● Plan: Develop an action plan.
● Pace: Establish achievable timelines and milestones.
● Action: Put the plan into motion and evaluate progress.
This model works well when emotions are tied to performance — for example, managing
workplace stress or dealing with self-doubt.
● The CLEAR Model
Developed by Peter Hawkins in 1985, the CLEAR model focuses on building a trusting,
reflective coaching relationship over time.
It unfolds in five steps:
● Contract: Set expectations, goals, and outcomes for the coaching process.
● Listen: Allow the coachee to speak openly while the coach listens carefully.
● Explore: Dig deeper into thoughts, emotions, and possible solutions.
● Action: Encourage commitment to specific steps.
● Review: Reflect on what worked, what didn’t, and what comes next.
This model is particularly useful for long-term professional development where trust and
reflection are key.
25

● The FUEL Model
Created by John Zenger and Kathleen Stinnett, the FUEL model encourages active
questioning and accountability.
It includes:
● Frame the Conversation: Define purpose and desired outcome.
● Understand the Current State: Explore present challenges and assumptions.
● Explore the Desired State: Discuss potential solutions and goals.
● Lay Out the Plan: Create a realistic plan with milestones.
This approach encourages employees to take responsibility for their own growth and is
effective in structured, ongoing coaching relationships.
● The AOR Model
The AOR model (Activities, Objectives, Results) focuses on problem-solving and
performance improvement through practice and feedback.
The process involves:
● Identifying key daily activities that influence performance.
● Setting achievable objectives linked to these activities.
● Reviewing the results to assess progress and adjust strategies.
This model is ideal for short, focused coaching sessions aimed at improving performance
quickly.
● The WOOP Model
Developed by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, the WOOP model helps people visualise
success and prepare for challenges.
It includes:
● Wish: Identify a meaningful goal.
● Outcome: Envision the positive results of achieving it.
● Obstacles: Identify potential challenges.
● Plan: Create concrete actions to overcome those obstacles.
This model is simple yet powerful — it helps coachees stay motivated and realistic while
working toward their dreams.

UNIT 4: KT0104 – Communication Behaviours and Communication
Processes
Communication is at the heart of every successful workplace — especially in coaching,
influencing, and conflict management. Whether you’re guiding a colleague, sharing
feedback, or resolving a disagreement, your ability to communicate clearly and respectfully
determines how effective you’ll be.
This unit explores the key communication behaviours and processes that shape how we
share ideas and connect with others. You’ll learn to recognise the difference between
effective and ineffective communication, and discover simple techniques to enhance
understanding and collaboration in professional settings.
Did You Know?
Only 7% of communication is verbal — the rest comes from tone, facial expressions,
and body language. That means what you don’t say often speaks louder than words.
4.1. Communication Behaviours
Communication behaviours are the actions people use to send, receive, and interpret
messages. They include verbal and non-verbal cues, listening, and feedback — all of which
influence how others understand your message.
4.1.1 Verbal Communication
Verbal communication includes the words we choose, how we say them, and the tone we
use.
Clarity is key — the clearer and more direct your message, the less room there is for
confusion.
Examples:
● Use plain, specific language instead of jargon.
● Adjust tone and pace to suit your audience.
● Avoid filler words or over-explaining.
A friendly tone builds trust, while a harsh tone can create distance or defensiveness.

Tip: Slow down your speech slightly during explanations. It gives others time to
process what you’re saying and shows patience and respect.
4.1.2 Non-Verbal Communication
Non-verbal cues include facial expressions, gestures, posture, and eye contact.
These signals often reveal more than our words. For instance:
● Open gestures and eye contact show honesty and attentiveness.
● Crossed arms, slouched posture, or avoiding gaze may signal discomfort or
disagreement.
Your body language should match your message. Saying “I’m happy to help” while frowning
sends mixed signals.

4.1.3 Listening Behaviours
Good communication isn’t just about speaking — it’s also about listening to understand.
Active listening involves:
● Paying full attention to the speaker.
● Avoiding interruptions.
● Using small affirmations (“I see,” “That makes sense”).
● Summarising what you heard to confirm understanding.
Empathetic listening goes further — it means understanding the other person’s feelings and
perspective. This type of listening builds trust, reduces tension, and makes people feel
valued.
4.1.4 Feedback Behaviours
Feedback is how we close the communication loop. It helps others know whether their
message was received and understood.
Good feedback is:
● Specific: Focus on actions, not personality.
● Constructive: Suggest improvement without criticism.
● Timely: Offer it soon after the event.
● Respectful: Consider tone and delivery.
Example: Instead of saying, “You’re not doing well in meetings,” say, “You raised good
points, but try to summarise your ideas more clearly at the end.”
