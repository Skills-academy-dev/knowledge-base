Unit 1: KT0101 – The History and Evolution of OHS Conditions and
Practices
1.1 Early Workplace Conditions
During the Industrial Revolution (18th–19th centuries), factories and mines were often unsafe
and unregulated. Workers faced long hours, exposure to hazardous materials, and unsafe
machinery.
Example: Children and adults worked underground in South African mines without protective
equipment, leading to injuries and diseases.
10

1.2 The Rise of Safety Legislation
● 1833 – The UK Factory Act introduced limits on working hours and improved
sanitation.
● 1911 – South Africa’s Mines and Works Act addressed safety in mining but was
poorly enforced.
● 1971 – The US established OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health
Administration) as a global benchmark.
● 1993 – South Africa passed the Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA),
providing a comprehensive legal framework for workplace safety.
Tip:
Legislation evolves from accidents, advocacy, and lessons learned always follow updates in
OHS law to stay compliant.
1.3 Modern Trends
Modern OHS emphasises:
● Proactive risk management rather than reactive enforcement.
● Digital tools like safety apps and analytics for real-time monitoring.
● Mental health awareness as part of holistic safety.
Did You Know? South Africa’s OHS Act (1993) aligns with ISO 45001, the international
standard for OHS management systems.
Reflection Questions
1. How did early workplace conditions influence modern OHS legislation?
2. Why is the 1993 OHS Act considered a landmark in South African
safety management?

Unit 2: KT0102 – The Purpose and Structure of OHS Legislation
2.1 Purpose of OHS Legislation
● Prevent injuries and illness through clear safety standards.
● Promote safe workplaces by enforcing employer duties.
● Protect workers’ rights to safety and fair treatment.
● Ensure compliance and enforcement through inspections and penalties.
Example: The OHS Act requires regular risk assessments and safety audits — preventing
accidents before they happen.
2.2 Basic Structure of OHS Legislation
● General Duty of Care – Employers must ensure health and safety “as far as reasonably
practicable.”
● Regulations & Standards – Specific rules for machinery, chemicals, ergonomics, and
emergencies.
● Rights & Responsibilities – Shared duties between employers and employees.
● Enforcement Mechanisms – Government inspections, compliance notices, and
penalties.
● Consultation & Participation – Health and Safety Committees and employee
involvement.
Did You Know?
Ignoring OHS regulations can lead to fines or imprisonment under the OHS Act (1993).
2.3 Link to the South African Constitution
● Section 23 of the Constitution (1996) guarantees the right to fair labour practices and a
safe workplace.
● The Department of Employment and Labour enforces OHS compliance.
● OHS legislation transforms constitutional rights into practical workplace duties.
12

Example: Establishing Health & Safety Committees ensures employee participation — a
direct reflection of constitutional democracy.
Reflection Questions
1. Why is the OHS Act important for upholding workers’ constitutional rights?
2. What role do safety committees play in linking legal compliance and
worker participation?

Unit 5: KT0105 – Regulatory Requirements Pertaining to Crane
Operations
5.1 Operator Competency
● Only certified operators may run cranes.
● Operators require accredited training and valid certification.
● Employers must verify credentials and maintain training records.
Example:
Before site work, a crane operator presents a valid NOSA certificate to the safety officer.
5.2 Inspection and Maintenance
● Cranes must undergo regular inspections by competent persons.
● Maintenance logs must include dates, faults, and corrective actions.
● Documentation must be stored securely and reviewed during audits.
Tip:
Keep a digital inspection checklist — easy to update and retrieve during compliance reviews.
5.3 Safe Operation
● Follow manufacturer instructions and load limits.
● Conduct pre-operation checks and site assessments.
● Stop operations during extreme weather.
Example:
A site manager halts crane operations during high winds to prevent tipping hazards.
5.4 Emergency and Reporting Procedures
● Have clear response plans for equipment failure
● Report incidents to supervisors immediately.
● Investigate all near-misses for root causes.

Introduction
Occupational Health, Occupational Safety, and Environmental Management are three
interconnected pillars that form the backbone of a responsible and sustainable workplace.
Occupational Health focuses on protecting the physical and mental wellbeing of employees by
preventing work-related illnesses and promoting wellness. Occupational Safety ensures that
work processes, equipment, and environments are free from hazards that could cause injury or
harm. Environmental Management, on the other hand, deals with the organisation’s impact on
the natural environment—ensuring that operations are conducted responsibly, in compliance
with laws, and with care for future generations. Together, these disciplines create a balanced
approach to managing people, processes, and the planet, ensuring that workplaces remain
safe, healthy, and environmentally sustainable.
Unit 1: KT0201 – Definition of Occupational Health
1.1 Understanding Occupational Health
Occupational Health (OH) focuses on the physical, mental, and social wellbeing of workers in all
occupations. Its goal is to prevent work-related illnesses and promote optimal health among
employees.
Key Objectives:
● Prevent diseases caused by work conditions.
● Promote healthy working environments.
● Support early detection and treatment of occupational illnesses.
Example:
A textile factory introduces regular medical check-ups to detect respiratory problems caused by
cotton dust exposure.
1.2 Components of Occupational Health
1. Health Surveillance – Monitoring employee health to identify work-related diseases
early.
2. Workplace Hygiene – Maintaining cleanliness to prevent infections and contamination.
3. Health Promotion – Educating workers about nutrition, exercise, and stress
management.
4. Rehabilitation and Return-to-Work – Supporting recovery and reintegration after illness
or injury.
24

Tip:
An effective health programme benefits both employees and employers — healthy workers are
more productive and have fewer absences.
1.3 Legal Framework
Occupational Health in South Africa is guided by:
● Occupational Health and Safety Act (85 of 1993)
● Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act (COIDA)
● National Health Act (61 of 2003)
Did You Know?
Employers are legally required to report occupational diseases such as hearing loss, dermatitis,
or lung conditions to the Department of Employment and Labour.
Reflection Questions

Unit 2: KT0202 – Definition of Occupational Safety
2.1 Understanding Occupational Safety
Occupational Safety involves protecting workers from injury or harm by controlling workplace
hazards and risks. It focuses on accident prevention, risk management, and safe systems of
work.
Core Functions:
● Identifying potential hazards.
● Assessing and controlling risks.
● Developing and enforcing safety policies and procedures.
Example:
A construction company requires all workers to complete daily toolbox talks before starting work
to discuss site hazards.
2.2 Safety Management Elements
Element Description
Hazard Identification Detecting physical, chemical, or procedural risks.
Risk Assessment Evaluating the likelihood and impact of each hazard.
Control Measures Implementing strategies to eliminate or reduce risks.
Training and Supervision Ensuring staff understand and follow safe practices.
Monitoring and Review Regularly checking the effectiveness of safety measures.
26

Tip:
Apply the Hierarchy of Controls eliminate hazards first, and use PPE as a last line of defence.
2.3 Legal Framework
Key South African laws and standards:
● Occupational Health and Safety Act (1993)
● Mine Health and Safety Act (1996)
● Driven Machinery Regulations
● Construction Regulations (2014)
Did You Know?
Under South African law, every employer must appoint a Competent Person to oversee safety
systems and report incidents.
Reflection Questions
1. How does hazard identification differ from risk assessment?
2. Why is worker participation important in maintaining occupational safety?

Unit 3: KT0203 – Definition of Environmental Management
3.1 Understanding Environmental Management
Environmental Management is the systematic process of protecting the natural environment by
minimising the negative impact of workplace activities.
It ensures that business operations are environmentally sustainable and compliant with legal
standards.
Main Objectives:
● Prevent environmental pollution.
● Conserve natural resources.
● Ensure compliance with environmental regulations.
● Promote sustainable development.
Example:
A manufacturing plant installs wastewater treatment systems to reduce pollution before
releasing it into municipal drains.
3.2 Core Principles of Environmental Management
1. Sustainability – Balancing human needs with environmental preservation.
2. Pollution Prevention – Minimising emissions, waste, and contamination.
3. Waste Management – Reducing, reusing, and recycling materials.
Compliance and Monitoring – Following environmental laws and conducting regular
audits.
Tip:
Implement the 3Rs Principle – Reduce, Reuse, Recycle to promote sustainable practices at
work.
28

3.3 Legal Framework
Environmental management in South Africa is governed by:
● National Environmental Management Act (NEMA), Act 107 of 1998
● Hazardous Substances Act, Act 15 of 1973
● Air Quality Act, Act 39 of 2004
● Waste Act, Act 59 of 2008
Did You Know?
Organisations that fail to comply with NEMA can face heavy fines or closure due to
environmental pollution.
Reflection Questions
1. What are the key goals of environmental management?
2. How does pollution prevention contribute to workplace sustainability?
