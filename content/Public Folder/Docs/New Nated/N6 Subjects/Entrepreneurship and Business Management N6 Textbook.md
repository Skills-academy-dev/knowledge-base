Introduction
Every successful business begins with a clear plan and a strong sense of direction. As an
entrepreneur, your ability to think strategically determines how effectively your business will
adapt, grow, and remain competitive in a changing environment. Strategic planning provides
a structured approach to defining where your business is now, where you want it to be in the
future, and how you will get there.
In this module, you will explore the strategic planning process step by step — from setting a
mission and objectives to evaluating your progress. You will learn how entrepreneurs use
planning tools such as the Ansoff Matrix and BCG Growth Matrix to identify growth
opportunities and manage business risks. You will also develop decision-making skills using
methods like cost–benefit analysis, risk matrices, and brainstorming techniques, which
help you choose the best course of action for your enterprise.
Strategic planning and sound decision-making are essential for ensuring business
sustainability, especially in South Africa’s dynamic economy, where entrepreneurs face
challenges such as competition, technological change, and resource constraints. Through
this module, you will gain the knowledge and confidence to plan effectively, make informed
decisions, and guide your business towards long-term success.

Unit 1: Introduction to Strategic Planning
Definitions:
● Mission: A concise statement of your business’s purpose and customers (e.g.
“Provide affordable, fresh lunch options to college students in Tembisa”).
● Vision: A picture of the future you want (e.g. “Be the most trusted student food brand
in our district”). (Used throughout N6 to guide objectives and strategy.)
● Objectives: Specific targets that turn the mission/vision into action (later in the
module you’ll make them SMART and time-bound).
● Strategy: The overall game plan to reach those objectives (e.g. low-cost menu + fast
service + campus partnerships).
● Implementation & Evaluation: Put plans into action and measure results so you can
improve continuously.

1.1 What is strategic planning?
Strategic planning is a structured, ongoing process that aligns the purpose of the business
with realistic goals, deliberate choices, and coordinated action. It helps a small business
owner move from “good ideas” to “repeatable results”, and to adapt whenever customers,
competitors or costs change.
10

Characteristics of good strategic planning
● Ongoing cycle, not a once-off document.
● Focused: a few priorities rather than a long wish-list.
● Evidence-based: uses simple data (sales, costs, customer feedback).
● Participatory: involves the people who will deliver the work.
● Time-bound with clear review points.
We’ll work through this cycle step-by-step in Unit 2, then practise using real SME
examples.
1.2 Why entrepreneurs need it
● Focus: It limits distractions so scarce time and cash go to the best opportunities.
● Fit: It aligns product, pricing, place, and promotion with the target customer.
● Confidence: It gives a reasoned basis for decisions (useful with partners and
lenders).
● Learning: It builds a habit of testing, measuring, and improving.
● Resilience: It prepares you for shocks (e.g., input price spikes, load-shedding, new
entrants).

Unit 2: The Strategic Planning Process
In Unit 1 you met the “why” of strategic planning. In Unit 2, we get into the “how”—the
practical, repeatable steps managers use to turn vision into action. Think of this as your
business GPS: set the destination, scan the route, choose the lane, then drive and
course-correct.
2.1 The Strategic Planning Process (9 Steps)
Step 1: Confirm Vision & Mission
● Vision = the ideal future state (“where we’re going”).
● Mission = the business’s purpose and primary activities (“why we exist”).
● Deliverable: Short, clear statements that guide all downstream choices.
● Quick Test: If a proposed objective doesn’t serve the mission or vision, it doesn’t
make the cut.

Objectives convert strategy into measurable targets.

● Measurable: numbers/indicators?
● Achievable: realistic with current constraints?
● Relevant: aligned to mission/vision?
● Time-bound: deadline or cadence?

● “Secure 3 corporate coffee carts by Q3 with 12-month contracts.”

The “SMART” objective framework has been used in management training since the early

2.2 The Continuous Cycle: Plan → Implement → Evaluate → Improve
Think of strategies like breathing: inhale (plan), exhale (do), check pulse (evaluate), get
fitter (improve)—repeat.
2.2.1 Plan
● Purpose: Choose direction and priorities.
● Key questions: What’s changing around us (PESTLE)? What can/can’t we do
(SWOT)? What 2–4 SMART objectives matter now?
● Outputs: Objectives, strategies (e.g., Ansoff choice), action plan (owners/dates),
budget, top-5 risk register.
2.2.2 Implement
● Purpose: Turn choices into results.
● Key questions: Who does what by when? What resources are needed this week?
● Outputs: Tasks executed, spend vs budget, progress logs, issues list.
22

2.2.3 Evaluate
● Purpose: Learn fast from reality.
● Key questions: Are KPIs on track? What surprised us (good/bad)? What must stop,
start, continue?
● Outputs: KPI dashboard, variance notes, decisions (double-down, fix, or pivot).
2.2.4 Improve (Back to Plan)
● Purpose: Make the plan sharper each cycle.
● Key questions: Which objective needs a new tactic? Which risk needs mitigation
now?
● Outputs: Updated plan, reallocated budget, refined KPIs.
Cadence that works for SMEs:
Weekly 20–30 min check (KPIs + blockers) • Monthly 60–90 min review (objectives,
budget, risks) • Quarterly half-day reset (bigger bets via Ansoff/BCG).

Characteristics of Effective Strategic Planning
1. Aligned – Vision/Mission → Objectives → Actions line up.
Watch-out: Pet projects that don’t serve the mission.
2. Prioritised – Few big rocks, not 100 pebbles.
Tool: Decision Matrix to rank options.
3. Evidence-based – Light data beats loud opinions.
Tool: PESTLE + simple customer counts, daily sales, complaint logs.
4. Time-bounded – Clear deadlines and review cadence.
Tool: Weekly KPI check; monthly mini-retro.
5. Adaptive – Willing to pivot with facts.
Tool: PDCA loop; exit rules for experiments.
6. Resource-aware – Budget and people fit the plan.
Tool: One-page budget tied to each objective.

2.3 Decision-Making Tools to Support the Cycle
A) Cost–Benefit Analysis (CBA)
● Use for: Quick financial comparison.
● How: List costs/benefits for each option; score or quantify; compute Net Benefit.
● Upgrade (optional): Discount multi-year cash flows at 10–12% for a rough NPV.
B) Risk Matrix (5×5)
● Use for: Top-5 threats/opportunities.
● How: Likelihood (1–5) × Impact (1–5). Treat ≥12 as urgent; assign owner & deadline.
C) Decision Matrix (Weighted Criteria)
● Use for: Non-financial trade-offs (brand control, speed, skills, compliance).
● How: Weight criteria (100%), score options, multiply & sum. Highest ≠
automatic—apply judgement.
Mini SA Case: Vuka Brew Cart—One Full Cycle
● Plan: Ansoff = Market Development (add 2 office parks); Objective: lift
average daily sales R3 000 → R4 200 by Month 6; Actions: pitches, mobile
POS, pop-ups; Budget: R18k capex; Risks: load-shedding (15), supplier
delay (8).
● Implement: 10 pitches, 2 pilots; inverter installed; loyalty QR live.
● Evaluate (Week 4): Leads → Pitches → Pilots → Contracts funnel shows
strong conversion in Park A, weak in Park B; COGS at 37% (target ≤35%).
● Improve: Drop Park B; negotiate bean contract (COGS ↓ to 34%); add
breakfast bundle where conversion is best.
