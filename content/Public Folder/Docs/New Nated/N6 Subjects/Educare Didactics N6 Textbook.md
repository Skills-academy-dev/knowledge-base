INTRODUCTION
Didactics is All Around You
Didactics is the heart of early childhood education. It is the study and practice of how children
learn and how educarers teach in ways that are meaningful, creative, and effective. By
studying Educare Didactics N6, you will strengthen your understanding of child development,
teaching strategies, and classroom organisation. In simple terms, Didactics is about learning
how to plan, present, and assess educational activities that support children’s holistic
growth — whether in language, play, art, movement, or music.
By learning Educare Didactics, you will:
● Develop strong teaching skills – Learn how to plan lessons, guide play, and use
creative methods to support children’s learning in different areas of development.
● Understand how children learn – Explore how children think, feel, and grow through
play, interaction, and exploration, and how to adapt activities to meet their needs.
● Encourage creativity and curiosity – Use expressive arts, music, and movement to
help children express themselves and build confidence.
● Promote effective communication – Learn how to observe children carefully, record
progress, and communicate with parents and colleagues in a professional manner.
● Create inclusive and safe learning environments – Develop the ability to design
spaces that are welcoming, stimulating, and suitable for all children.
● Prepare for your future in early childhood education – Gain essential knowledge
and practical skills that will help you excel as an educarer, assistant teacher, or
childcare professional.
● Make a difference – By mastering Didactics, you will not only teach but also inspire
young minds, helping each child reach their full potential through learning and play.

Introduction
Observation is an essential skill for every educarer. It allows you to understand each child’s
development, interests, and needs. By observing, you learn how children interact with others,
how they approach challenges, and how they learn through play and daily activities.
Observation is more than simply watching—it involves focused attention, accurate recording,
and fair interpretation. A good educarer observes objectively, without bias, and uses the
information gathered to support each child’s growth. Through observation, you can identify early
signs of difficulties, celebrate progress, and share valuable feedback with parents and
colleagues.

Unit 1: Understanding Observation
1.1 Definition and Purpose
Observation means carefully watching a child’s behaviour, activities, and interactions in a
natural setting without interfering. It provides insight into how children develop physically,
socially, emotionally, and intellectually.
1.2 Purpose of Observation:
● To understand individual differences and learning styles.
● To identify developmental milestones and possible delays.
● To plan suitable learning activities based on observed needs.
● To assess progress and evaluate programme effectiveness.
● To communicate accurately with parents and other professionals.
1.3 Key Terminology
● Evidence: Information or proof collected through careful watching or listening.
● Observation: The act of watching a child at play or work without disturbing the activity.
● Recording: Writing down or documenting what you see and hear.
● Objective: Based on facts and what you observe, not on personal feelings or opinions.
● Subjective: Based on personal judgment or opinion rather than evidence.
1.4 Tips for Effective Observation
1. Be Objective: Focus on what the child does or says, not what you think or feel.
2. Be Consistent: Observe at different times and during various activities.
3. Stay Unobtrusive: Watch quietly and do not interfere with the child’s play.
4. Use All Senses: Pay attention to visual, verbal, and non-verbal cues.
5. Record Immediately: Write your notes as soon as possible to ensure accuracy.
6. Respect Privacy: Keep all records confidential and share only with authorised persons.

Did You Know?
Observation is one of the oldest and most reliable methods in child development studies.
Even famous educators like Maria Montessori used it to design effective learning
environments.
Reflection Question
Think about a time when you observed a child learning through play.
What did you notice about how the child explored or communicated?
How could that observation help you plan future activities?
Tip
Always use neutral language in your observation notes.
Instead of writing “Lebo was naughty,” write “Lebo left the table before finishing his drawing.”
This keeps the observation factual and useful for assessment.

Unit 2: Methods of Observation
Observation is not a once-off activity — it happens throughout the day, from when children
arrive until they go home. Every routine, playtime, or learning experience provides valuable
information about how a child grows and interacts. To be effective, an educarer must use
structured methods that ensure observations are accurate, objective, and helpful for
assessment and planning.
2.1 Continuous Observation
Observation should take place naturally during the child’s daily routine. It includes everything
from watching a child’s body language to listening to their conversations and noticing how they
solve problems.
● Be patient — children show different behaviours at different times.
● Record small but meaningful actions that indicate development.
● Observe both group and individual activities.
Did You Know?
Children behave differently in different settings — for example, they may be quiet during
group time but more expressive during outdoor play. Observing across multiple contexts
gives a fairer picture of their development.
2.2 Methods of Observation
There are several ways to observe and record children’s behaviour. Each method has its own
purpose and use:
1. Anecdotal Records
These are short, factual notes describing what happened during a specific incident.
● Focus on one event or behaviour.
● Record only what you see and hear.
● Include the date, time, and setting.
14

Example:
During outdoor play, Naledi built a sandcastle with three towers. She invited two friends
to help and explained where to place the shells.
Anecdotal records are helpful for identifying progress and patterns in social interaction,
language, and problem-solving.
2. Checklists
Checklists contain a list of skills or behaviours that the child may show. The educarer ticks each
skill when observed.
● Useful for large groups.
● Quick to complete.
● Helps identify which skills need more support.
Example: A motor-skill checklist might include items such as:
● Can balance on one foot for 5 seconds.
● Can cut along a straight line with scissors.
● Can throw and catch a large ball.
3. Rating Scales
A rating scale records the degree or frequency of a child’s behaviour.
For example, you might rate how often a child participates in group discussions:
● 1 = Rarely
● 2 = Sometimes
● 3 = Often
● 4 = Always
Rating scales are useful for tracking improvement or identifying areas needing encouragement.
15

4. Time Sampling
This method involves observing a child at set time intervals to see what they are doing.
● Helps identify how a child spends their time during the day.
● Useful for assessing attention span or activity preferences.
Example: Observe every 10 minutes during free play and note what the child is engaged in
(building blocks, reading, or talking).
5. Event Sampling
This focuses on specific types of behaviour or incidents whenever they occur.
● Useful for understanding the cause and pattern of certain actions (e.g., aggression,
frustration, or sharing).
● Records what happens before and after the behaviour to understand triggers.
Example: Recording each time a child grabs a toy from another and noting what happens next.
6. Work Samples and Portfolios
Children’s work, such as drawings, writing attempts, or crafts, can serve as valuable observation
evidence.
● Collect samples over time to track progress.
● Encourage children to describe their work to you.
● Keep the portfolio neat and labelled with the child’s name and date.
Tip
Always date and sign your observations. This helps you track a child’s development
accurately and discuss progress confidently during meetings with parents or
supervisors.
16

2.3 Choosing the Right Method
The method you choose depends on your purpose:
● For detailed understanding: Use anecdotal records or event sampling.
● For skill tracking: Use checklists or rating scales.
● For long-term growth: Use portfolios and work samples.
The best practice is to combine different methods for a complete and balanced view of each
child’s development.
Reflection Question
Which observation method do you think would be most suitable for a child who is very active
and often moves from one activity to another? Explain your choice.
Practical Example: Observation in Action
During free play, Zanele spent most of her time building with blocks but avoided group
games. Anecdotal records over three weeks showed gradual changes as she started
helping others with their block structures. This observation helped the educarer plan
more small-group play to strengthen Zanele’s social confidence.

Unit 3: Record Keeping and Reporting
Effective record keeping is an essential part of every educarer’s role. It helps ensure that
observations are not forgotten, information is accurate, and children’s progress is tracked over
time. Records provide evidence of what has been observed, and they guide future lesson planning,
support, and communication with parents.
Good record keeping turns observation into action — it allows educators to respond meaningfully to
children’s individual needs and to share progress clearly and confidently with others.
3.1 Importance of Record Keeping
Record keeping serves several key purposes:
● Evidence of development: Records show each child’s learning journey and
developmental milestones.
● Assessment tool: Records help measure progress and identify areas needing support.
● Planning aid: Helps the educarer design activities that suit children’s current skills and
interests.
● Legal requirement: Maintains accountability and transparency in childcare and education.
● Communication tool: Provides accurate information for discussions with parents or
other professionals.
Did You Know?
Keeping records is not just about storing information — it’s about using that information
to make learning more meaningful and responsive to each child.

3.3 Guidelines for Effective Record Keeping
To ensure that records are useful, follow these guidelines:
1. Be Objective: Record facts, not opinions.
● Correct: “Thabo stacked four blocks before they fell.”
● Incorrect: “Thabo was being lazy today.”
2. Be Clear: Use simple, accurate language.
3. Be Consistent: Record regularly to keep information up to date.
4. Be Confidential: Keep all child records private and secure.
5. Be Organised: Use standard formats and keep records neat and easy to read.
Did You Know?
A well-maintained record file can reveal patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed —
such as a child’s consistent difficulty with certain skills or social interactions.
3.4 Reporting Observations
Reporting is the process of sharing your recorded observations with parents, colleagues, or
supervisors. Reports turn raw data into useful information for decision-making.
When Reporting to Parents:
● Use positive and encouraging language.
● Highlight the child’s strengths before mentioning areas of improvement.
● Avoid educational jargon — use terms parents understand.
● Always link your comments to factual observations.
Example:
“Ayanda enjoys storytelling time and can recall details from stories. We are now
encouraging her to use new words in her own stories.”

● Focus on developmental goals.

Unit 4: Communicating with Parents
Effective communication between educarers and parents is essential for the holistic
development of young children. Observation provides valuable insights into a child’s growth, and
these findings must be shared respectfully and clearly with parents. Open communication
strengthens trust, builds partnerships, and ensures that both the home and school environments
work together to support the child’s learning journey.
4.1 The Importance of Communication
Parents are the first and most important educators in a child’s life. When they are informed and
involved, children feel more secure and perform better. Communication about a child’s
development helps parents understand:
● Their child’s strengths and achievements.
● Areas where extra support may be needed.
● How to reinforce learning at home.
● How the educarer is supporting the child’s development at school.
Did You Know?
Research shows that children whose parents communicate regularly with their
teachers develop stronger social and language skills in the early years.
24

4.3 Guidelines for Effective Communication
Successful communication with parents requires empathy, professionalism, and confidentiality.
Key Guidelines:
1. Be Respectful: Always value the parent’s perspective and avoid judgment.
2. Be Clear: Use simple, easy-to-understand language.
3. Be Honest: Share both strengths and challenges truthfully but sensitively.
4. Be Confidential: Never discuss one child’s progress with another parent.
5. Be Supportive: Offer practical ideas for how parents can help at home.
4.4 Communicating Observation Results
When sharing observation results, focus on the child’s development rather than labels or
comparisons.
Example Approach:
1. Begin with a friendly introduction.
2. Highlight positive progress observed in different areas.
3. Discuss challenges factually, referring to your observations (not opinions).
4. Suggest ways the parent can support learning at home.
5. End the conversation with encouragement and appreciation.
Did You Know?
Parents respond more positively when educators use examples of what the child can do
rather than what they cannot do. For instance, say “Lerato is beginning to recognise shapes”
instead of “Lerato doesn’t know her shapes yet.”

4.6 Building Positive Relationships
Good relationships with parents are built over time. Parents should feel that the educarer
genuinely cares about their child’s wellbeing.
Ways to Strengthen Relationships:
● Greet parents warmly each day.
● Send home positive notes about achievements.
● Celebrate special occasions and cultural events together.
● Be available to listen when parents have concerns.

Reflection Question
Think about how you would handle a situation where a parent becomes defensive after you
share an observation about their child’s behaviour.
How could you stay professional and positive while maintaining trust?
Practical Example: Communication in Practice
After observing that Musa was often distracted during story time, the educarer invited his
parents for a short meeting. Instead of focusing on the problem, she shared a few of
Musa’s drawings and explained how he pays attention during art activities. Together, they
decided to create a short reading routine at home. Two weeks later, Musa’s focus
improved, and his parents felt encouraged to stay involved.

Summary
Observation is a key part of early childhood education. It allows the educarer to understand
each child’s unique way of learning, behaving, and developing. By carefully observing and
recording children’s actions, the educarer can plan suitable activities, support individual needs,

for assessment, planning, and communication.
● Reporting must always be factual, professional, and positive.
● Effective communication with parents builds trust and ensures continuity between
home and school.
● Educarers can use informal talks, meetings, newsletters, or written reports to

● Observation, record keeping, and communication work together to support each child’s
overall development.
● These practices help the educarer make informed decisions, plan suitable learning
experiences, and build strong relationships with families.
