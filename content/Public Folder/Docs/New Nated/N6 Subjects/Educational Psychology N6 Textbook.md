Introduction

In the world of education, knowledge of child development and learning behaviour is essential

for every teacher and caregiver. Educational Psychology explores how children grow physically,

mentally, socially, and emotionally. It helps educators understand why children behave in certain

ways and how to create a positive learning environment that supports their development.

Whether you are beginning your Educare studies or expanding your teaching knowledge, this

textbook will equip you with practical tools, insights, and real-life examples relevant to South

African classrooms.

Educational Psychology is one of the most valuable subjects for anyone working with children. It

helps you understand how children learn best and how their environment, emotions, and

experiences shape who they become.

Here’s why Educational Psychology is important:

● Understanding Development: Learn how children grow and change from birth through

early school years.

● Improving Behaviour Management: Gain strategies to handle inappropriate behaviour

calmly and effectively.

● Supporting Learning: Discover how to adapt teaching methods to different learning

needs and abilities.

● Encouraging School Readiness: Understand what makes a child ready for formal

learning.

● Building Confidence as an Educator: Develop skills to support children’s emotional

well-being and classroom success.

In short, studying Educational Psychology helps you become an effective, empathetic educator

who understands both the mind and heart of the child.

Introduction

The early years of a child’s life form the foundation for all future learning and development.

During this period, children experience rapid growth in their physical, emotional, social, and

cognitive abilities. These early experiences strongly influence how a child will later adapt to

school and respond to formal learning.

School readiness refers to the stage when a child has developed the maturity, skills, and

attitudes needed to succeed in school. It goes beyond knowing numbers or letters — it includes

emotional confidence, curiosity, independence, and social interaction. A child who is not ready

may struggle to adjust, while a ready child learns easily and feels secure.

This module explores what school readiness means, how it differs from school maturity, and

how parents and educators can support children in reaching this stage. It also examines key

areas such as motor and perceptual development, language, and early mathematical skills, all

of which prepare children for successful learning.

Unit 1: Understanding School Readiness

Children’s early experiences shape who they become and how well they adjust to formal

learning. This unit explores what it means for a child to be ready for school. You will learn about

school maturity and readiness, how they differ, and the roles that families and educators play in

preparing young learners.

By the end of this unit, you should understand how to identify when a child is ready for school,

recognise signs of unreadiness, and know how to assess readiness through both formal and

informal methods. These concepts are vital for early childhood educators and anyone working

with children entering Grade R or Grade 1.

1.1 Background

The early years of life are the most critical period for development. During this time, the brain

grows faster than at any other stage, forming millions of new connections every second. This

rapid development means that children are especially sensitive to their environment and the

quality of care and stimulation they receive.

Early experiences influence how children think, move, communicate, and relate to others.

Educational psychology describes this as holistic development — where physical, cognitive,

emotional, and social growth occur together. When these areas develop in harmony, children

are better prepared for school and later success in life.

The Role of the Home and Pre-School Environment

Learning begins long before a child enters formal school. The home and pre-school

environment both play vital roles in shaping early learning habits and readiness.

The Home Environment

A child’s family is their first teacher. Parents and caregivers influence learning by providing

emotional support, communication, and stimulation.

Ways the Home Supports Readiness:

● Reading stories and encouraging questions.

● Providing toys and materials that promote creativity and fine motor skills.

● Offering love and stability, which help children feel secure and ready to learn.

● Setting simple routines that mirror school structure, such as meal and bedtime

schedules.

The Pre-School Environment

Pre-schools and ECD centres build on what the child has learned at home by introducing

structured play, group work, and exposure to learning materials.

1.2 Getting Ready for School

Getting ready for school involves more than just knowing letters and numbers. It means being

ready physically, socially, emotionally, and intellectually for the formal demands of learning.

School Maturity

Before entering school, children need to reach a level of biological and emotional growth known

as school maturity. This maturity ensures that they are physically and emotionally ready to cope

with formal learning demands.

12

School maturity refers to the natural stage of growth and development where a child is able to

manage school-related activities such as sitting still, concentrating, and following structured

instructions.

Significance

School maturity allows children to:

● Adapt easily to routines and classroom expectations.

● Develop patience and emotional control.

● Show curiosity and motivation to learn.

● Form positive relationships with teachers and peers.

Definition

School maturity: The child’s natural stage of development that enables them to handle

formal education successfully.

Table: Indicators of Maturity

Developmental Area Indicators of Maturity

Physical Good muscle control, posture, and coordination.

Emotional Can separate from parents calmly; manages frustration.

Social Shares, takes turns, and respects group rules.

Cognitive Understands simple patterns, remembers instructions, and asks

13

Example

In a Grade R class in Limpopo, children who can follow story sequences, recognise numbers up

to 10, and cooperate during playtime show signs of maturity and readiness for Grade 1.

Tip

Use a table or chart to remember the five development areas and their indicators — physical,

emotional, social, cognitive, and language. You can be asked to name two indicators of

maturity or identify a child who is not yet mature in a case study.

School Readiness

While school maturity comes naturally with growth, school readiness develops through experience and environmental support. A mature child may not be ready if they lack exposure to learning experiences, while a less mature child may succeed with the right guidance.

School readiness is the level of physical, emotional, social, and intellectual development that

allows a child to meet school expectations confidently.

A Child Who Is Not Ready for School

When a child lacks the skills or confidence needed for school, they may experience anxiety,

frustration, or poor academic performance. Recognising these signs early allows teachers and

parents to provide additional support.

16

Common Indicators:

● Short attention span and easy distractibility.

● Poor coordination or clumsy movements.

● Limited vocabulary or unclear speech.

● Difficulty following instructions.

● Resistance to routine or separation from parents.

● Low self-confidence or social withdrawal.

Example

Nomsa, aged six, enjoys drawing but struggles to listen to instructions and complete tasks. Her

teacher notices she often isolates herself and becomes upset when corrected. These are signs

of emotional unreadiness.

Definition

Readiness delay: A temporary lack of maturity or skill needed to begin formal schooling.

Tip

Practise identifying signs of unreadiness in case studies. For example, poor concentration,

weak coordination, or anxiety when separating from parents are clues that may appear in an

exam scenario.

Test for School Readiness

Testing for school readiness helps educators and parents decide if a child can manage the

academic and emotional demands of formal schooling.

17

Formal Assessments:

● Conducted by psychologists or trained specialists.

● Measure memory, coordination, reasoning, and emotional stability.

● Common examples include:

○ Aptitude Tests (e.g., the Junior South African Individual Scale).

○ Motor Skills Tests for balance and fine motor control.

○ Language Assessments for comprehension and expression.

Informal Assessments:

● Done by teachers and parents through observation and play.

● May include:

○ Watching how a child listens and follows directions.

○ Drawing or cutting tasks to evaluate coordination.

○ Storytelling activities to assess communication skills.

○ Group activities to observe social behaviour.

Did You Know?

Informal assessment through play is often more accurate for young children because it shows

how they behave naturally, without anxiety or pressure.

Tip

Know the difference between formal and informal assessments. Examiners often ask you to

give one example of each. Remember:

● Formal = professional testing (e.g., aptitude or motor skills tests).

● Informal = observation during play or class activities.

Unit 2: School Readiness Programme

This unit explains how children develop the physical, perceptual, and cognitive skills needed to

succeed in formal schooling. The school readiness programme is designed to strengthen these

skills through structured play and purposeful activities.

Each part of the programme — from large-muscle development to language and mathematical

concepts — helps prepare children for reading, writing, and reasoning. By the end of this unit,

you should understand how to support the growth of motor, perceptual, language, and

mathematical abilities in young learners and how to design classroom or home activities that

encourage readiness.

2.1 General Gross Motor Skills

Gross motor skills involve the use of large muscles in the arms, legs, and torso. These

movements support a child’s ability to run, jump, balance, and play confidently. Strong gross

motor skills are the foundation for later physical and academic tasks, including sitting upright,

writing, and concentrating for longer periods.

Importance of Large-Muscle Movement

● Promotes healthy growth and body strength.

● Improves coordination and confidence.

● Helps maintain attention during learning.

● Supports brain development through active movement.

Examples of Gross Motor Activities:

● Running, skipping, and jumping games.

● Throwing and catching balls.

● Crawling under and over objects.

● Dancing to music or rhythm games.

22

Definition

Gross motor skills: Movements that use large muscles of the body for coordination, balance,

and strength.

Did You Know?

According to the Education Psychology N6 (2023) textbook, children who engage in regular

outdoor play develop stronger focus and better posture for classroom learning.

Tip

Be prepared to define gross motor skills and give one example. In exams, these questions

usually test your ability to link physical activities (like running or balancing) to learning

readiness.

2.2 Body Awareness

Body awareness is a child’s understanding of their body and how it moves in space. It helps

them coordinate movement and control their actions when playing, writing, or performing

physical activities.

Children who develop body awareness early are better at following directions like “touch your

left shoulder” or “stand behind the chair.”

Laterality – Awareness of Left and Right

Laterality is the ability to identify and use the left and right sides of the body. It is essential for

tasks such as reading and writing, where directionality matters (left to right on a page).

23

Activities to Develop Laterality:

● Games like “Simon Says” using left and right movements.

● Tracing shapes from left to right.

● Sorting objects on the left or right side of the table.

Definition

Laterality: Awareness of the left and right sides of the body and the ability to coordinate them.

Dominance – Preference for One Hand, Eye, or Foot

Dominance means a child develops a preferred hand, eye, or foot for tasks like drawing, writing,

or kicking. It usually appears between the ages of four and six.

Examples of Dominance:

● Writing mostly with the right hand.

● Kicking a ball with the same foot.

● Looking through a telescope with one dominant eye.

Did You Know?

Some children show mixed dominance, meaning they use different hands or eyes for different

tasks — this is normal and develops with time.

24

Spatial Orientation (Position in Space)

Spatial orientation refers to understanding where the body is in space and how it relates to objects

and people. It is important for activities like drawing, letter formation, and reading direction.

Example:

● Recognising positions such as “under”, “behind”, “next to”, or “in front of”.

● Building with blocks or puzzles.

● Following floor patterns or obstacle courses.

Definition

Spatial orientation: The ability to understand position, direction, and movement in space.

Midline – Crossing the Body’s Midpoint

The midline is an imaginary line down the centre of the body. Crossing the midline (using both

sides of the body together) supports coordination between the brain’s two hemispheres and is

vital for writing and reading.

Activities to Strengthen Midline Crossing:

● Drawing large figure eights with both hands.

● Touching opposite hand to knee.

● Threading beads across the body.

● Swinging a bat or skipping rope.

25

Did You Know?

Crossing the midline improves both hand coordination and eye tracking, which are crucial for

reading fluency.

Tip

Revise the terms laterality, dominance, spatial orientation, and midline. A “match the column”

or “fill in the blank” question could test your understanding of these. Make a short table to help

you remember what each one means.

2.3 Balance

Balance is the ability to maintain body stability whether moving or standing still. It helps children

control movement, posture, and coordination — skills needed for sitting upright, walking in line,

or performing sports.

Examples of Balance Activities:

● Walking along a straight line or low beam.

● Standing on one leg.

● Playing “statue” games that require freezing in position.

● Balancing beanbags on heads or shoulders.

Definition

Balance: The ability to maintain control of body position while moving or still.

26

Tip

When answering questions about balance, always link it to classroom readiness — for

example, children who can balance well are better at sitting still and focusing. Include this in

long-answer or application questions.

2.4 Large Muscle Movements

Large muscle movements strengthen the body’s major muscles and build endurance. They form

the basis for athletic ability and overall health.

Activities That Develop Large Muscles:

● Running and skipping games.

● Hopping races or climbing frames.

● Tug-of-war for arm and leg strength.

● Throwing and catching beanbags or balls.

Did You Know?

Physical movement increases blood flow to the brain, helping children concentrate better and

remember new information.

Tip

Remember that large muscle movements are the foundation for coordination. You can be

asked to list two activities that develop large muscles — e.g., skipping or climbing.

27

2.5 Fine Motor Skills

Fine motor skills involve small muscle movements, especially those in the hands and fingers.

These skills are necessary for writing, drawing, dressing, and manipulating small tools.

Children with strong fine motor skills can control pencils, scissors, and other classroom

materials more easily.

Eye–Hand Coordination

Eye–hand coordination means the eyes guide the hands to complete tasks accurately.

Examples of Activities:

● Cutting shapes along lines.

● Threading beads or pasta.

● Drawing and colouring within outlines.

● Catching small objects like beanbags.

Definition

Eye–hand coordination: The ability to use the eyes and hands together for precise

movement.

Eye–Foot Coordination and Small Muscle Movements

Eye–foot coordination involves using the eyes and feet together for accurate movement, such

as kicking a ball or stepping onto a mark.

Activities:

● Kicking or dribbling a ball between cones.

● Hopscotch games.

● Jumping over small objects or rope.

28

Did You Know?

Fine motor control is linked to cognitive development — when children manipulate small

objects, they also develop problem-solving and concentration skills.

Tip

Fine motor skills are important for writing, drawing, and dressing. In tests, you may need to

provide examples of activities that improve them, such as threading, cutting, or building

blocks.

2.6 Perceptual Abilities

Perceptual skills involve using the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell) to understand

and respond to the environment. They form the link between sensory input and learning.

Visual Perception

Visual perception helps children recognise shapes, letters, and patterns. It is crucial for reading

and mathematics.

Activities:

● Matching pictures and shadows.

● Sorting objects by colour or size.

● Completing puzzles and mazes.

29

Definition

Visual perception: The ability to interpret and understand visual information.

Auditory Perception

Auditory perception allows children to recognise and distinguish sounds — essential for

language and reading skills.

Activities:

● Identifying rhyming words.

● Listening for specific sounds in stories.

● Clapping rhythms or repeating patterns.

Did You Know?

Children with weak auditory perception often struggle with spelling and phonics later in

school.

Tactile, Gustatory, and Olfactory Perception

These senses — touch, taste, and smell — help children explore and understand their

surroundings.

Activities:

● Touch-and-feel boxes for texture recognition.

● Smell games using fruit or spices.

● Tasting safe food samples for comparison (sweet, sour, salty).

30

Tip

Understand that perception means using the senses to learn. When revising, pair each type of

perception (visual, auditory, tactile, gustatory, olfactory) with one classroom activity. This helps

in short-answer questions and matching exercises.

2.7 Language Development, Concept Formation, and Expanding Knowledge

Language is at the heart of learning. Through language, children express ideas, understand

instructions, and build relationships.

Key Areas:

● Passive Language Development – Listening and understanding.

● Active Language Development – Speaking and expressing ideas.

Examples:

● Storytelling and singing songs.

● Asking questions about a story’s characters.

● Repeating rhymes and naming objects in pictures.

Definition

Concept formation: The process of understanding and categorising ideas, such as colours,

sizes, or emotions.

31

Did You Know?

Language development supports emotional growth — children who can express feelings with

words are less likely to show frustration through behaviour.

Tip

Know the difference between passive and active language development. This is a frequent

exam question.

● Passive = listening and understanding.

● Active = speaking and expressing ideas.

32

Mathematical readiness starts in early play — children who stack, compare, and count objects

Tip

Revise early mathematical concepts like counting, size, quantity, and patterns. In

multiple-choice questions, you may be asked to choose an example that shows one of these

concepts in action (e.g., “Which activity teaches sequence?”).
