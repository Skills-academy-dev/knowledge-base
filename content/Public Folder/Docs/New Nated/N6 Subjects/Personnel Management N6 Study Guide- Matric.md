Introduction
Motivation is central to personnel management. It influences how employees behave, perform
and commit to their work. When employees are motivated, they work willingly and contribute
positively to organisational goals.
Refer to page 9 of the textbook.
Unit 1: Uderstanding Employee Motivation
This unit explains the meaning of motivation, how the motivation cycle works, and why
motivated employees are important for organisational success.
1.1 Meaning and Purpose of Motivation
Motivation refers to the internal and external forces that influence employee behaviour. It helps
explain why people act as they do and what drives them to maintain certain behaviours.
Motivation is important because it encourages effort, supports goal achievement, and reduces
absenteeism and conflict.
1.2 The Motivation Cycle
The motivation cycle describes how employees move from recognising a need to taking action
to satisfy it. The main stages are:
1. Need – A desire or lack is identified.
2. Tension – The unmet need creates pressure.
3. Action – Steps are taken to satisfy the need.
4. Goal Achievement – The need is met.
5. Feedback – The employee evaluates the result.
The cycle continues as new needs appear.
1.3 Benefits of Motivation
Motivation improves productivity, increases job satisfaction, strengthens commitment and
reduces workplace problems such as absenteeism. Motivated employees generally perform
better and contribute to organisational effectiveness.
Refer to page 10–12 of the textbook for detailed information.

Unit 2: Major Motivation Models
This unit summarises the four major motivation models presented in the textbook. These
theories explain what drives employees, how they respond to rewards, and how fairness and
expectations influence work behaviour.
2.1 Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs
Maslow explains that employees are motivated by a hierarchy of needs arranged from basic to
advanced. These needs develop in stages:
1. Physiological needs – wages for food, rest breaks, comfortable environment.
2. Safety needs – job security, safe conditions, fair policies.
3. Social needs – teamwork, acceptance, positive interactions.
4. Esteem needs – recognition, respect, responsibility.
5. Self-actualisation – creativity, challenging tasks, leadership opportunities.
Employees must first satisfy lower-level needs before higher-level needs can motivate them.
2.2 Herzberg’s Motivation–Hygiene Theory
Herzberg identifies two sets of factors affecting motivation:
Hygiene Factors (Prevent dissatisfaction)
● Salary
● Company policies
● Working conditions
● Supervision
● Job security
● Workplace relationships
These factors do not motivate, but poor hygiene factors cause dissatisfaction.
Motivators (Increase satisfaction)
● Achievement
● Recognition
● Responsibility
13

● Advancement
● Meaningful work
These factors relate to the job itself and encourage long-term motivation.
2.3 Vroom’s Expectancy Theory
Vroom states that motivation depends on three beliefs:
1. Expectancy – effort will lead to good performance.
2. Instrumentality – good performance will lead to rewards.
3. Valence – the reward is valuable to the employee.
Employees put in more effort when all three expectations are met.
2.4 Adams’ Equity Theory
Adams explains that employees compare their:
● Inputs (effort, skills, experience)
● Outputs (pay, recognition, benefits)
If they see fairness in the comparison, they stay motivated. If they feel unfairly treated, they may
reduce effort or become dissatisfied.
Refer to pages 13–19 of the textbook for full detailed information.

Introduction
Communication allows information to flow clearly between managers, employees and teams,
helping the organisation function effectively. This module explains what communication is, how
the process works, and the roles of senders and receivers. It highlights the importance of clear
communication for productivity, the barriers that can interrupt messages, and how structure,
communication flow and personal differences affect understanding in the workplace.
Refer to pages 32 of the textbook for detailed infromation.
Unit 1: Key Terms and Foundational Concepts
Unit 1 introduces the basic meaning of communication, the steps involved in the communication
process, and the responsibilities of both the sender and receiver. It also explains why
communication is essential in workplace operations.
1.1 Definitions Related to Communication
Communication is described as the process of sending and receiving messages to create
understanding between people. It includes sharing information, ideas, instructions, emotions
and opinions.
Key points from the textbook:
● Communication is a two-way process.
● The aim is to create shared understanding.
● Communication can be verbal (spoken/written) or non-verbal (body language, facial
expressions).
● It always requires a sender and a receiver.
1.2 Components of the Communication Process
The communication process is made up of several steps that help ensure clarity and

1.3 Sender–Receiver Responsibilities
Effective communication requires both parties to play an active role.
Responsibilities of the Sender
● Use clear, simple language
● Choose an appropriate channel
● Ensure the message is complete and accurate
● Check understanding by requesting feedback
Responsibilities of the Receiver
● Listen or read carefully
● Ask questions when unclear
● Avoid assumptions
● Provide honest, timely feedback
1.4 Purpose of Communication in the Workplace
Communication helps organisations run smoothly by ensuring information flows correctly and
tasks are understood. According to the textbook, workplace communication is important
because:
● Employees understand what is expected of them
● Managers can give clear instructions, feedback and guidance
● It prevents misunderstandings and reduces mistakes
● It strengthens teamwork by enabling employees to share ideas and information
Refer to page 33–36 of the textbook for detailed information.
Unit 2: Essentials of Effective Communication
Unit 2 explains what makes communication effective in the workplace. It expands on the basics
covered in Unit 1 by showing why communication sometimes fails, why effective communication
is important, and how organisations can overcome communication barriers.
22

2.1 Importance of Clear and Accurate Communication
Clear communication helps organisations function smoothly by ensuring messages are
understood in the way the sender intends. The textbook emphasises that effective
communication:
● Improves teamwork
● Reduces misunderstandings
● Supports decision-making
● Prevents unnecessary conflict
● Ensures employees understand instructions and expectations
2.2 Barriers That Interrupt Communication
The textbook identifies barriers that prevent messages from being understood correctly. These
include:
Physical Barriers
● Noise, distance, interruptions
● Poor network connection
Language Barriers
● Misinterpretation of words
● Use of jargon or complex terms
Emotional or Psychological Barriers
● Stress, anger, fear
● Low confidence or lack of trust
Cultural Barriers
● Differences in norms, values and communication styles
Perceptual Barriers
● Misreading tone, intention or attitude
These barriers make communication unclear and increase mistakes and conflict.
23

2.3 Techniques to Improve Communication Effectiveness
According to the textbook, organisations can strengthen communication by applying the
following techniques:
1. Use Simple and Clear Language
Speakers should use words that employees understand.
2. Listen Actively
Good communication requires paying attention, asking questions and showing understanding.
3. Encourage Feedback
Feedback helps confirm whether the message was understood correctly.
4. Choose the Right Channel
Complex messages may require face-to-face communication instead of email.
5. Reduce Noise and Distractions
Quiet environments reduce confusion and misunderstanding.
6. Be Aware of Non-Verbal Communication
Body language, facial expressions and tone send strong signals.
2.4 How Organisational Structure Affects Communication
● Communication moves differently depending on the hierarchy.
● Downward: From managers to employees for instructions and feedback.
● Upward: From employees to managers for progress reports and suggestions.
● Lateral: Between employees at the same level for coordination and teamwork.
2.5 Formal and Informal Communication Channels
● Communication can follow official or unofficial paths.
● Formal channels: Memos, reports, notices and official meetings.
● Informal channels: Casual conversations, social chats and quick messages among
co-workers.
Refer to pages 37–42 of the textbook for detailed information.

Unit 3: Communication Systems and Patterns
Unit 3 describes how communication moves within an organisation. It explains how
organisational structure affects communication, the different flow patterns of communication,
and the formal and informal networks through which messages travel.
3.1 How Organisational Structure Affects Communication
Organisational structure influences the speed and clarity of communication. Structures with
many levels communicate more slowly, while flatter structures communicate more quickly.
How Structure Shapes Communication
● Tall structures: Many levels → slower communication.
● Flat structures: Few levels → faster communication and direct contact.
● Centralised structures: Decisions made at the top → mostly downward
communication.
● Decentralised structures: Employees participate more → more upward and lateral
communication.

3.4 Informal Networks
Informal networks develop naturally based on personal relationships and shared interests. They
are not controlled by management. They can spread information quickly but may create
misunderstandings if inaccurate.
Common Types of Informal Networks
●  Single-Strand Network: Information passes from one person to the next in a straight
line.
Example: A tells B, who tells C.

● Gossip Chain: One person shares information with several others.
Example: An employee shares a rumour with everyone.
● Probability Chain: Information is passed randomly without order.
Example: People share information with whoever they meet.
● Cluster Chain: Individuals share information only with selected people they trust.
Example: Two close colleagues share news with a few trusted co-workers.

Unit 4: Personal Factors Influencing Communication
Unit 4 explains how individual differences influence workplace communication. These personal
factors affect how messages are sent, interpreted and responded to, which may improve or
hinder effective communication.
4.1 Gender, Cultural and Age Differences
Personal background strongly influences how people communicate. The textbook highlights
three key factors:
Gender Differences
Men and women may communicate differently because of socialisation and communication
styles.
Examples from the textbook include:
● Men often communicate to show status, give information or assert position.
● Women often communicate to build connection, express emotions or create

Cultural Differences
People from different cultural backgrounds may interpret messages differently.
The textbook highlights:
● Words may carry different meanings across cultures.
● Tone and volume differ across groups (e.g., loud vs soft speaking).
27

● Non-verbal cues such as eye contact, gestures and facial expressions may be
interpreted differently.
● Sarcasm or humour may be misunderstood across cultures.
Age Differences
Age groups often use and interpret language differently. Examples include:
● Younger and older employees may attach different meanings to the same words.
● Younger employees may prioritise social interaction; older employees may prioritise
work structure and routine.
● Differing values and interpretations may cause miscommunication between supervisors
and subordinates.
