and global markets.

real-life South African examples, and practical guidance that reflect modern marketing practices

compete, grow, and create value for customers while achieving their goals.

market share, and profitability.
● Building professional skills: Develop skills needed to function effectively in
marketing-related roles.
In short, studying Marketing Management helps you become a confident decision-maker who
understands both organisational objectives and customer expectations.

Introduction
Marketing plays a vital role in the success of organisations operating in competitive and
dynamic business environments. As a management function, marketing involves the planning,
organisation, leadership, co-ordination, and control of activities aimed at satisfying customer
needs while achieving organisational goals. This module introduces marketing management as
an essential part of overall business management and explains how marketing contributes to
growth, profitability, and long-term sustainability.
The module also focuses on the strategic marketing process and the responsibilities of the
marketing manager. You will learn how organisations analyse their internal and external
marketing environments, set strategic direction through mission and goal formulation, and
develop suitable marketing strategies. The implementation, control, evaluation, and revision of
marketing plans are also covered, enabling you to apply marketing management principles
effectively in real business situations and in preparation for the Marketing Management N6
examination.
Unit 1: Marketing as a Management Function
Marketing is more than advertising and selling. It is a core management function that helps
organisations understand customer needs, plan appropriate responses, and achieve business
objectives. In this unit, you will learn what marketing as a management function involves, how
marketing fits into the organisation, how the marketing department is structured, and the key
management tasks performed by marketing managers.
Meaning of Marketing as a Management Function
Marketing as a management function refers to the process through which an organisation plans,
organises, leads, co-ordinates, and controls its marketing activities to satisfy customer needs
profitably and achieve organisational goals. It ensures that marketing decisions are not made
randomly, but are guided by clear objectives and informed analysis.
Marketing management focuses on:
● Identifying customer needs and wants through market research
● Planning marketing strategies to meet these needs
9

● Managing the marketing mix (product, price, place and promotion)
● Monitoring marketing performance and taking corrective action
Quick Definition
Marketing management: The planning, organising, leading and controlling of marketing
activities to achieve organisational objectives by satisfying customer needs.
South African Example
A retailer such as Checkers uses marketing management to decide which products to stock,
how to price them competitively, and how to promote special offers to attract customers.

1.1 The Position of Marketing in the Enterprise
Marketing occupies a central position in the organisation because it links the business with its
customers and the market environment. All other departments depend on marketing information
to make informed decisions.
The Role of Marketing in the Organisation
Marketing plays an important role by:
●  Identifying customer needs and expectations
●  Developing products and services that meet these needs
●  Communicating value through promotion and branding
●  Building long-term customer relationships
Marketing directly influences sales levels, profitability, market share, and organisational growth.

Companies such as Coca-Cola use marketing research and branding to maintain strong

Relationship Between Marketing and Other Organisational Functions
Marketing does not operate in isolation. It works closely with other functional areas to ensure
organisational success.
● Production: Ensures products meet customer requirements
● Finance: Assists with pricing decisions and budgeting
● Human Resources: Recruits and trains marketing staff

1.2 The Organisation of the Marketing Department
The marketing department can be organised in different ways, depending on the size of the
organisation, the range of products, and the markets served.
Departmentalisation According to Function
Marketing activities are grouped according to specific functions such as advertising, sales, and
market research. This method allows employees to specialise in their areas of expertise.
Departmentalisation According to Product / Service
Each product or service has its own marketing team responsible for planning, implementation,
and performance.
Example
A food manufacturer may have separate marketing teams for snacks and beverages.
Departmentalisation According to Geographical Area
Marketing activities are organised according to regions or areas. This allows marketing
strategies to be adapted to local customer needs.
Example
A national retailer may have different marketing strategies for Gauteng and the Western Cape.
Departmentalisation According to Client
Marketing activities are organised according to different customer groups, such as individual
consumers and business clients.

Unit 2: The Strategic Marketing Process
In a competitive and changing business environment, organisations cannot rely on short-term
decisions alone. They must plan their marketing activities carefully to achieve long-term
success. The strategic marketing process provides a structured approach that helps
organisations analyse their situation, set clear direction, choose suitable strategies, and manage
marketing activities effectively. This unit explains each stage of the strategic marketing process
and shows how it is applied in practice.
Nature of the Strategic Marketing Process
The strategic marketing process is a logical and continuous process used by organisations to
plan, implement, and control their marketing activities over the long term. It ensures that
marketing decisions are based on research and analysis rather than guesswork.
The strategic marketing process involves the following main stages:
● Analysing the marketing situation
● Determining strategic direction
● Developing marketing strategies
● Drafting action plans
● Implementing strategies
● Controlling, evaluating, and revising strategies
Strategic marketing focuses on long-term organisational goals, such as growth, market share,
and competitive advantage, rather than only short-term sales.
Quick Definition
Strategic marketing process: A systematic process used to analyse the market
environment, set marketing objectives, develop strategies, implement plans, and evaluate
performance.
17

Extent and Importance of Planning in Strategic Marketing Management
Planning is the foundation of strategic marketing management. Without proper planning,
marketing activities may be uncoordinated, ineffective, and costly. Strategic planning helps
organisations anticipate changes in the market and respond proactively.
Strategic marketing planning enables organisations to:
● Identify opportunities and threats in the environment
● Reduce uncertainty and risk
● Use resources efficiently
● Gain and maintain a competitive advantage
● Improve long-term decision-making
South African Example
A retail chain such as Mr Price plans its marketing strategies well in advance by analysing
economic conditions, customer spending patterns, and competitor behaviour.
Tip
When asked about the importance of planning, always link it to risk reduction, better
decision-making, and competitive advantage.
2.1 The Importance of Strategic Marketing Management
Strategic marketing management ensures that marketing activities support the organisation’s
overall mission and long-term objectives. It provides direction and consistency in marketing
decision-making.
18

Marketing Planning as Part of Strategic Organisational Planning
Marketing planning does not take place in isolation. It forms part of the organisation’s overall
strategic planning process. Marketing objectives must support organisational goals such as
growth, profitability, sustainability, and customer satisfaction.
Example
If an organisation’s strategic goal is to expand into new markets, the marketing plan may focus
on market development and promotional strategies.
Designing and Planning the Strategic Marketing Process
Designing the strategic marketing process involves careful analysis and planning. Marketing
managers must ensure that strategies are realistic and aligned with available resources.
Key steps include:
● Conducting a situation analysis
● Setting clear marketing objectives
● Developing alternative marketing strategies
● Selecting the most suitable strategy
Good planning helps avoid poor decisions and wasted resources.
Comparing and Evaluating Marketing Plans and Strategies
Before selecting a marketing strategy, managers compare different options. Each strategy is
evaluated based on:
● Costs and benefits
● Risk involved
● Available resources
● Market conditions
The strategy that best meets organisational objectives and market requirements is selected.

2.2 Analysis of the Marketing Situation
Situation analysis helps organisations understand where they currently stand in the market. It
involves analysing both internal and external factors that affect marketing performance.
A. The Internal Environment
The internal environment consists of factors within the organisation that can be controlled by
management.
Strong Points
Strong points are internal strengths that give the organisation an advantage, such as:
● Skilled and experienced staff
● Strong brand reputation
● Efficient distribution systems
● Financial stability
Weak Points
Weak points are internal limitations that may hinder performance, such as:
● Poor customer service
● Limited resources
● Weak brand image
● Outdated technology
Example
A strong brand image may help an organisation attract customers more easily than competitors.
B. The External Environment
The external environment includes factors outside the organisation’s control that influence
marketing decisions.
20

Opportunities
Opportunities are favourable external factors, such as:
● Growing markets
● New technologies
● Changes in consumer lifestyles
Limitations
Limitations refer to threats or challenges, such as:
● Increased competition
● Economic downturns
● Government regulations
Quick Definition
SWOT analysis: A method used to identify an organisation’s strengths, weaknesses,
opportunities, and threats.

2.3 Specification of Strategic Direction
Once the situation analysis is completed, the organisation must decide on its future direction.
This provides guidance for all marketing decisions.
Mission
The mission statement explains the organisation’s purpose and what it aims to achieve. It
guides decision-making and provides focus. A well-defined mission:
● Provides long-term direction
● Helps align marketing objectives
● Guides employee behaviour
Example
A mission may emphasise quality, customer satisfaction, or innovation.
Goals
Goals are specific and measurable outcomes that the organisation wants to achieve within a
certain period. Goals translate the mission into specific outcomes. Effective goals should be:
● Clear
● Measurable
● Time-bound
Example
To increase market share by 5% within one year.
Target Market
Selecting a target market allows organisations to focus their marketing efforts. Trying to satisfy
all customers often leads to inefficiency and poor results.
22

Target marketing helps organisations to:
● Design suitable products
● Set appropriate prices
● Select effective promotional methods
Quick Definition
Target market: A specific group of customers chosen as the focus of marketing activities.
2.4 Consider, Choose and Develop Alternative Marketing Strategies
Marketing strategies determine how the organisation will compete in the market and achieve its
goals.
Models for Planning a Product Portfolio
Portfolio models help marketing managers evaluate products and markets.
● Market growth / market share matrix
● Market attractiveness / organisational strength model
These models assist in deciding which products to develop, maintain, or withdraw.
Competitive Marketing Strategies
Competitive strategies help organisations gain an advantage over competitors.
● Differentiation: Offering unique products or services
● Low-cost strategy: Offering products at lower prices
● Focus strategy: Targeting a specific market segment
Example
A budget airline uses a low-cost strategy to attract price-sensitive customers.
23

Growth Strategies
Growth strategies focus on expanding the organisation.
● Market penetration
● Product development
● Market development
● Integration
● Diversification
Each strategy involves different levels of risk and investment.
Strategies for Mature and Declining Markets
When markets mature or decline, organisations may:
● Retain existing customers
● Win customers from competitors
● Withdraw from unprofitable markets
2.5 Drafting of a Plan of Action
Once strategies are chosen, they are converted into detailed action plans. These plans specify:
● Marketing activities
● Time frames
● Budgets
● Responsible persons
Action plans ensure strategies are implemented effectively.
2.6 Implementation
Implementation is often the most challenging stage of the strategic marketing process. Even
good strategies can fail if poorly implemented.

Responsibility of Marketing Management
Marketing managers are responsible for:
● Communicating plans to staff

● Monitoring progress
Role of Leadership and Management in Execution
Effective leadership motivates employees and ensures commitment to marketing objectives.
Example
Clear communication and feedback help staff understand their roles.
2.7 Control
Marketing control ensures that objectives are achieved. It allows managers to identify problems
early and take corrective action.
Steps in the Control Process
1. Setting performance standards
2. Measuring actual performance
3. Taking corrective action
Control Methods / Techniques
Marketing managers use:
● Marketing audits
● Sales analysis
● Marketing cost analysis
● Effectiveness analysis
● Observation

Unit 3: Application
Marketing management theory is only valuable when it can be applied in real business
situations. This unit focuses on applying the strategic marketing process by compiling a simple
strategic marketing plan for an organisation. It brings together the concepts covered in Units 1
and 2 and helps you develop practical skills that are essential for both the N6 examination and
the workplace.
Purpose of a Strategic Marketing Plan
A strategic marketing plan is a written document that outlines how an organisation will achieve
its marketing objectives over a specific period. It provides direction and ensures that marketing
activities are coordinated and aligned with organisational goals.
The purpose of a strategic marketing plan is to:
● Provide clear marketing objectives and strategies
● Guide decision-making and resource allocation
● Identify target markets and competitive strategies
● Measure and evaluate marketing performance
26

Quick Definition
Strategic marketing plan: A structured document that outlines an organisation’s marketing
objectives, strategies, and actions to achieve long-term success.
Key Components of a Simple Strategic Marketing Plan
For Marketing Management N6, learners are expected to understand and apply the basic
components of a strategic marketing plan rather than produce a complex business plan.
A simple strategic marketing plan typically includes the following sections:
1. Organisational Overview
This section provides background information about the organisation.
It includes:
● Name of the organisation
● Nature of the business
● Products or services offered
● Industry in which the organisation operates
Example
A small South African catering business operating in Gauteng that provides services for
corporate events.
2. Situation Analysis
Situation analysis forms the foundation of the marketing plan. It helps the organisation
understand its current position in the market.
27

Internal Analysis
● Strengths (e.g. skilled staff, strong reputation)
● Weaknesses (e.g. limited budget, small market share)
External Analysis
● Opportunities (e.g. growing demand, new markets)
● Threats (e.g. competition, rising costs)
3. Strategic Direction
This section explains where the organisation is heading.
● Mission
The organisation’s purpose and reason for existence.
● Marketing Goals
Clear and measurable marketing objectives.
Example
To increase sales by 10% within the next year.
● Target Market
The specific group of customers the organisation aims to serve.
4. Marketing Strategies
Marketing strategies explain how the organisation will achieve its objectives.
Strategies may include:
● Competitive strategies (e.g. differentiation or low-cost)
● Growth strategies (e.g. market penetration or product development)
28

Example
A coffee shop may use differentiation by offering premium-quality products and excellent
customer service.
5. Marketing Mix
The marketing mix explains how strategies will be implemented in practice.
● Product
What is offered to satisfy customer needs.
● Price
How products or services are priced competitively.
● Place (Distribution)
How products or services reach customers.
● Promotion
How customers are informed and persuaded to buy.
Tip
Always explain the marketing mix in relation to the chosen target market.
6. Action Plan
The action plan converts strategies into practical steps.
It specifies:
● Marketing activities
● Time frames
● Responsible persons
Example
Launching a social media promotion over three months to increase brand awareness.
29

7. Control and Evaluation
This section explains how marketing performance will be monitored.
Control methods may include:
● Sales analysis
● Customer feedback
● Marketing audits
Evaluation ensures that corrective action can be taken if objectives are not achieved.
