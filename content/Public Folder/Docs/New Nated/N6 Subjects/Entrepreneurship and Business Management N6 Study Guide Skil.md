Introduction to the Entrepreneurship and Business

Management N6 Study Guide

Welcome to Your Entrepreneurship and Business Management N6 Study Guide!

This study guide has been developed to support you as a distance learning student,

making it easier to understand your textbook and prepare for your assessments — even if

you are studying from home.

Studying Entrepreneurship and Business Management N6 gives you the knowledge and

skills needed to start, manage, and grow a successful business. The subject explores

important topics such as strategic planning, competitive analysis, change management,

business expansion, and business plan development. These concepts are essential for

anyone who wants to build a strong career in business, leadership, or entrepreneurship.

This guide is your companion as you progress through the course. It is designed to:

●  Explain key content from your textbook in a clear and structured format.

●  Provide concise summaries of each module, unit, and sub-topic.

●  Help you revise and prepare for tests, assignments, and final assessments.

●  Build your confidence in applying business and management principles to real-world

situations.

Each module follows the structure of your prescribed textbook and breaks the content into

manageable sections. The summaries focus on the most important ideas, using simple

academic language to support your independent learning.

As a distance learner, you may not attend traditional classes, but this guide will help you

stay organised and understand what is expected in each part of the course. Use it to review

your work, test your understanding, and identify areas that require additional study.

Remember: You are not alone on this journey. Stay focused, manage your study time

effectively, and reach out to your tutor or academic support team if you need help. With

commitment and consistent effort, you can succeed in Entrepreneurship and Business

Management N6 and apply your knowledge to create or manage successful business

ventures.

Let’s get started and explore the exciting world of entrepreneurship, innovation, and

business leadership — at your own pace!

Overview of the Entrepreneurship and Business

Management N6 Exam Papers

Entrepreneurship and Business Management N6 is assessed through one written paper

that tests both theoretical understanding and the application of business principles to

practical entrepreneurial situations.

The exam focuses on your ability to think critically, plan strategically, and apply management

and entrepreneurial concepts to real-world contexts.

EXAM PAPER

Duration: 3 hours

Total Marks: 200

Structure:

Section A: Short and Objective Questions (40 marks)

This section tests your understanding of key definitions, concepts, and basic theory from all

six modules.

All questions in this section are compulsory.

Section B: Long and Structured Questions (160 marks)

This section assesses your ability to apply theory, analyse business situations, and propose

solutions.

You will be required to answer four (4) out of six (6) long questions. Each question carries

40 marks.

Key Focus Areas

The exam covers the entire N6 syllabus, including:

●  Strategic Planning and Decision Making

(The planning process, decision-making techniques, monitoring and evaluation.)

●  Business Environment and Change Management

(Internal and external environmental factors, managing resistance, and adapting to

change.)

●  Strategy Implementation and Competitive Analysis

(SWOT analysis, Porter’s Five Forces, key success factors, and benchmarking.)

●  Strategy, Competitive Advantage and Protection

(Generic strategies, offensive and defensive strategies, sustaining advantage, and

intellectual property protection.)

1.1 What is strategic planning?

●  A structured, ongoing process that aligns purpose, goals, and actions.

●  Turns ideas into measurable, repeatable results.

●  Keeps a business adaptable when customers or costs change.

●

Involves setting priorities, allocating resources, and reviewing progress.

●  Works best when it’s evidence-based, participatory, and time-bound.

1.2 Why entrepreneurs need it

●  Focus: It limits distractions so scarce time and cash go to the best opportunities.

●  Fit: It aligns product, pricing, place, and promotion with the target customer.

●  Confidence: It gives a reasoned basis for decisions (useful with partners and

lenders).

●  Learning: It builds a habit of testing, measuring, and improving.

●  Resilience: It prepares you for shocks (e.g., input price spikes, load-shedding, new

entrants).

1.3  Importance of planning in uncertain business environments

There are three major levels of planning:

●  Uncertainty (e.g. power cuts, inflation, competition) increases risk and cost.

●  Planning allows early detection of problems and faster, calmer responses.

●  Tools: PESTEL scans external trends; scenario strips plan best/base/worst cases.

●  Small firms use trigger points (“if sales fall below target → act”) and contingency

budgets.

●  Effective plans are flexible, simple, and regularly reviewed.

1.4 Relationship between entrepreneurial thinking and strategic management

●  Entrepreneurial thinking discovers opportunities; strategic management allocates

resources to act on them.

●  Together they form a cycle: discover → decide → deliver → learn.

●  Both require customer focus, experimentation, and resilience.

●  Key questions: Where will we compete? How will we win? What must we do now?

●  Using models like Ansoff and BCG, entrepreneurs turn creative ideas into structured,

results-driven strategies.

Unit 2 – The Strategic Planning Process

In Unit 1 you met the “why” of strategic planning. In Unit 2, we get into the “how”—the

practical, repeatable steps managers use to turn vision into action. Think of this as your

business GPS: set the destination, scan the route, choose the lane, then drive and

course-correct.

2.1 The Strategic Planning Process (9 Steps)

●  Confirm the vision (future goal) and mission (purpose).

●  Scan the external environment with PESTLE and analyse the internal situation using

SWOT.

●  Set SMART objectives that are realistic and measurable.

●  Select suitable strategies and convert them into action plans, budgets, and timelines.

●  Monitor and evaluatePERFORMANCE

2.2 The Continuous Cycle: Plan → Implement → Evaluate → Improve

Strategic planning is ongoing:

●  Plan: For

●

Implement: Carry out tasks and allocate resources.

●  Evaluate: Measure results and identify lessons learned.

●

Improve: Adjust actions and budgets for better performance.

2.3 Decision-Making Tools to Support the Cycle

Managers use simple tools to make informed choices:

●  Cost–Benefit Analysis: Compare total costs and benefits before deciding.

●  Risk Matrix: Rank threats by likelihood × impact to prioritise responses.

●  Decision Matrix: Weigh non-financial criteria such as brand control or skills fit.

2.4 Choosing Growth Paths with the Ansoff Matrix

The Ansoff Matrix guides expansion:

●  Market Penetration: Sell more to current customers.

●  Market Development: Enter new markets with existing products.

●  Product Development: Create new products for existing customers.

●  Diversify Launch new products in new markets (highest risk).

2.5 Prioritising Offerings with the BCG Matrix

Used to decide where to invest:

●  Stars: High growth & share – invest more.

●  Cash Cows: Low growth & high share – maintain and fund others.

●  Question Marks: High growth & low share – test potential.

●  Dogs: Low growth & low share – consider withdrawal.

2.6 Step 5 – Evaluation and Control

Strategic decisions use evidence and structure:

●  CBA for financial comparison.

●  Risk Matrix for identifying urgent threats.

●  Decision Matrix for balancing qualitative factors.

These tools help ensure decisions are logical, transparent, and aligned with

objectives.

Unit 3 – Decision-Making Process and Techniques

Successful strategy depends on effective decision-making. This unit introduces an eight-step

process and practical techniques to help entrepreneurs move from identifying a problem to

taking confident, well-informed action.

3.1 The Eight-Step Decision Process

●  Define the problem: Identify the gap without jumping to solutions.

●  Collect information: Base decisions on facts, data, and relevant evidence.

●

Identify alternatives: Generate multiple realistic options for action.

●  Evaluate and select: Use cost, benefit, and risk to compare options objectively.

●

Implement, monitor, and review: Assign responsibility, track KPIs, and record lessons

learned.

3.2 Fast Techniques (to improve Steps 3–5)

The process usually includes:

1.  Brainstorming: Generate ideas freely before judging them.

2.  Five Whys: Ask “why?” repeatedly to find root causes.

3.  Suspend Judgment: Separate idea creation from evaluation to avoid bias.

4.  Fractioning: Break complex problems into smaller, easier decisions.

5.  Reverse Brainstorming: Imagine worsening the problem, then reverse the ideas

into practical solutions.

Unit 4 – Developing Alternatives and Choosing Strategy

A good strategy is not a guess — it’s a deliberate choice between real options. This unit

teaches how to create, evaluate, and select the best strategy using creative methods, logical

screening, and financial tools that ensure every decision supports the mission and

objectives.

Part A – Generating Alternatives

●  Use creative thinking to produce many possible strategies before judging them.

●  Brainstorming: Write down every idea, then vote on the best few.

●  Five Whys: Ask “why?” repeatedly to find root causes.

●  Reverse Brainstorming: Imagine making the issue worse, then flip ideas into

solutions.

●  Fractioning: Break complex choices into smaller, safer decisions.

Part B – Selecting the Best Strategy (Four Steps)

Step 1: Alignment Gate

●  Keep only options that support your mission, vision, and SMART objectives.

●  Remove any ideas that do not fit your purpose or brand direction.

Step 2: F-A-S Screening

●  Feasibility: Can we afford and execute it?

●  Acceptability: Are risks and returns suitable for stakeholders?

●  Suitability: Does it match our environment and positioning?

●

Include an Ethics & Compliance check for legality and reputation.

Step 3: Quantitative Checks

●  Apply simple financial tests such as payback, break-even, ROI, or NPV.

●  These confirm whether each option is profitable and realistic.

Payback (months)

Use for short-horizon, cash-sensitive choices.

18

Break-Even Quantity (units)

Use for unit-based offerings.

ROI (simple)

Spend R12 000, gain R18 000 over the year → ROI = (18 000−12 000)/12 000 = 50%.

NPV (multi-year cash flows)

Use a sensible discount rrr (e.g., 10–12%) for SME risk.

Cash flows 6k, 6k, 6k; r=10%; NPV ≈ 6 000/1.1 + 6 000/1.1² + 6 000/1.1³ − C₀.

Step 4: Decision Matrix

●  Choose 4–6 weighted criteria (e.g., payback ↓, risk ↓, brand ↑, skills ↑).

●  Score each option 1–5 and multiply by weight to compare totals.

●  Record reasons for your final choice to ensure a clear audit trail.

Unit 5 – Monitoring and Evaluation of Strategy

A strategy that isn’t measured is just a wish list. This unit explains how to use KPIs and

feedback loops to track performance, identify problems early, and take corrective action. The

focus is on continuous improvement: plan → act → measure → learn → adjust → repeat.

5.1 Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

●  Track 3–5 KPIs per objective (mix of leading and lagging indicators).

●

Include baseline, target, and thresholds (green/amber/red).

●  Common KPIs: sales growth, profit margin, NPS, on-time delivery, repeat purchases.

●  Each KPI must have a data source, owner, and review schedule.

Balanced Scorecard (BSC)

●  Measures performance across four areas:

Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth.

●  Keeps goals balanced between profit, service, efficiency, and employee

development.

Feedback Loops and Reviews

●  Weekly: Check leading KPIs and fix quick issues.

●  Monthly: Analyse lagging KPIs and lessons learned.

●  Quarterly: Refresh SWOT/PESTLE, update targets, rebalance budgets.

●  Take action to correct variances and sustain performance.

Micro-Case: KasiBakery

●  Raised margin from 31% → 35% using KPI tracking.

●  Adjusted pricing, reduced waste, and monitored customer complaints.

●  Proved that small, data-driven changes can boost profit and service.

Unit 1 – Growth of Entrepreneurship and the Role of Small

Business

Entrepreneurship grows where challenges meet opportunity. In South Africa, unemployment,

Economic, Social & Demographic Forces

●  Economic: Job scarcity and inflation drive side-hustles; mobile payments improve

●  Social: Community trust and networks replace big marketing budgets.

●  Demographic: Youthful, urban populations create new markets and talent.

1.1 Why Small Businesses Matter

●  Create jobs and local income.

●  Keep money circulating in communities.

●  Adapt quickly to customer needs.

●

Innovate faster than big firms.

●  Strengthen resilience after setbacks.

1.2 Enablers vs Barriers

●  Enablers: Mobile payments, online markets, local networks.

●  Barriers: Power cuts, high rent, limited skills, red tape.

●  Smart moves: shared inverters, pop-ups, peer mentoring, compliance checklists.

●  Use the SIR method – Signal → Implication → Response – to act on local changes.

Unit 2 – Entrepreneurial Profiles

Entrepreneurs come in many forms. Knowing your profile helps you set goals, manage risks,

and choose the right support for your business.

Women Entrepreneurs

●  Build strong community brands while balancing home duties.

●  Strengths: trust, authenticity, quick product testing.

●  Barriers: finance, safety, limited networks.

●  Supports: micro-finance, safe venues, flexible hours.

Home-Based Businesses

●  Run from home (baking, sewing, tutoring).

●  Strengths: low cost, local trust.

●  Barriers: space limits, power issues, blurred roles.

●  Supports: pop-ups, shared kitchens, compliance checks.

Part-Time (Side-Hustle) Founders

●  Work or study while trading part-time.

●  Strengths: income security, low risk.

●  Barriers: limited time, burnout.

●  Supports: focus on one product, automation, set service limits.

Family Businesses

●  Owned and managed by relatives.

●  Strengths: trust, shared effort, long-term focus.

●  Barriers: conflict, unclear roles.

●  Supports: family charter, clear systems, outside advice.

Intrapreneurs

●  Employees innovating within a company.

3.1 Grow Now or Stabilise First?

●  Green lights: Strong demand, stable quality, repeat customers, and healthy margins.

●  Red lights: Cash-flow strain, weak processes, burnout, or unmanaged risks.

●  Growth amplifies strengths and weaknesses — fix quality or cash issues first.

●  Test readiness across five areas: quality, capacity, cash, risk, and repeatability.

●  Only scale when systems and people can sustain higher volume.

3.2 Pilot → Scale (Avoid Costly Mistakes)

●  Start small with a 4–6 week pilot at one site or channel.

●  Set measurable targets (e.g., OTD ≥ 92%, margin ≥ 35%).

●  Review progress weekly using PDCA: plan, do, check, act.

●  Document systems and training before expanding.

●  SA tips: plan for load-shedding, use digital tools, and ensure legal compliance.

4.1 PESTEL in Plain Language

●  Political: By-laws, permits, trade rules (e.g., new food-safety checks).

●  Economic: Inflation, fuel, jobs, prices affecting margins and demand.

●  Social: Demographics, culture, safety, lifestyle trends.

●  Technological: Payments, apps, AI tools, delivery platforms.

●  Environmental & Legal: Power, water, waste, labour law, POPIA compliance.

4.1.1 Identifying Change

●  Watch trends, weak signals, and inflection points that shift costs or demand.

●  Sources: customer feedback, sales data, local traffic, digital activity, supplier updates.

●  Ask: So what? How soon? How big? to decide which signals need action.

4.1.2 From Scan to Action – The SIR Method

●  Signal: What changed (e.g., “Outages 3× per week”).

●

Implication: What it means (“Late deliveries, lost sales”).

●  Response: First step (“Pre-batch at 11:00, inverter for POS, outage menu”).

●  Keep responses short, assign an owner and due date.

●  Act on small pilots weekly—don’t wait for big plans.

4.1.3 Scanning Cadence

●  Weekly (20 min): Spot one key change and test one response.
