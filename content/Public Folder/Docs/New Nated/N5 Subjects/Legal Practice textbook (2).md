Statutory Update
This summary outlines recent legislative and judicial changes relevant to contract law, succession law, and
litigation procedures in South Africa. These updates ensure students have the most current legal
knowledge for their studies and future practice.
1. Contract Law Updates
1.1 Suspensive Conditions in Contracts
Legislation: No direct statutory amendments, but recent case law emphasizes compliance with contractual
suspensive conditions.
Key Case: Vantage Goldfields SA (Pty) Ltd v Siyakhula Sonke Empowerment Corporation (Pty) Ltd and
Another (2024)
• Issue: Can a contract with an unfulfilled suspensive condition be revived?
• Ruling: The Supreme Court of Appeal held that contracts containing suspensive conditions lapse if not
met within the specified timeframe and cannot be revived unless explicitly agreed upon by both parties.
• Impact: This reinforces the importance of clear contract drafting to specify how suspensive conditions
are managed and the consequences of failure.
1.2 Contractual Freedom and Public Policy
Key Development: The Constitutional Court has reiterated that contractual freedom must be balanced
with public interest considerations to prevent unfair contractual terms from being enforced.
Application: Ensure contracts do not contain unreasonably harsh clauses that could be considered con-
trary to public policy or constitutional rights.
2. Succession Law Updates
2.1 Recognition of Life Partnerships in Intestate Succession
Legislation: The Intestate Succession Act has been interpreted to align with constitutional principles fol-
lowing judicial decisions.
8

Key Case: Bwanya v Master of the High Court, Cape Town, and Others (2021)
• Issue: Whether a surviving life partner in a permanent opposite-sex relationship has inheritance rights
under intestate succession laws.
• Ruling: The Constitutional Court declared that excluding life partners from inheritance rights is un-
constitutional.
• Impact: Courts now recognize permanent life partnerships in the same manner as marriage, ensuring
fair treatment of surviving partners.
2.2 Customary Law of Succession and Equality
Key Case: Hassam v Jacobs NO and Others (2009)
• Issue: Whether spouses in polygamous Muslim marriages are entitled to inherit under intestate succes-
sion.
• Ruling: The Constitutional Court ruled that Muslim spouses must be included in intestate succes-
sion laws, ensuring gender equality.
• Impact: Customary succession laws must now align with constitutional rights, protecting surviving
spouses and children.
3. Litigation Procedures Updates
3.1 Corporate Litigation and Director Accountability
Legislation: Companies Amendment Acts 16 and 17 of 2024
Key Changes:
• Extended deadlines for director delinquency applications (from 5 to 10 years).
• Greater transparency in company litigation, requiring disclosure in financial statements.
• Impact: Legal practitioners must ensure corporate clients comply with these new regulations to avoid
penalties and ethical corporate governance.
3.2 Court Oversight in Execution of Judgments
Key Case: Gundwana v Steko Development CC and Others (2011)
9

• Issue: Whether a court must review execution orders involving the sale of a primary residence.
• Ruling: The Constitutional Court held that execution orders affecting homes require judicial oversight to
prevent unfair dispossession.
• Impact: Strengthens the protection of homeowners in debt recovery proceedings, ensuring compliance
with constitutional rights to housing.
These statutory updates reflect recent case law and legislative changes that shape contract law, succes-
sion law, and litigation procedures in South Africa. Students should be aware of these developments when
interpreting legal principles, drafting documents, and applying legal reasoning in practice.
These updates should be integrated into study materials to ensure alignment with the current legal frame-
work. Future revisions should continuously monitor new case law and statutory amendments to maintain
accuracy and relevance in legal education.

The Role and Function of the Legal Profession
The legal profession is a pillar of justice in society, ensuring the fair application of laws, protection of rights,
and resolution of disputes. Attorneys play a crucial role in advocating for clients, drafting legal documents,
and advising individuals, businesses, and governments on legal matters. The legal profession is diverse,
encompassing attorneys, advocates, and legal advisers who work in both public and private sectors.
A well-functioning legal system relies on ethical and professional legal practitioners who uphold justice and
ensure due process. The functions of the legal profession extend beyond courtrooms and litigation, playing
an essential role in economic transactions, contract enforcement, corporate governance, and legislative
development.
Key Functions of the Legal Profession
Administration of Justice:
• Legal practitioners interpret and apply the law to maintain social order.
12

• They work within the judicial system to resolve disputes fairly and lawfully.
Legal Representation and Advocacy:
Attorneys and advocates represent clients in civil and criminal cases.
They ensure that individuals accused of crimes receive a fair trial and defense.
Providing Legal Advice and Consultation:
• Lawyers advise clients on legal rights, obligations, and potential risks.
• Legal practitioners assist businesses in compliance, taxation, and employment law.
Drafting and Reviewing Legal Documents:
• Lawyers prepare and review contracts, wills, property deeds, and business agreements.
• They ensure documents adhere to legal standards and protect client interests.
Protection of Rights and Freedoms:
• Lawyers play an active role in human rights advocacy.
• They challenge discriminatory laws and practices that infringe on constitutional rights.
Law Reform and Policy Development:
• The legal profession contributes to shaping and modifying laws in response to societal needs.
• Legal experts provide guidance to policymakers on constitutional and legislative matters.
A society without a robust legal profession risks instability, lack of accountability, and violation of fundamen-
tal rights. The role of legal practitioners extends beyond individuals and businesses to national governance,
economic development, and social justice.
Types of Legal Practitioners
Legal practitioners serve different functions depending on their qualifications, areas of specialization, and

1. Attorneys (Lawyers/Solicitors)
Attorneys are general legal practitioners who provide direct legal services to clients. They are registered
with the Legal Practice Council and can practice independently or as part of a firm. Attorneys handle both
litigation and non-litigation work, meaning they can:
• Represent clients in court cases.
• Draft and review legal documents.
• Advise businesses on corporate law and regulatory compliance.
• Handle matters related to contracts, labor law, and family law.
• Facilitate property transactions and estate planning.
Attorneys are licensed to appear in lower courts, but some can appear in the High Court after additional
certification. They act as legal representatives in civil disputes, criminal cases, and arbitration proceedings.
2. Advocates (Barristers)
Advocates specialize in courtroom advocacy and legal argumentation. They are independent practitioners
who are briefed by attorneys to represent clients in complex cases. Unlike attorneys, advocates:
• Focus on litigation rather than direct client engagement.
• Have specialized expertise in legal interpretation and case law.
• Represent clients in High Courts, Supreme Court of Appeal, and Constitutional Court.
• Are regulated by the General Council of the Bar (Bar Association).
Advocates undergo pupillage (one-year training) before becoming fully qualified. They do not engage in
general legal work but concentrate on presenting cases, cross-examining witnesses, and persuading judg-
es through legal reasoning.
3. Legal Advisers (Corporate Lawyers / In-House Counsel)
Legal advisers work within organizations, corporations, and government departments to provide internal
legal counsel. They:
•

• Ensure compliance with laws and regulations.
• Draft and review business contracts, policies, and agreements
• Advise management on risk assessment and dispute resolution.
• Represent companies in negotiations and legal settlements.
Legal advisers do not appear in court but focus on preventing legal disputes. Their role is critical in
ensuring companies follow laws related to employment, taxation, and governance.
The Legal Practice Act and Regulatory Bodies
The Legal Practice Act 28 of 2014 (LPA) governs the legal profession in South Africa. It establishes the
Legal Practice Council (LPC) as the regulatory body responsible for overseeing attorneys and advocates.
Key Provisions of the Legal Practice Act
• Regulation of Legal Practitioners – Establishes the qualifications and professional standards for at-
torneys and advocates.
• Code of Conduct – Defines the ethical obligations of legal professionals.
• Disciplinary Procedures – Outlines the processes for handling complaints and misconduct cases.
• Trust Account Management – Regulates the handling of client funds to prevent misappropriation.
• Transformation of the Legal Profession – Ensures diversity, equitable access, and fair opportunities
in legal practice.
The Role of the Legal Practice Council (LPC)
The LPC enforces ethical standards and professional conduct by:
• Licensing and admitting attorneys and advocates into legal practice.
• Monitoring compliance with ethical and financial regulations.
• Investigating complaints of misconduct and imposing disciplinary actions.
Attorneys must comply with LPC regulations to maintain good standing in the profession.
Ethical Responsibilities of Attorneys
Ethics in legal practice ensures that attorneys conduct themselves with integrity, fairness, and profession-
alism. Ethical violations can lead to disciplinary action, suspension, or disbarment.
Key Ethical Obligations
Attorneys must adhere to the following ethical principles:
• Confidentiality – Maintain client confidentiality and attorney-client privilege.
• Avoiding Conflicts of Interest – Attorneys cannot represent opposing parties in a dispute.
15

• Duty of Competence – Provide high-quality legal services based on knowledge, skill, and diligence.
• Honesty and Integrity – Refrain from fraudulent, misleading, or deceptive practices.
• Fair Billing Practices – Charge reasonable fees in accordance with prescribed tariffs.
Failure to uphold these obligations damages professional credibility and can lead to legal consequences.
Confidentiality and Attorney-Client Privilege
Confidentiality is a cornerstone of legal ethics, ensuring that clients can share sensitive information without
fear of exposure. However, exceptions apply where:
• Disclosure is required by law (e.g., reporting a client’s intent to commit a crime).
• The client waives privilege voluntarily.
Breaching confidentiality can result in serious legal repercussions, including sanctions and loss of practic-
ing rights.
Handling Client Funds and Trust Accounts
Attorneys often hold funds on behalf of clients, including settlement proceeds, property transactions, and
legal fees. The Legal Practice Act mandates strict trust account regulations to prevent misappropriation
and ensure financial integrity.
Trust Account Regulations
• Trust money must be kept separate from the attorney’s business funds.
• Trust accounts must be audited annually to ensure compliance.
• Funds must only be withdrawn with proper authorization from the client.
Interest on trust accounts is regulated and, in some cases, must be paid into the Legal Practitioners’ Fideli-
ty Fund.
The Legal Practitioners' Fidelity Fund (LPFF)
The LPFF protects the public from financial loss due to theft or mismanagement of trust funds by attor-
neys. Clients may claim compensation from this fund if an attorney misappropriates trust money.
Mismanagement of trust accounts is one of the most serious ethical violations and can result in permanent
disbarment and criminal charges.
Conflicts of Interest in Legal Practice
A conflict of interest arises when an attorney's personal interests or relationships interfere with their duty to
a client.
Common Conflicts of Interest
• Representing both parties in a legal dispute.
16

• Having a financial stake in a client’s case.
• Serving as both legal advisor and witness in the same case.
• Accepting a case where the attorney has a personal bias.
To avoid conflicts of interest, attorneys must disclose any potential conflicts and recuse themselves when
necessary.
Professional Conduct in Litigation
Attorneys must maintain high standards of conduct during litigation to ensure fairness and uphold the cred-
ibility of the judicial system.
Ethical Conduct During Trial
• Respect for the court – Attorneys must behave professionally in court, addressing judges and oppos-
ing counsel with decorum.
• Truthfulness in pleadings – All documents submitted to the court must be accurate and truthful.
• Avoiding frivolous litigation – Attorneys must not file cases that lack legal merit solely to delay pro-
ceedings or harass opponents.
Misconduct in litigation can result in contempt of court charges and disciplinary action.
Disciplinary Procedures for Attorneys
Attorneys who violate ethical rules may face disciplinary proceedings initiated by the Legal Practice Coun-
cil.
Stages of Disciplinary Action
1. Complaint Filing – A complaint is lodged against the attorney.
2. Investigation – The LPC reviews evidence and determines if a hearing is required.
3. Disciplinary Hearing – The attorney presents their defense before the disciplinary committee.
4. Sanctions – If found guilty, sanctions may include fines, suspension, or disbarment.
In serious cases, criminal prosecution may also be pursued against the attorney.

Public vs. Private Legal Practitioners
Legal professionals can work in either public service or private practice, depending on their career paths
and preferences.
Public Legal Practitioners (Government Lawyers)
Public legal practitioners serve in government institutions and act in the interest of the state. They include:
• State Attorneys: Represent the government in civil matters.
• Public Prosecutors: Work for the National Prosecuting Authority (NPA) and prosecute criminal
cases.
• Legal Aid Lawyers: Provide free legal services to individuals who cannot afford attorneys.
• Judges and Magistrates: Preside over court cases, delivering rulings and interpreting laws.
Advantages of Public Practice:
• Job security and stable salary.
• Serving the public interest and access to justice.
• Opportunities to specialize in criminal law, constitutional law, and regulatory affairs.
Challenges of Public Practice:
• Limited financial incentives compared to private firms.
• High caseloads and government bureaucracy.

Private Legal Practitioners
Private legal practitioners work in private firms, corporations, or as independent consultants.
Examples of Private Legal Practice:
• Solo Practitioners: Operate independent law firms.

Advantages of Private Practice:
• Higher earning potential.
• Freedom to choose clients and cases.
• Opportunity to specialize in various legal fields.
Challenges of Private Practice:
• Competitive environment requiring networking and business acumen.
• High workloads, especially for new attorneys building their careers.

Types of Legal Firms
Legal firms can be classified into different categories based on ownership, size, and operational structure.
Each type has its advantages and challenges, depending on the nature of the legal services offered.
1. Sole Proprietorship (Single-Attorney Practice)
A sole proprietorship is a law practice owned and managed by a single attorney. This type of firm is com-
mon among small-scale practitioners who handle general legal matters.
Features:
• Owned and managed by one attorney.
• Typically focuses on general law practice (e.g., family law, civil litigation, wills, and estates).
• The attorney is responsible for all business operations, legal work, and financial management.
21

Advantages:
• Lower operational costs compared to larger firms.
• The attorney has full control over decision-making and client relationships.
• Flexible work structure and direct client engagement.
Challenges:
• Heavy workload, as all legal and administrative duties rest on one person.
• Limited resources, making it difficult to handle complex cases.
• Difficulty in expanding the business due to financial and operational constraints.
2. Partnership Law Firms
A partnership firm consists of two or more attorneys who jointly own and manage the practice. The partners
share responsibilities, profits, and liabilities while working under a common firm name.
Features:
• Co-owned by two or more partners.
• Can specialize in multiple areas of law (e.g., litigation, corporate law, property law).
• Partners share profits, expenses, and decision-making responsibilities.
• May employ junior associates, clerks, and paralegals for support.
Advantages:
• The pooling of resources allows for better case management and marketing.
• Clients benefit from a wider range of expertise.
• Improved financial stability and business growth opportunities.
Challenges:
• Disagreements between partners can affect business operations.
• Profits must be shared among all partners.
• Requires clear partnership agreements to avoid disputes.
22

3. Corporate Law Firms (Large Law Firms)
Corporate firms operate as registered business entities, often employing dozens or hundreds of legal
professionals. They handle high-profile clients such as government agencies, multinational corporations,
and financial institutions.
Features:
• Large-scale operations with specialized legal departments.
• Employ partners, associates, paralegals, and administrative staff.
• Work on complex legal matters, such as mergers, acquisitions, corporate litigation, and intellectual
property law.
• May have offices in multiple locations or countries.
Advantages:
• Access to large clients and high-value cases.
• Strong financial backing allows for expansion and investment in technology.
• Specialization enhances service quality and client satisfaction.
Challenges:
• High operational costs, including salaries, office space, and technology.
• Intense workload and pressure to meet performance targets.
• Less personal client engagement compared to smaller firms.
Key Personnel in a Legal Firm
A legal firm consists of various professionals, each playing a role in legal service delivery and firm
management.
1. Partners (Senior Attorneys / Owners)
• Equity Partners: Own part of the firm and share profits.
• Non-Equity Partners: Senior attorneys without ownership stakes but hold management roles.
• Responsible for decision-making, strategy, and high-value cases.
23

2. Associates (Junior Attorneys)
• Employed by the firm but do not have ownership rights.
• Work under partners to gain experience and expertise.
• Can specialize in corporate law, litigation, labor law, or real estate.
3. Paralegals / Legal Assistants
• Provide administrative and research support to attorneys.
• Draft legal documents, organize case files, and manage court schedules.
• Cannot offer legal advice or represent clients in court.
4. Clerks (Legal Secretaries)
• Handle documentation, client communication, and office organization.
• Manage court filings, appointment scheduling, and legal correspondence.
5. Administrative Staff
• Includes office managers, receptionists, and accountants.
• Oversee billing, HR, technology, and office operations.
A well-managed firm balances legal expertise with administrative efficiency to ensure smooth service
