5. Structure of the ICB Exam
Your ICB Exam will be 3.5 hours long and count for a maximum of 180 marks. The paper will consist of
three sections. Section A is compulsory, and will be in the form of 20 multiple choice questions for a
total of 40 marks. Section B consists of three longer questions, all of which are compulsory. For one of
the questions in Section B, the topics will vary from exam session to exam session. Section C consists
of two longer questions, only one of which is compulsory. Five possible questions are included in the
table below for Section C, but only two of the five possible questions will appear in the ICB Exam.

Introduction
Aim of this Learning Module
The aim of this Learning Module is to embed the main principles of economics that govern the way in
which business is conducted in South Africa and around the world. A distinction is drawn between
micro- and macroeconomics, and the laws of supply and demand are demonstrated on a demand and
supply schedule. The ways by which market equilibrium can be achieved, distorted and renegotiated
are discussed with reference to the demand and supply schedule. Through all of this, the interaction
between the main participants in the economy is explored. The Learning Module endeavours to answer
questions such as:
• What is meant by the ‘unspoken laws’ of business?
• What are the main economic forces that govern the manner in which businesses operate?
• How does interest rate fluctuations affect an economy and people’s spending habits?
• Why is inflation a problem?
• What is deflation and is it good for an economy?
The answers to all of these questions will assist you in forming an informed opinion about the basics of
economics and the related unspoken laws of business.
Learning Module outline
• Defining economics
• The five main participants in the economy
• Demand and supply
• Movements in the demand and supply curves
• The effects of inflation on an economy

affects the ‘man on the street’;

Feedback on prior knowledge
Economists use graphs to illustrate the relationships between different quantities and qualities. To
understand any theory about economics and to reason in economic terms, you must therefore be able
to use graphs. Graphs are a very useful tool, because they enable one to see quite complicated
relationships at a glance.
The first step in understanding graphs is to become familiar with what we call a ‘number line’. Such a
line is shown in Figure A below.
Figure A
There are several things you should notice about the number line. As you can see in Figure A, there is
a small line above the zero point, ‘0’, that is at right angles to the number line. This line divides the
number line into two parts. The part to the right of the zero point contains positive numbers, and is
called the positive segment of the line. The part to the left of the zero point contains negative numbers
(‘minus’ numbers, like -5), and is called the negative segment. Each number represents a point on the
line. The numbers increase in value from left to right. For example, 10 is to the right of 5, and it is bigger

than 5. The same is true of the negative segment of the line: -5 is to the right of -10, and it is a bigger
number than -10. (This sometimes confuses people, but remember that when negative numbers get
‘bigger’, they are in fact smaller: when you have -R 10 in your bank account, you have less money than
when you have -R 5.). The spaces between the numbers on the number line are called intervals. In
Figure A, the intervals are all equal. Every interval is 5 units long. In graphs, we use the numbers on the
number line to stand for quantities of things. The distance between 0 and 5 might represent 5 bottles of
milk, and the distance between 0 and 20 might represent 20 bottles of milk. On different number lines,
the scale may differ. For instance, a distance of 1 centimetre could represent 5 units on one number
line, but 5 000 units on another. The number line in Figure A is horizontal – it runs from left to right.
(‘Horizontal’ actually means ‘in line with the surface of the earth’.) We can also draw a vertical line, that
is, one that runs ‘up and down’, or at right angles to the horizontal line. Consider the one in Figure B. As
we move up the vertical line, the numbers increase in value. Below zero the numbers are negative and
above zero the numbers are positive. As was the case with our horizontal number line, the intervals are
the same.

We create a graph by drawing a horizontal and vertical number line in such a way that they intersect
(cross) each other at the zero point of both lines. The two lines of the graph are called ‘axes’ – the
horizontal axis and the vertical axis. Note the following terms that are used when we are
talking about graphs:
• The vertical axis is called the y-axis.
• The horizontal axis is called the x-axis.
• The point of intersection between the two lines (the zero point) is the origin.
When we have only one line, we need only one number to specify any point on the line. In a graph,

Feedback on Integration task 1.1
(a) A strike of transport workers might mean that ordinary people have no transport to work. This
could mean that fewer goods are produced in that time, and companies lose money. This in turn
could mean that companies will be unable to give their workers a good increase in the
forthcoming year. This could cause a ripple effect resulting in reduced wages and spending,
nervousness of investors and negative sentiment.
(b) An increase in the petrol price means that companies that produce food and clothes will have to
pay more to have their products transported to the retail outlets. To avoid making a loss, they
may increase the costs of their products. Consumers would then have to pay more for their food
and clothes. Taxi, bus and train fare might also increase.
(c) Until recently, Telkom has been the only provider of landline telephone services in the country.
Because we need phones and they were the only service provider, they could charge what they
liked for telephone calls. If their new competitors charge less than this, everyone may want to use
the competitors’ services and Telkom may have to lower their rates. The fact that Telkom is
getting competitors may mean that in the future people will pay less for their telephone calls.
(d) An increase in the repo-rate is usually introduced to counter rising inflation rates. When interest
rates are low, it becomes cheaper to borrow money. People therefore spend more money on

goods and services as real income levels increase, resulting in higher inflation rates. By raising
the repo-rate (the rate at which the central bank lends funds to commercial banks), the SARB is

reduced demand causes a drop in prices (or smaller increases).
From this exercise we begin to see how money, poor people, prices, interest rates, strikes, the
exchange rate, taxes and so on, affect us all. In economics we try to analyse these problems in order
to understand them better, and sometimes in order to try and suggest solutions for them. Thus,
economics is about what is happening around us. Learning more about the subject can help you to
better understand your own situation, and help you plan better for the future.

Suppose that, for some reason, the price of distilled water drops to R 10 per litre. This is exceptionally
cheap, and many people will subsequently like to buy the product at this price. In our example,
10 kilolitres are demanded at the price of R 10 (point A on the graph). However, at this price, there
simply is not enough distilled water supplied to meet the demand. Remember the law of supply: the
lower the price, the less producers will supply in the market. At a price of R 10, only 2 kilolitres of
distilled water will be supplied to the market (point B on the graph). At this price there is thus a demand
for 10 kilolitres, but a supply of only 2 kilolitres. There is thus an excess demand of 8 kilolitres. One
could also say there is a shortage of the product.
The question now arises: What can be expected in the case of a shortage? Since there is simply not
nearly enough distilled water for everybody at the low price, some of the potential buyers will be willing
to offer a higher price to get hold of the product. Say, for example, some buyers are willing to offer R 20
per kilolitre. Not all the buyers who are willing to buy at R 10 are willing to buy at R 20: at this price,
there is a demand for 8 kilolitres (point C). At this price, suppliers can produce more and are willing to
supply 4 kilolitres (point D). There is thus still an excess demand, but at these prices it has decreased:
the excess demand is now 4 kilolitres (i.e. the 8 kilolitres that are demanded minus the 4 kilolitres that
are supplied).

However, because there is still a large excess demand, some buyers are willing to pay even more to
get distilled water. Some of them are even prepared to pay R 30 per litre (we say that buyers ‘bid up’
the price to R 30).Together, the number of buyers who are willing to pay R 30 per litre demand
6 kilolitres of distilled water. When the price is R 30, more suppliers are willing to supply distilled water,
and collectively the suppliers offer 6 kilolitres to the market. Supply and demand are now in equilibrium:
at R 30, 6 kilolitres are demanded and 6 kilolitres are supplied. There is now also no excess demand,
and no excess supply, and the price is set at R 30.
Of course, what may also happen is that the suppliers, who will see that many people are eager to get
the product, may raise the price. Either the buyers or the sellers, or both of them together, can thus
cause the price to increase.
We can thus draw the following conclusion:
When prices are low and there is excess demand or shortage, buyers will tend to bid up the price,
and/or suppliers will raise the price, until it reaches the equilibrium point.
We have now seen what happens when prices start out at a lower point than the equilibrium point.
What happens in the opposite situation, when prices are very high, i.e. much higher that the equilibrium
point?
Of course, suppliers want to sell at as high a price as possible, and (in terms of the law of supply) the
higher the prices, the more they are willing to supply. Let’s say, then, that suppliers decide that they will
charge R 50 per litre of distilled water, and at this price they can supply 10 kilolitres (point F in the
graph). However, very few buyers are willing to pay this price, because they simply cannot afford it. At
R 50, only 2 kilolitres of distilled water are demanded (point G). Here we therefore have an excess
supply, also called a surplus, of 8 kilolitres: 10 kilolitres are supplied, but only 2 kilolitres are demanded.
Suppliers are thus left with a huge surplus of distilled water, and will make severe losses if they do
nothing further about the situation. In such a case, suppliers tend to lower the price of the product, so
that they can sell more (or buyers persuade them to lower the price by offering to buy the product at a
lower price). Let’s say the suppliers lower the price to R 40. At these prices, they are able to supply 8
kilolitres of distilled water; however, demand at this price is only 4 kilolitres. There is thus still a surplus
of distilled water, although the surplus is now smaller (4 kilolitres). Eventually, the suppliers will lower
the price still further, or “bid down” the price (or the buyers will persuade them to lower the price by
offering lower prices). When the price reaches R 30, equilibrium point will once again have been
reached: 6 kilolitres will be supplied, and 6 kilolitres demanded.
We can therefore draw a similar conclusion to the one we stated above:
When prices are high and there is an excess supply or surplus, suppliers will tend to bid down the price
and/or buyers will offer to buy at lower prices, until the price reaches equilibrium point.
