Feedback on Integration task 1.1

Accounting is currently one of the fastest changing and complex subjects in the world. The South African body of accountants have adopted the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), a set of reporting standards that are constantly being updated to ensure that all member countries speak the same language of business. The idea of a single global accounting language is nothing new. It all began with the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC) in 1973. Over the years, this committee developed many global accounting standards, referred to as International Accounting Standards (IAS). This committee was then replaced by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) in 2001. The new board adopted all of the standards developed by the IASC and started the development of additional standards for global use. This committee has developed additional global accounting standards referred to as the International Financial Reporting Standards.

It is important to note that these standards are reporting standards, not recording standards. They also have a bearing on listed companies only. Sole proprietorships and partnerships in South Africa can set their financial statements up in accordance with ‘gaap’ (notice, we nowadays distinguish between of a ‘small gaap’ and a ‘big GAAP’). Close corporations have to abide by the requirements of the IAS statements, but there is no need for these entities to adopt the IFRS statements as yet.

For now we need to highlight a few of the internationally used terminologies and compare these with the traditionally used terms in South Africa:

Terminologies used in South Africa IAS / IFRS equivalent

Debtors Accounts receivable, trade and other receivables

Creditors Accounts payable, trade and other payables

Feedback on Integration task 1.2

This is a very important integration task. It explains, to a large extent, why there is an accounting framework and why GAAP, IAS and IFRS exist. If one looks at the two statements of financial position given, it becomes clear that the total assets and total liabilities (and subsequently the total owner’s equity) of the two companies are equal in value. Both companies are therefore equally solvent. However, as an investment opportunity, the one company offers much less risk than the other. The key to this realisation lies in the relative weighting of the current vs. the non-current components of the assets and liabilities. Company B is preferred above Company A as a possible investment, because Company A has a current liquidity crisis. Current liabilities for Company A amount to R 4 million. These current liabilities need to be settled within one year, and this will surely be done by disposing of assets that are liquid i.e. which can be turned into cash within a year. Company A’s current assets amount to R 3 million, which means they have insufficient liquid assets to cover their short-term obligations. This essentially means that Company A will have to start selling off their fixed assets soon to pay off their current debts, unless their liquidity position improves. If a company needs to dispose of fixed assets to cover short-term debts, it could lead to eventual sequestration.

Note the following:

• In order for the accounting equation to balance, the R 500 must be written down twice. In this instance, both sides of the accounting equation add up to minus R 500.

• The ‘- R 500’ under assets refers to the reduction in assets in the form of cash (the bank balance is decreasing). Refer to the rules of double entry. All asset accounts will be credited if their balances are to be reduced. Hence, the amount of R 500 is recorded on the credit side (right-hand side) of the bank account.

• The ‘- R 500’ under owner’s equity merely shows that a reduction in the owner’s wealth has taken place by means of an increase in expenses (the more costs you incur, the poorer you become). The application account is going to be the wages account. Expenses are increasing, and the rules of double entry clearly state that expenses are to be debited when their balances are increased.

• It is general practice to include a cross-reference in every T-account. When an account is debited, we write the name of the contra-account (the account that is credited) as a cross-reference. When an account is credited, we write the name of the contra-account (the account that is debited) as a cross-reference. This helps to create a proper audit trail. In layman’s terms: we have two accounts, namely bank and wages. To cross-reference, we will write ‘bank’ in wages and ‘wages’ in bank.

Practice exercise 1f

(i) A business will be liable to register for VAT if the income earned from selling goods or fees earned from services supplied is more than R 1 million per annum, or is reasonably expected to exceed this amount and provided that the business is making taxable (not exempt) supplies.

This type of registration is referred to as a compulsory registration. A person who is registered, or who is obliged to register is referred to as a ‘vendor’. (Note: it is the legal person and not the trading name of a business which is required to register.)

If your sales or fees earned do not exceed R 1 million, you can register for VAT voluntarily if you make annual taxable supplies above R 50 000 (R 120 000 in the case of persons who supply commercial accommodation). This type of registration is referred to as a voluntary registration.

In order to further clarify under which circumstances a business should register for VAT let us look at the following examples:

Fani Nkula owns a business. His yearly turnover is R 750 000. This entire turnover comes from the sale of standard-rated items. Since his turnover falls below R 1 million he is not required to register. However, because his turnover falls between R 50 000 and R 1 million and he is selling taxable supplies, he may register on a voluntary basis. Also, should his yearly turnover increase to more than R 1 million then it would be compulsory for him to register as a VAT vendor.

Note: If you start a new business and expect to earn more than R 1 million in 12 months from taxable supplies; or if in the course of trading you suddenly expect to earn more than R 1 million, then it is essential that you contact SARS to clarify whether or not you should be registered. Failing to do so may result in a charge of VAT evasion for which there are stiff penalties and possible prosecution.

Peter Mohlare owns a business. His yearly turnover is R 1 200 000. This entire turnover comes from the sales of zero-rated items. Since his turnover is more than R 1 million, and zero-rated items fall under taxable supplies, Peter must be registered as a VAT vendor. Because Peter is registered for VAT he may claim back certain of the VAT inputs he has paid in the production of his sales.
