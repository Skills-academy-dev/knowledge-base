2. The ICB
The ICB was established in 1931. Their role is to improve and maintain the quality of bookkeeping,
accounting, office administration, financial management, and entrepreneurship training and
qualifications in southern Africa. Their aim is to build the credibility and reputation of these fields.
The QCTO has delegated functions to both Fasset and the Institute of Certified Bookkeepers (ICB) as
Quality Assurance Partners, for the qualifications associated with the ICB. Under this delegation, the
ICB is responsible for the assessment of student competency and Fasset/QCTO is responsible for the
certification of such achievements, amongst other things.

Learning Module 1:
The key principles of effective business communication
Introduction
Aim of this Learning Module
The aim of this Learning Module is to introduce the key principles of effective business communication.
The module endeavours to answer questions such as the following:
• Why is effective communication important in the workplace?
• How can one correctly identify and own an audience?
• How does one adapt one’s communication style to different communication situations?
• Why is it important to harmonise one’s verbal and non-verbal communication?
• How can we overcome the barriers to effective communication?
The answers to all of these questions will assist you in forming an informed opinion about the basics of
effective business communication.
Learning Module outline
• The importance of effective communication
• The communication process
• Categories of communication
• Formal and informal communication
• Verbal and non-verbal communication
• Listening skills
• Barriers to effective communication
Learning Module outcomes
At the end of the Learning Module you should be able to:
• list reasons why effective communication is important in the workplace;
• explain the communication process;
• identify your audience in this process;
• distinguish between different communication situations/categories;
• recognise the importance of adapting your communication style to different communication
situations;
• distinguish between formal and informal communication;
• differentiate between and recognise verbal and non-verbal communication in the workplace;
• identify explicit and implicit messages in verbal and non-verbal communication
• understand the importance of listening to achieve effective communication; and
• list the barriers to effective communication and how they can be overcome.

Feedback on Integration task 1.4
In this Integration task the key idea is to introduce you to the appropriate register to use in different
situations. Greeting friends is different to greeting a customer for the first time.
Greeting 1
Although this is a very casual greeting, it is acceptable because John and Charlie know each other well.
They have not greeted each other in front of a customer or business associate, so it is appropriate.
Suggesting having a beer after work with a friend is quite acceptable. The slang used – ‘howzit’ and
‘wanna’ – is also understandable, so no miscommunication takes place. However, some more formal
organisations would prefer even old friends to greet each other more formally in an office environment.
The most correct language to use is:
‘Hello Charlie, how are you? I have not seen you for ages – do you want to have a beer after work?’
Greeting 2
This greeting is inappropriate for the following reasons:
• It is too casual for a senior partner.
• The words like ‘howzit’ and the actions of hugging are too familiar for a person you do not know
and ‘howzit’ and ‘grab a beer’ are also slang so they may not be understood by a Japanese
person.
• Assuming that a Japanese woman greets others in this manner shows lack of business
understanding – using simple, inoffensive verbal and body language is safest when meeting
someone from another country, and talking about alcohol should be avoided. If in doubt, waiting

Feedback on Integration task 1.5
• Discussions on this task can be very broad and can generate debate about whether non-verbal
communication is the same for all cultures, genders and age groups. It is very important to realise
that interpretation of non-verbal communication is subjective, even within like population groups.
Figure A: Boredom
A hand on the chin resting on a surface and a slumped posture usually indicates lack of interest or
boredom, or even fatigue. Eyes not looking directly at you or roving around the room also imply that the
person would rather be doing something else. This individual could also be seen as being frustrated,
tired or sad.
Figure B: Laughing
Laughing is one of the few examples of body language or facial expression that tend to be universal to
all cultures. The message is friendliness, interest, and having something in common. Laughing can
sometimes be subtle but it can also be overt.
Figure C: Thinking
Leaning forward, frowning slightly, holding an object like a pen to the mouth indicates concentration or
thinking. The message is that the person is focused and interested.
Figure D: Formal but friendly
Having some personal space between persons and walking next to them in a set formation shows a
level of formality, but smiling at each other implies that a friendship exists and that the relationship is
amicable.
Figure E: Threatening
Another of the universal non-verbal messages is anger, or being threatened. The two police officers are
standing very close to each other, and they each have solemn and frowning expressions. One officer
has his hand placed on his gun which may indicate animosity. In this figure, the interpretation is
extremely subjective: a criminal would interpret charging police officers as threatening, but a victim
would view the police officers as safe and welcome them with relief.
Figure F: Serious
Often, when a person folds their arms, they are indicating that they are being serious or stern. In some
instances, folding one's arms could indicate formality and could make a person seem less
approachable, as their body language is closed off to others.
• Problems can arise in the workplace if a person's verbal communication does not correspond with
their non-verbal communication, as it can lead to confusion and misunderstandings. For instance,
imagine a new employees tells their boss that they are extremely excited to join their new company.
