Feedback to Integration task 1.1

Accounting is currently one of the fastest changing and complex subjects in the world. The South African body of accountants adopted the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), a set of reporting standards that are constantly updated to ensure that all member countries speak the same language of business. The idea of a single global accounting language is nothing new. It all began with the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC) in 1973. Over the years, this committee developed 41 global accounting standards, referred to as International Accounting Standards (IAS). This committee was then replaced by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) in 2001. The new board adopted all 41 of these standards, and started the development of additional standards for global use. However, some of these 41 IASs have either been fully withdrawn or replaced by new International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRSs). This committee has developed 16 additional global accounting standards thus far, referred to as the International Financial Reporting Standards. We therefore now have a total of 41 global accounting standards (GAS):

• 25 IASs (produced by the old committee); and

• 16 IFRSs (produced by the new board).

Feedback to Integration task 1.2

This is a very important Integration task. It explains, to a large extent, why there is an accounting framework and why gaap, GAAP, IAS and IFRS exist. If one looks at the two statements of financial position given, it becomes clear that the total assets and total liabilities (and subsequently the total owner’s equity) of the two companies are equal in value. Both companies are therefore equally solvent. However, as an investment opportunity, the one company offers much less risk than the other. The key to this realisation lies in the relative weighting of the current vs. the non-current components of the assets and liabilities. Company B is preferred above company A as a possible investment, because company A has a current liquidity crisis. Current liabilities for Company A amount to R 4 million. These current liabilities need to be settled within one year, and this will surely be done by disposing assets that are liquid – i.e. which can be turned into cash within a year. Company A’s current assets amount to R 3 million, which means they have insufficient liquid assets to cover their short-term obligations. This essentially means that Company A will have to start selling off their fixed assets soon to pay off their current debts, unless their liquidity position improves. If a company needs to dispose of fixed assets to cover short-term debts, it could lead to eventual sequestration.

Consider the following analyses under the accounting equation:

Illustration 1 (paying for expenses from the bank account)

Transaction: Issue a cash cheque of R 500 for wages.

Assets (R) = Owner’s equity (R) + Liabilities (R)

\- 500 - 500 0

Dr Cr Dr Cr + Bank - + Wages -

Wages 500 Bank 500

Note the following:

• In order for the accounting equation to balance, the R 500 must be written down twice. In this instance, both sides of the accounting equation add up to minus R 500.

• The ‘- R 500’ under assets refers to the reduction in assets in the form of cash (the bank balance is decreasing). Refer to the rules of double entry. All asset accounts will be credited if their balances are to be reduced. Hence, the amount of R 500 is recorded on the credit side (right-hand side) of the bank account.

• The ‘- R 500’ under owner’s equity merely shows that a reduction in the owner’s wealth has taken place by means of an increase in expenses (the more costs you incur, the poorer you become). The application account is going to be the wages account. Expenses are increasing, and the rules of double entry clearly state that expenses are to be debited when their balances are increased.

• It is general practice to include a cross-reference in every T-account. When an account is debited, we write the name of the contra-account (the account that is credited) as a cross-reference. When an account is credited, we write the name of the contra-account (the account that is debited) as a cross-reference. This helps to create a proper audit trail. In layman’s terms: we have two accounts, namely bank and wages. To cross-reference, we will write ‘bank’ in wages and ‘wages’ in bank.

Feedback to Integration task 1.4

No, one cannot merely assume that all transactions have been recorded accurately. It only proves that the total debits equal the total credits (in numerical value). It does not prove that the correct amounts were recorded, nor does it prove that the correct accounts were debited and credited. For example, say the business purchased a computer, but debits the stationery account instead of the equipment account. Albeit that the transaction was not accurately recorded, the trial balance would still balance. Say the bookkeeper records a transaction with an inclusive price of R 1 000 instead of R 100, but the debits equal the credits – then the trial balance will still balance, even though an error has been made.

Practice exercise 1d

When dealing with trade payables, businesses need to perform regular reconciliations before settlements of accounts are done. Creditors reconciliation is a regular procedure followed before making payments to creditors. Reconciliations are done with regards to each and every creditor from which we have received an account statement. The purpose of this procedure is to make sure that the ‘amount due’ as reflected on the statement received from the creditor is accurate. We also need to ensure that the amounts reflected in the creditor’s individual account shown in our creditors ledger, are correct.

Creditors reconciliation is very similar to bank reconciliation. Firstly, mistakes or omissions in our books (i.e. in the creditor’s individual account in the creditors ledger) will be corrected in our books. It is very important to check how far-reaching our mistake is. For example, if we recorded the incorrect amount in the creditor’s individual account in our creditors ledger, we need to determine whether or not the same mistake was made in the subsidiary journal. If it was, we will need to correct the entry in our general journal. Theoretically, we could correct errors in some of the other journals, but in most cases the creditors reconciliation is done after the journals have been closed off already, which makes the general journal the most appropriate journal for such corrections.

Secondly, mistakes or omissions on the statement received from the creditor will be shown on a creditors reconciliation statement, which we keep as an internal document. This document serves as a ‘reminder’ of the amounts and corrections that need to be made by the creditor on our account as kept in their debtors ledger. It is common practice to use a proxy of the creditors reconciliation statement as a remittance advice, which we can attach to the cheque sent to the creditor in settlement of our account.

While we do not physically send a bank reconciliation statement to the bank, creditors’ reconciliation statements (in the form of a remittance advice) are in actual fact sent back to the creditor. The reason for this difference is that most of the amounts shown on a bank reconciliation statement are not actual mistakes made by the bank; they are only outstanding deposits and cheques, which will be reflected on the bank statement in due course anyway. On the other hand, a creditors reconciliation statement reflects actual mistakes or omissions by the creditor in question. Hence, the creditor will have to be informed of these errors before payment is made.
