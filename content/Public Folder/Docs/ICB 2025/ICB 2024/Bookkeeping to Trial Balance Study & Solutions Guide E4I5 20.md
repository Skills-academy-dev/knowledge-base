Feedback on Integration task 1.1

The main limitations of Financial Accounting information are the following:

Differences in standards and valuation methods

Financial accounting permits alternative approaches. In some cases there exists more than one principle for the treatment of any one item. This permits alternative treatments. For example, the closing inventory of a business may be valued using any one of the following methods: FIFO (First-in-First-out), Weighted Average Price, Standard Price etc., but the results are not comparable.

Information is not timely

Financial accounting does not provide timely information – it provides an after-the-fact analysis. Financial accounting is designed to supply information in the form of statements (statement of financial position and statement of profit or losss and other comprehensive income) for a period; normally one year. So the information is, at best, of historical interest and only 'post-mortem' analysis of the past can be conducted. The business requires timely information at frequent intervals to enable the management to plan and take corrective action. For example, if a business has budgeted that during the current year sales should be R 12 000 000, then it requires information as to whether or not the sales in the first month of the year amounted to R 1 000 000 and reasons for any shortfall or excess.

Having said this, most computerised accounting packages have largely overcome this limitation, by making internal reporting to management at shorter intervals a reality.

Subjectivity DOES play a role

There is always some element of subjectivity in Financial Accounting. Although modern accounting standards are geared towards making financial information as objective as possible, certain events estimates have to be made which requires personal judgement. For example, in order to determine the amount of depreciation to be charged every year for the use of a fixed asset it is required estimation and the income disclosed by accounting is not authoritative but a mere 'approximation'.

Financial accounting ignores important non-monetary information

Financial accounting does not consider those transactions that are not monetary in nature. For example, extent of competition faced by the business, technical innovations possessed by the business, loyalty and efficiency of the employees; changes in the value of money etc. are the important matters in which management of the business is highly interested, but accounting is not tailored to take note of such matters. Thus, any user of financial information is naturally deprived of vital information which is of non- monetary character.

Financial Accounting does not provide detailed analysis

The information supplied by the financial accounting is in reality aggregates of the financial transactions during the course of the year. Of course, it enables users to study the overall results of the business information but lacks detailed analysis of cost, revenue and profit of each product.

The effects of inflation is ignored

Accounting information is provided in monetary terms, but does not take into account the buying power of the stated figures at the time they were incurred. For example, the financial records for 20.0 may show a net profit of R 1 000 000, and the records for 20.1 a net profit of R 1 200 000, but the 20% ‘growth’ may well be offset by an equal (but unrecorded) inflation rate.

11 Step 1 (explaining the accounting equation) Start off by asking yourself what is meant by the term ‘equity’. Think of a layman’s term that could be used instead. Acceptable terms could range from ‘wealth or belongings’ to ‘capital or earnings’. We believe the most appropriate term to use here is ‘wealth’.

Focus on the word ‘owners’. A bit of grammatical editing is now called for. Note that an apostrophe is missing: i.e. Owner’s (referring to ONE owner) or Owners’ (referring to MORE THAN ONE owner). When we speak of John’s car, it essentially means ‘the car that belongs to John’. The same with the accounting equation: Owner’s equity means ‘the wealth of the sole proprietor in his/her business. In a partnership, we will speak of the owners’ equity or the partners’ funds. In a company, the term shareholders’ equity will be used (the apostrophe appears after the ‘s’ if there is more than one owner).

Owner’s equity refers to the owner’s wealth in his/her business, and can be calculated by subtracting total liabilities from total assets. This concept will be best understood if you know exactly what an asset is, and what a liability is.

At this point in time you should note that the accounting equation is in fact nothing else but a framework for the statement of financial position – a statement that shows financial position at any point in time. When analysing a statement of financial position, one should ask hypothetically: ‘Suppose we were to close the business at this point in time, sold all the things that could be sold, and paid back all our debts. Whatever we would have left is the equity that belongs to the owner.’

You should now think of the real reason why people start businesses, namely to make a profit.

Drawings and capital

Transactions between the sole trader and his/her business will be recorded via the capital and drawings accounts. The capital account is involved when the owner makes capital contributions to the business. The drawings account is involved when the owner withdraws valuables from the business.

Expenses and income

Income and expense accounts will be used to record changes in owner’s equity. Income accounts are used to record increases in equity, and expense accounts show decreases in equity.

Sales or services rendered/current income, for a business that renders services is usually the first account listed under ‘income’.

Refer to the last four accounts listed under expenses in the solution. These accounts are used to record valuables that will lose their value within one year, hence making them expenses. A good idea might be to refer back to the assets discussed earlier. Would one be able to sell vehicles, equipment, furniture or trading inventory? The answer to all of these is yes\!. Now, one would also be able to sell stationery or packing materials – but these items are not regarded as assets. Why? The reason is quite simple: although stationery, packing materials and fuel have trading value, they are not intended for resale and do not retain value; i.e. they are not likely to be turned into cash by the business.

A quick reminder: Assets are resources controlled by the firm from which future economic benefits are likely to flow.

Step 2 (defining assets)

A series of definitions are provided for assets. Choose the definition you feel most comfortable with. A common mistake is to define assets as the possessions of the business, in other words to refer to them as those resources that are ‘owned’ by the business. Albeit that some assets could well be owned by the entity, this is not a prerequisite. For example, if a business were to purchase a vehicle on credit, it would still show the vehicle as an asset in the books, even though it does not own it yet (note that the business does control the vehicle, and this makes it an asset).

Non-current assets

Distinguishing between non-current and current assets is relatively simple. An easy way to remember the difference is to look at the potential inflows of cash. If the asset is expected to be in the possession of the business for longer than a year before it is disposed of, then it will be regarded as a non-current asset. A good example is that of a fixed deposit. An investment that is expected to mature within one year should be listed as a current asset. An investment that is expected to mature after one year should be listed as non-current.

Land and buildings

Think of reasons why there would be a land and buildings account in the books of the business in the event of the business renting their premises. We would only have a land and buildings account in the books if we were to be the landlord over the property. This is an asset, since it is a resource controlled by the business that could be sold for cash. Since the premises will be occupied by the business for longer than one year, it is regarded as non-current in nature.

Machinery, equipment and furniture

The concept of grouping has been applied here. In accounting we group similar items together into collective accounts. For example, office chairs, tables and couches would all be grouped together as ‘office furniture’. Computers bought will be debited to the ‘equipment’ account. You could also discuss the difference between machinery and equipment. Remember that machinery always refers to something that is used to manufacture a product.

Vehicles

This is a good example of an account to use in revisiting the definition of an asset. ‘An asset is a resource controlled by the firm….’. A business does not have to necessarily own a delivery vehicle to regard it as an asset. It could merely purchase it on credit; as long as it is in the business’s control, the vehicle will be brought into the books as a non-current asset.

Long-term investments

These are investments with a distant maturity date (at least one year from now). Long-term investments are not expected to mature within one year.
