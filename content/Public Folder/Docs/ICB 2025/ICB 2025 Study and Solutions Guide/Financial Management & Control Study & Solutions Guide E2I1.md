(v) Accuracy
Information must be correct and truthful. Inaccurate management information could result in
management making the wrong decisions, which could affect the business negatively. Accurate
management information is typically a result of accurate data collection.
Complete
Management information must be complete in order to aid management in their decision-making.
Incomplete management information could result in the wrong decisions being taken. Complete
management information has to present all relevant and useful facts for a particular decision to be
made. Complete information is valuable because it gives a better representation of the situation
under consideration.
Reliable
Management information and its sources must be trustworthy, and management must be able to
rely upon that information. If management information cannot be relied upon, it is not good or
useful management information.
Timely
Management information should be available when it is needed in order for the necessary actions
to be taken. For example, a company in the same industry is for sale and it would be a fantastic
opportunity for an organisation to buy this company and embark on a new business venture.
However, management receives the news only after offers for the company have closed. Another
example could be information indicating that a production line is not performing as expected. If
there is a problem with one of the production lines, that problem has to be addressed as soon as
possible to ensure that productivity is not affected. It is of no use to get this information at a stage
when nothing can be done to recover any losses or at a stage when changes to the processes

are not an option. If information is only made available to management after a decision has been
made or too late for a decision to be made, the information is useless.
Relevant
Only information that is significant enough for management to base a decision on should be
included – unnecessary information should be omitted. For example, a report on the financial
position of the company may be relevant to management in planning for the following year (e.g.
budgeting for capital assets, or making decisions for investment purposes for the company). In
this instance it would be irrelevant for management to receive a report on sales statistics for the
month, for example.
Cost-effective
Information costs money, and as data is costly to collect, analyse and report, management has to
consider its cost-effectiveness. Management has to consider whether the information is
worthwhile obtaining. The cost of obtaining the information should be compared with the benefits
that will result in the information being available. It should never cost more to obtain information
than the benefit to be derived from its use.
Understandable
Information must be understood by the receiver so that he or she is able to interpret it correctly. If
management information is not understandable or is misinterpreted, this could lead to incorrect
decisions being taken, which could impact the company negatively. Information should be clearly
presented, unambiguous and properly communicated.
(vi) Planning
Planning is the primary function of management. It involves defining a course of action to achieve
the desired organisational objectives. Planning is the key to the success, stability and prosperity
of an organisation. Planning helps management to gain insight on future prospects and
hindrances that could affect the organisation.
Organising
Organising involves bringing together and implementing the resources that are needed to achieve
the objectives of the organisation.
Staffing
Staffing involves the recruitment, selection and development of personnel. Employees are the
most important asset of an organisation, therefore most companies have a department dedicated
to the human resources function.
Directing
Directing involves the guiding of the organisation and its employees, with the ultimate goal of
achieving the organisation’s objectives.
