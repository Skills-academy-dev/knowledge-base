Solution to Activity 1b
In order to gain access to a professional career in HRM it is essential to acquire some formal education,
training and development in the field of HRM. To become a successful Human Resource practitioner it
is important to be competent in the necessary knowledge, skills, orientations and attitudes required by a
Human Resource professional. A high level of skill in certain areas and different focus is needed to
become competitive in the market. The nature of a Human Resource career requires a Human
Resource professional to be increasingly involved in the core of the business, requiring in-depth
knowledge and understanding of aspects such as the economic environment, business economics,
including the concept of productivity, value-adding processes and marketing and the business activities
of the organisation. Must have the skills and abilities to conceptualise, resolve problems, negotiate,
mediate, facilitate, and communicate. A Human Resource professional must also have the ability to
think strategically and to be adaptable in a rapidly changing environment. Currently one can study a
national certificate and national diploma in HRM and it is approved in terms of the National
Qualifications Framework (NQF). HRM has become such a complex and demanding field of theory and
practice that true professionalism has come to be regarded as a major requirement for HRM in South
Africa and this emphasises the reason why a formal qualification is so essential to qualify as a Human
Resource professional. It is desirable to gain general business management experience in order to
understand the business of an organisation and the type of work in which an organisation’s human
resources are really involved.
Most tertiary institutions in South Africa offer courses in fields relevant to HRM. General social science
studies in the fields of psychology or sociology help to provide knowledge of human and behavioral
processes organisational contexts. Legal studies provide knowledge of relevant legal requirements
which have a bearing on the acquisition, utilisation and separation of the organisation’s human
resources.
It will be to your advantage if you are employed and work in a Human Resource field, since you will
gain the relevant experience and apply the theoretical knowledge gained through your studies.
Associations you can approach for membership after completion of your studies are the South African
Board for Personnel Practice. Their aim is to promote the profession of personnel practice in South
Africa, as well as the standard of education and training of persons engaged in personnel practices.

(a)
Human Resource planning is influenced by external and internal factors.
Internal factors could include:
• Enterprise objectives in the form of growth and expansion programmes or entering
new markets
• Change in the structure of the enterprise to incorporate new technologies or new
management policies
• An assessment of staff quality which can be developed to provide the skills that the
enterprise will need for the future
External factors could include:
• The prevailing of the economic climate, which influences consumer spending, higher
consumer spending results in the expansion of opportunities, making the need for
new recruits more likely.
• Technological advances, which provide enterprises with strategic advantages over
local and global competitors.
• Skilled labour, which is costly and in demand in South Africa and requires advanced
planning to ensure its availability.
• Government regulations, including employment equity, the Skills Development Act
and Skills Levy Act which influences recruitment.
• Trade union agreements concluded through collective bargaining with the unions,
which also influence recruitment.

Step 2: Analysis of current staff. Current staff needs to be audited, in order to determine the
HR supply within the organisation. Analyse the strengths and weaknesses of the current
workforce, and supply factors within the labour market. The supply analysis is done to
determine what skills are currently present in the organisation and what internal and
external factors will affect the supply of Human Resource in the future. Assessment of
strengths and weaknesses when doing a supply analysis is important, and avoiding a pure
headcount approach should be avoided. Rather examine how jobs can be restructured and
designed better, for example if jobs are redesigned to include diversity issues such as age,
gender and race.
Step 3: Human Resource forecasting. Determine the gaps that need to be addressed.
Where is there a skills shortage? Compare the information from the supply and demand
analysis to identify the differences between the current workforce and the workforce
needed in the future. Identify shortfalls and surpluses in staffing levels, anticipated
surpluses or shortfalls of skills. Also identify which job functions and skills will no longer be
required, what current skills exist that meet future requirements.
Step 4: Strategy formulation. Make the necessary plans to address the needs that emerge
from the gap analysis. Develop an action plan with deadlines to address the differences in
the workforce requirements you have identified in step 3. Include actions that are clear,
concise, and measurable, including achievable milestones agreed with the employees
involved.
Examples of actions you can include in your action plan may include:
• Recruitment of external resources.
• Contracting or outsourcing of work.
• Job design.
• Training plans.
• Succession planning.
Once you have action plans mapped out, the next step is to formulate these into an overall
workforce strategy. When you develop the workforce strategies consider the following:
• Create opportunities to improve the effectiveness of recruitment programmes.
• Explore alternatives to the recruitment of permanent staff, such as outsourcing work
or using contract employment.
• Examine the cost-effectiveness of contracting or outsourcing programmes.
• Explore training and development programmes and opportunities for filling gaps.
• Gear the design of performance management systems to provide information that
could be relevant to workforce planning programmes.
• Introduce or review retention strategies.
• Review techniques to make better use of available skills.
• Implement career development programmes, including promotions, transfers and
reassignments.

Step 5: Implementation phase. Implement the plans to make the transition from the present
workforce to the future workforce requirements. Monitor the implementation of the plans
against timelines you have agreed on. Review actual costs incurred against the budget
projections and changes which occur in terms of cost allocations. Incorporate necessary
consultations with stakeholders and interest groups, including employee representative
forums and unions within the planned time frames.
Step 6: Human Resource audit, the evaluation and review. In this step you need to conduct
an audit to review:
• Policies, procedures and systems
• Human Resource practices and systems
• Statutory compliance
• Existing action plans
• Accountability and responsibilities
A Human Resource audit will determine what actions are required to develop a short,
medium or long term HR action plan. There are three methods you can use to do a Human
Resource audit:
• Use a checklist or questionnaire.
• Identify areas of concern by asking questions such as: ‘Do we comply with all
applicable legislation?’ Or ‘Have we had a change of staff that could influence the
organisation’s HR plan?’
Conduct review sessions to ascertain any problem areas. An example may be that you hold
a review session with your production department to determine:
• If they have any problems regarding Human Resource practices
• Their concerns regarding Human Resource Management
• What they consider to be priorities in human resources
You need to develop an action plan based on the results of your audit. The workforce
planning process is a continues process of forecasting, planning, assessing the impact of
changes introduced and revising the plans that were tailored to meet the changing needs of
employees and strategies within the organisation. The process must be continuously
evaluated to assess what has been successful and where adjustments are needed.
