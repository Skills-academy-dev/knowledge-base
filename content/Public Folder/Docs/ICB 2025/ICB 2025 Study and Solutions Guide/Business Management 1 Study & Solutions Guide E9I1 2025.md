Practice exercise 1a

First-line managers:

• Maintain general discipline in a business unit or department. For example, they ensure that staff do the work assigned to them and that staff do not disrupt the overall harmony in the workplace.

• Ensure that targets are met within a business unit or department.

• Make decisions (such as giving discounts) within specific guidelines that the first-line manager received from their seniors.

• Advise staff on how to do things better and more quickly.

• Assign tasks to workers so that the flow of work can be optimised.

• Provide an insight to seniors regarding the job performance of workers who report to the first-line manager.

Middle managers:

• Traditionally, middle managers receive specific instructions or goals from top managers, and mid- dle managers have to ensure that workers and supervisors achieve those targets.

• Middle managers have a semi-executive role. They are no longer responsible for detailed work, but they are responsible for the implementation of business strategies.

• Middle managers do not manage workers. They manage those who supervise the workers.

• Middle managers depend on the results of others (at lower levels) to achieve goals.

• Middle managers motivate and inspire others to achieve goals, but they do not necessarily control and organise work.

• Middle managers are sometimes referred to as ‘paper pushers’ because they receive reports from first-line managers. These reports have to be repackaged to create specific reports that top management wants.

Top managers:

• Designing an organisation’s strategy. They must also make sure that the strategy is communicated to:

▪ the board of directors; and

▪ the whole organisation.

• Sourcing operating capital for the organisation.

• Drafting budgets so that income and expenditures can be tracked.

• Finding the right people who can add the right value to the organisation.

• Communicating matters relating to the organisation’s performance to shareholders and other stakeholders.

• Establishing and maintaining relationships with shareholders.

• Ensuring compliance with regulations.

• Ensuring that the organisation’s code of conduct is followed.

• Guiding the organisation through difficult times.

• Developing staff potential. For example, a worker may show all the attributes of a good leader. It would be the responsibility of top management to train and educate that worker to fulfil a manage- rial role.

• Developing and implementing business continuity plans.

• Top managers have the additional responsibility to ensure that the organisation remains sustainable over a long period of time.

• Plan

Planning refers to an intention to do something. It also refers to the drafting of proposals, which detail how things need to be done. Most plans are future-oriented and describe how organisations will meet

specific goals at specific times. Planning is important for management to stay focused and to ensure that they know where the organisation is going.

• Decide

Deciding on something is an intention to act, and has usually been considered for some time. Typically, decisions follow after many facts and options are considered by the decision-maker. Decision-making is an important tool to put words into action.

• Organise and delegate

Organising refers to systematically arranging things or coordinating activities of people as efficiently as possible in a business. In other cases, organising refers to making preparations or arrangements for something that is going to occur at some point in the future. Organising is important to ensure that the resources available to management is used efficiently and that responsibility can be allocated to different individuals.

To delegate means that one entrusts a task or responsibility to someone else. In other words, the act of delegation is an authorisation to do something on behalf of someone else. Managers normally delegate tasks from a higher level to a lower level. Delegation is important to ensure that all tasks get done, as one person cannot be held responsible for doing everything. It also serves as a training opportunity for inexperienced staff.

• Lead

Leading refers to actions taken by seniors that will inspire juniors to behave in a certain way. Leading also means that seniors are guiding others to take certain actions, which will generate results that the seniors want; and it means being in charge of something. Lastly, leading can refer to an act to organise and direct something (such as a meeting). Leadership is important to illustrate to employees what the organisation stands for and how it is expected of them to behave. It also shows them the way through times of uncertainty.

• Motivate

To motivate someone means to provide a reason for doing something. It is important for management to motivate employees to ensure that productivity is high and that staff morale is kept at an optimum level.

• Communicate

Communication is about the sharing of information between people. Organisations make use of different modes of communication such as oral, written, visual and non-verbal communication. Management should communicate properly to ensure that employees always know what is expected of them and what is happening in the organisation.

• Control

Control refers to the power of managers to influence or direct people’s behaviour over time. It also refers to the ability to take into account factors that could influence an organisation positively or negatively. Finally, control refers to a manager’s ability to deal with situations successfully. Through controls, organisations can create a sense of order and structure, which aids in the achievement of optimum results.

Instead of appointing specialist managers, companies can make use of consultants to serve the same purpose. Consultants work across a range of different industries and specialities, which means that they can bring more experience and greater perspective to companies who have to solve specific problems. A manager working for a specific company may be narrow-minded in his or her thoughts, while a consultant is objective and may bring more creativity to the table.

Feedback on Integration task 3.1

• Obtaining finance

Your business plan will show how much money is needed from external financiers, what it is needed for and when and for how long it is required. Numerous new businesses fail due to a lack of cash flow, a risk that can be reduced with proper planning by means of a business plan. A good business plan is no guarantee that sufficient finance will be provided. It will, however, help you display your understanding of the financial dynamics of your business to the full and to communicate your ideas to stakeholders in a way that will be easier for them to understand and to appreciate the reasoning behind your ideas. Once they know what you are trying to do, they will be in a better position to help and advise you.

Testing your ideas

The systematic approach to planning that a business plan offers enables you to make mistakes on paper, rather than in the market place. One entrepreneur might find, while gathering data for the cash flow forecast of his/her business plan, that if he/she delayed purchasing some machinery and equipment, and leased others, this could substantially decrease the initial funding requirements.

(i) Give a brief description of what you intend with your business or explain your business idea in broad terms. Explain why you have chosen your product or service and how it has been developed. Feedback on Integration task 3.4

The use of capital budgeting in investment appraisals has been with us for over 40 years. The capital budgeting procedure works well when cash flows are certain, but in the real world it is often very hard to predict cash flows. Since the whole model is based on assumptions of the future, the results are often fraught with error. Learning example 10 B shows how crucial calculations of cash flows from projects are for capital budgeting decisions. Consequently, capital budgeting decisions can only be as accurate as cash flow projections. The temptation to ‘shade’ cash flow projections to ‘make the numbers work’ is a practice that can have dire consequences for any enterprise. Of course, if the cash flow projections and the calculation of the cost of capital are accurate, this model is very powerful.
