(i) The following three definitions have been cited:

Strategic management can be described as the identification of the purpose of the organisation and the plans and actions to achieve that purpose (Lynch, 2009: 5).

Strategy is the game plan indicating the choices a manger needs to make, for example how to attract and meet customer needs, how to compete successfully, how to grow the organisation, how to manage the organisational architecture and develop dynamic capabilities, how to achieve performance targets by implementing strategies successfully (Thompson and Strickland, 2005: 3).

Strategic management is the process whereby all the organisational functions and resources are integrated and coordinated to implement formulated strategies (Ehlers et al., 2007: 2).

The importance of strategic management in modern-day management can be summarised as follows:

Traditionally, management activities include planning, organising, decision-making negotiating, training, controlling, conflict resolution, rewarding, evaluating, and innovation. But management is more than this, and it involves continuous change. Strategic management is important because it helps in setting detailed goals, analyses all internal and external resources of a business, and analyses a business’s external environment. Like most people, you have probably worked somewhere during your life as a student or as a practitioner, even if only part-time. You might have held jobs in retail sales, fast-food chains, restaurants, or the construction industry. During this time, did you feel as if the organisation had some sort of strategic plan or success? If so, did you understand how your specific job was integrated into the organisation’s big plan? For example, if you worked as a waiter in a restaurant, was your objective to get the customers in and out quickly, or was it to encourage a long eating cycle with appetizers and before-and after-dinner drinks? In considering such questions, you can begin to understand the importance of strategic planning in the management process. Strategic planning is the process by which a system maintains its competitiveness within its work environment by determining where the organisation is, where it wants to go, and how it wishes to get there.

World trade continues to be dominated by ‘the Big Four’:

As we discussed in the first unit, international trade has gained in prominence, and indeed the way we go about it has changed irrevocably. Share in world trade can be grouped or classified in terms of the headings below.

By country

The Big Four currently dominate international trade, but global business patterns tend to change and evolve quickly. There are many reasons a country’s share in international trade changes. Some of these reasons concern the competitive advantage or position of a country. If a country is at the forefront of technology or even manufacturing, it will tend to hold a greater share of international trade due to the basic principles of demand. A prime example of this is Japan.

By level of economic development

Share of international trade may also be classified according to the country’s degree of economic development. We differentiate between the following:

• Less-developed countries e.g. Chile, Argentina, Kenya, Bulgaria, Romania

• Newly industrialised countries e.g. Brazil, China, India, Malaysia, Turkey

It is logical, to some extent, that the higher the degree of economic development, the higher the share of international trade will be.

Practice exercise 3c

To draw up a comparative analysis, countries may be ranked according to their gross national product (GNP). These figures are easily accessible, enabling a fair and simple method of ranking or classifying. The most common currency used to ensure the most reliable comparison is the US dollar. Still, some discrepanicies can slip in due to the continually changing exchange rates and errors made in currency conversions.

Why use GNP? In general, a country with a higher GNP tends to supply more market opportunities than a country with a lower GNP. This implies there exist more business opportunities for international or global trade.

Another – and more reliable – classification tool is that of per-capita GNP. Per-capita GNP takes the number of people living in a country into consideration, which enables a more precise measurement of the actual buying power in each country. This enables us to differentiate between higher-income and lower-income countries.

• It has an underperforming or weak communications structure.

Lesser-developed countries are not a popular choice for investment due to the reasons listed above. If a country is currently classified as lesser developed, it does not mean that it will remain in this classification group because change and economic growth can be facilitated through foreign financial aid (for example).

Newly industrialised countries

Newly industrialised countries include Indonesia and Taiwan. They offer many business opportunities, because their development tends to be fast, especially in markets for industrial and consumer products. They are also very aggressive in their approach to business, which is allowed by their rapid economic development. They are known to grab market share from other existing competitors and even take over market share in highly industrialised countries. Newly industrialised countries are also a popular choice for the establishment of a production or manufacturing plant due to their low cost base for production.

Practice exercise 3e

The balance of trade is the relationship of exports to imports. A favourable balance of trade or trade surplus occurs when the value of exports exceeds that of imports. An unfavourable balance of trade or trade deficit occurs when the value of imports exceeds that of exports. It is easy to understand why countries prefer to export more than they import. For example, if I sell you R 200 worth of goods and buy only R 100 worth, I have an extra R 100 available to buy other goods and services. However, I am in an unfavourable position if I buy R 200 worth of goods and sell only R 100.

The balance of payments is the difference between money flowing into a country (from exports and investments by foreigners in South African companies) and money leaving the country (for imports and divestments by foreigners or South Africans from South African holdings) plus money flows from other factors such as tourism, foreign aid and military expenditures. The amount of money flowing into or out of a country for tourism and other reasons may offset a trade imbalance. The goal is always to have more money flowing into the country than flowing out of the country. This is called a favourable balance of payments. The USA and South Africa have unfavourable balance of payments compared to Germany and China who have sizeable positive balance of payments.

There are five major advantages of trade bloc agreements:

• Foreign direct investment

• Economies of scale

• Competition

• Trade effects

• Market efficiency

Foreign direct investment

An increase in foreign direct investment results from trade blocs and benefits the economies of participating nations. Larger markets are created, resulting in lower costs to manufacture products locally.

Economies of scale

The larger markets created via trading blocs permit economies of scale. The average cost of production is decreased because mass production is allowed.

Competition

Trade blocs bring manufacturers in numerous countries close together, resulting in greater competition. Accordingly, the increased competition promotes greater efficiency within firms.
