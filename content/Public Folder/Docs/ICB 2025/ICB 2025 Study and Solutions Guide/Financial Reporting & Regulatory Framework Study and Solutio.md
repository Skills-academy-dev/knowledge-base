• Provision for losses;

4 Accrual accounting Accrual accounting requires that financial statement elements are recognised when they meet the definition and recognition criteria. Hence, income and expenses will be matched and dealt with in the same accounting period (e.g. depreciation of a non-current asset). If the going concern does not hold, then the accrual basis cannot hold. For example, a company has a highly specialised machine that cost R 8 000, a current carrying value of R 4 000 and can be used for a further two years. As the machine is highly specialised, it cannot be sold and has a nil (0) market value.

If the going concern assumption holds, the machine will be carried at R 4 000 (cost less depreciation) as it still meets the definition of an asset (generating future economic benefits for a further two years).

If the going concern assumption does not apply, the machine will carried at its market value of nil (0) Rand.

Solution to Question 1g

Useful financial statements contain the two fundamental qualitative characteristics (i.e. relevance and faithful representation).

To be reliable, financial statements must faithfully represent an entity's underlying transactions and other events, i.e. transactions must be accounted for and presented in accordance with their substance and economic reality rather than their legal form.

For example, if an entity sold an asset to a third party, but continued to enjoy the benefits of its use, then this transaction would not be represented faithfully by recording it as a sale (it is probably a lease/ financing transaction).

Features that provide an indication that the substance of a transaction differs from its legal form:

• Control differs from the ownership of the asset;

• Assets are ‘sold’ at prices that are greater or less than their fair values;

• Use of options as part of the agreement;

• Existence of a series of ‘linked’ transactions.

Solution to Question 1i

Financial information has the characteristic of relevance when it has a predictive value, confirmatory value or both. Financial information has a predictive value if it can be used by users as an input in processes used to predict future outcomes. Financial information has confirmatory value when it provides feedback about past evaluations and enables users to correct or improve their processes used to make their predictions. The predictive value and confirmatory value of financial information are interrelated. Information with predictive value can also simultaneously have confirmatory value.

To have a predictive value, information need not necessarily be in the form of a forecast. Forecast information has the disadvantage in that it does not have a strong confirmatory value and it will invariably be based on management expectations about the future, which are not necessarily accurate or decision- useful.

The presentation of historical actual information, as opposed to estimated forecast information, allows the user to confirm past evaluations and, when compared on a period-by-period basis, can be used to identify trends and form an assessment about future performance.

Solution to Question 1p

In South Africa, the financial statements of most companies are prepared under IFRS. The disclosures required by IFRS can be complex and detailed and many users will not be able to fully understand what the disclosures are trying to communicate. For example, the disclosure of derivative financial instruments can involve detailed rules which can be confusing and difficult to understand. Management’s decision- making processes require the availability of timely information on matters that are not necessarily incorporated in financial statements. Hence, financial statements may be of limited use to management. The monetary values contained in financial statements can be different or mixed in nature. Some transactions and balances are based on historic cost and others are at fair value. Without detailed knowledge of the underlying processes used to derive these values, their interpretation can be difficult.

Financial statements present the result of historic transactions and events and not on what is going to happen. Hence, they cannot be used to accurately predict the future performance or position of an entity. Many items are excluded from the financial statements. For example, internally-generated intangible assets (such as brands) cannot be recognised in the statement of financial position. Management, in preparing financial statements, can be creative in how information is presented. The use of significant judgements and estimates means that some information is subjective in nature. For a listed company, the value associated with market perception cannot be recognised in the financial statements. The market value of a company (being the share price multiplied by the number of share in issue) is different from the carrying value in the financial statements as the market value reflects shareholder expectations of future returns.

Financial statements are drawn up at a specific point in time. The point of cut-off needs to be established in order to prepare the financial statements. This point of cut-off could be in the middle of very detailed or complex transactions which the financial statements may not be able to fully reflect.

Solution to Question 1q

(i) The Conceptual Framework is not an IFRS and does not overrule any individual IFRS.

Where a conflict arises between an IFRS and the Conceptual Framework, the IFRS must take precedence.

(ii) Fundamental qualitative characteristics: Relevance and Faithful representation. Enhancing

qualitative characteristics: Comparability, Verifiability, Timeliness, Understandability.

(iii) Financial statements will normally be prepared on a going concern basis. Going concern is the assumption that an entity will continue in operation for the foreseeable future. The entity has neither the intention nor the need to liquidate or materially curtail (cut back) the scale of its operations.

If such an intention were to exist, the financial statements would need to be prepared on a different basis whereby the net assets are measured and disclosed at their liquidation values.

In either circumstance, an entity must disclose what basis has been used in the preparation of the financial statements.

(iv) Recognition criteria: An item is recognised if it:

• it meets the definition of an element; and

• provides to the users of financial statements relevant and faithfully represented information about the element.
