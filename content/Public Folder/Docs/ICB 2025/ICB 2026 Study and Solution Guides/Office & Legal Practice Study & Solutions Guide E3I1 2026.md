(i) Visitors should always be received in a warm and courteous manner. The secretary should
ensure the visitor is comfortable, particularly, if they have to wait for any length of time. Where
visitors may have traveled a distance they should always be advised where the restroom facilities
are to be found. The secretary should take the initiative here and not wait to be asked. It may also
be appropriate to offer to take their coats, bags or cases. The secretary should offer the visitors
coffee or tea.
(ii) This is something secretaries need to clarify with their respective managers. It needs to be
established in a clear precise way what will the guidelines be in respect of visitors arriving without
the necessary appointment. A company policy should actually stipulate that if a visitor has not
made an appointment they will not be able to see a person at the company. There might be the
occasional exception to the rule, but it will be largely up to the secretary. The role of the secretary
is to ‘protect’ the boss from unwanted intrusion. Experience will help the secretary develop the
ability to strike an effective balance and developing a sixth sense for what or who is and is not
important.
(iii) Effective delegation is a key factor in making organisations work and yet delegating effectively
can present problems for personnel at all levels. Delegation does not simple mean passing the
responsibility for an aspect of the work to someone else in a junior position. As well as the
responsibility for the task or duty, the junior must have the authority to match that responsibility
and yet the delegator must be the person who is ultimately accountable for the success or failure
of the work.
(iv) Not all tasks can be delegated and it is important to select things which are appropriate and which
fall within the capabilities of junior staff.
(v) A secretary must possess the following qualities and attitudes to ensure effective working
relationships:
• Common sense.
• Patience and tolerance.
• Adaptability to change and circumstances.
• A sense of humour.

Feedback on Integration task 2.2
(i) Ill-mannered visitors are found everywhere in society, and the business world is no exception.
People sometimes abuse the idea that ‘the client is always right’, as they know that they as
clients have power over a receptionist. Do not be upset if a client bursts the reception area and
wrongly accuses or insults you. Try to ignore any aggressive or foul language the visitor might
use. Remain calm and behave professionally by being polite but firm. If you persist in being polite,
it is sure to have an influence on the behaviour of the visitor. The fact that you are able to remain
calm and courteous in such a situation will make a positive impression on the visitor and will help
to calm them down.
(ii) Curious or spying visitors are always trying to gain access to information. They will be extremely
friendly and easy to talk to, and will try to make the receptionist like them. Be alert. They may be
so charming that you might not even be aware that they are leading you into divulging
information. You should guard your tongue, as visitors who are interested in industrial espionage
can be clever at obtaining information from people. To ensure that you do not fall into this trap, it

is important not to talk of business matters to any visitors. A good policy is to keep documentation
in files and not open on your desk where people can read it. If you are busy with documents when
a visitor comes to your desk you should politely turn the documents face down or cover them.
(iii) Talkative visitors are demanding and can be tiresome. Do not show impatience with this kind of

relevant waiting area where they can read while waiting for their appointment. You should also

(iv) Visitors who have to sit and wait need to feel welcome. Never give them the impression that they
are interfering with your work. If they have to wait because they did not have an appointment,
then you need not apologise, but you should still make them feel welcome. In all cases, visitors
should be put at ease and should feel that they are important to the organisation. Any person who
has to wait should be able to do so in the comfort of the waiting area, which should meet the
following criteria:
• The area must be neat, clean and tastefully decorated at all times.
• Current reading material that will cover the interests of different readers should be made
available. Magazines should be in good condition, and there should be none that may
cause offence.
• A kitchenette or vending machine is ideal for serving refreshments to visitors who have to
wait.
• The area should be close to cloakrooms, which should be kept to the highest standards of
hygiene.
• Any pot plants and fresh flowers in the area should be well kept.
(v) Visitors with a complaint should be handed as follows:
• All clients are assets to the organisation, because it costs time and money to build up a
clientele.
• The principle of ‘the client is always right’ is important. All complaints should receive
attention, because they indicate that there are problems.
• When a client states their complaint, they might be impatient and aggressive. Try to remain
calm and listen attentively. Do not take any accusations personally and do not get involved
in an argument.
• If the client has a valid complaint you should apologise and find a solution, or refer the
client to somebody who can help to solve the problem.
• Thank the client for making the complaint and assure them that it will receive attention.
• Make sure that you do not refer any angry client to the wrong person, as this will aggravate
the matter and cause more anger.
• If there is nobody to whom you can refer the client, apologise and try to solve the problem
yourself.
• If the matter cannot be solved immediately, promise the client that you will have it
investigated and that you will inform them of the outcome. Do not forget to do exactly what
you promised.
• If a matter is settled satisfactorily, you have secured the goodwill and loyalty of the client.

(vi) Beggars or donation collectors should not be allowed into the reception area. If there is no system
in place to keep such persons out, the receptionist should be firm and not give anything to these
people, as this will simply encourage more such visits.
(vii) Marketing and sales representatives
As a receptionist, you should know the senior management's preferences in dealing with
representatives, especially if they deal in sales or production. It is best to follow these
suggestions:
• Do not send away people who could introduce the organisation to new products or
developments.
• If a representative arrives without an appointment, make certain that the relevant
department is the correct place for them to visit. Once that is done, you should find out if
that senior manager is the right person to refer them to.
• Find out if the senior wants to see the representative, or make an appointment for a more
convenient time.
• If nobody is interested in seeing the representative, they could be asked to leave a
business card for future enquiries.
(viii) Visitors that wish to apply for a position within the organisation, can be divided into three groups.
• Those applying for a vacancy
If there is a vacancy in the organisation, it is normally advertised in a variety of ways, such as
through the newspaper or magazines, or even on the internet. Interested persons should call for
application forms and information. You should be well informed about the vacancy and have all
relevant information so that you can answer enquiries properly. You should treat these visitors
well, as they may become future colleagues. It is not your duty to judge applicants, but to accept
their documents and to give information.
• Those enquiring whether a vacancy exists
Job seekers who are not answering advertisements may come to the organisation to make
enquiries. In this day and age, jobs are not easy to find and these people should be treated well.
They can be invited to complete an application form and attach any relevant documents which
they may want to provide, such as their curriculum vitae or a summary of their work experience.
All these documents should be filed or forwarded to the personnel department.
• Those leaving their curriculum vitae
These visitors are also job seekers but are more prepared than the visitor who merely makes
enquiries about vacancies. These visitors should be dealt with in exactly the same way as the
visitors enquiring whether a vacancy exists.

(ix) You should treat any visitor who is not feeling well with sympathy. You are not expected to have
knowledge of nursing, but you should know how to handle such situations.
It is suggested that you use these guidelines in such a situation:
• Keep emergency numbers and a well-equipped first aid kit readily available.
• Do a first aid course.
• Before offering medication to visitors, make sure that they are not suffering from heart

(x) If visitors are injured while on the premises, give them sympathy and assistance. They may
decide to make a claim against the organisation. Compile a full report with all details about the
occurrence, such as the date, the circumstances that led to the injury, the time at which the injury

(xi) If a senior does not want to see a visitor, for whatever reason, the visitor should be informed
immediately in a polite but firm manner. Do not tell lies simply to get rid of the visitor. If there is
another person the visitor could see, they should be assisted to do so. The visitor must not be
made to feel unwelcomed at any time and they must leave the office with a positive image of the
organisation.
(xii) Should a visitor arrive at your office and demand to see the senior and no one else, it is best to
deal with such visitors as follows:
• Determine the reason for the visit.
• If the senior is the correct person, find out whether they can see the visitor.
• If the senior can see the visitor immediately, take them to the senior’s office.
• If the visitor has a complaint or problem and the senior cannot see them immediately, try to
find a solution yourself, especially if the visitor is difficult or bad-mannered.
• If the senior cannot receive the visitor, you should say so tactfully and suggest that an
appointment is made for a suitable time.
• If there is someone else who may be able to help the visitor, suggest that the visitor sees
this person rather than the senior.
• If the visitor insists on seeing the senior, suggest that you write down the complaint or
matter, so that the senior can reply in time.
• The reply could be made telephonically or by e-mail, which would save time for both the
visitor and yourself.

(v) Non-verbal communication is usually understood as the process of communication through
sending and receiving wordless (mostly visual) messages between people. Messages can be
communicated through gestures and touch, by body language or posture, by facial expression
and eye contact. Non-verbal messages could also be communicated through material
exponential; meaning, objects or artifacts (such as clothing, hairstyles or architecture). Speech
contains non-verbal elements known as paralanguage, including voice quality, rate, pitch, tone,
volume, and speaking style, as well prosodic features such as rhythm, intonation, and stress.
Likewise, written texts have non-verbal elements such as handwriting style, spatial arrangement
of words, or the physical layout of a page. However, much of the study of non-verbal
communication has focused on face-to-face interaction, where it can be classified into three
principal areas: environmental conditions where communication takes place, physical
characteristics of the communicators, and behaviours of communicators during interaction.
