Chapter 1: Introduction to Income Tax

Solution to Exercise 1A

(i) The purpose of the Income Tax Act is to provide the rules and regulations regarding income tax and various other taxes. The Act ensures that income tax is a legal obligation for all persons (i.e. natural and legal) who earn income, and it regulates the amount of tax payable and how and when tax payments must be made.

(ii) The following four taxes are also governed by the Income Tax Act:

1\. Turnover tax 2. Donations tax 3. Dividends withholding tax 4. Withholding tax

(iii) False. The calculation of net profit and taxable income differ, because they are calculated using different rules. Net profit is an accounting concept that is calculated based on a business’s income and expenses, as shown in its income statement. On the other hand, taxable income is a tax concept that is based on rules in the Income Tax Act, and it is used to determine the amount of tax that a business must pay.

The difference between the calculation of net profit and taxable income can be seen in the following examples:

• Certain types of income, such as local dividends, are included in accounting income, but they are exempt from income tax and are therefore excluded from taxable income.

• Capital income, such as profits on the disposal of assets, may be fully included in the income statement, but only partially included in taxable income through the taxable capital gain.

• On the deduction side, certain expenses that are reflected in the income statement, such as fines, may not be allowed for tax purposes.

• There may be timing differences in how certain deductions are treated for tax, such as capital allowances that are calculated differently for tax purposes.

However, net profit remains an important starting point. Businesses first prepare their income statement in order to determine accounting profit, and then they make specific adjustments as required by the Income Tax Act in order to calculate taxable income.

5 Solution to Exercise 1D (i) Farhaan’s income is subject to the following types of tax under the South African tax system:

• Income tax: This is applied to his freelance earnings, as he is effectively a sole proprietor.

• Provisional tax: As a sole proprietor / self-employed individual, he is responsible for paying provisional tax, which is a biannual prepayment of annual income tax.

• Dividends withholding tax: The dividends that he received from a South African company are subject to a withholding tax.

(ii) The two main Acts governing the rules and regulations for income tax are the Income Tax Act and

the TAA.

The Income Tax Act outlines the rules and regulations for determining who is required to pay income tax, as well as how to calculate taxable income and tax payable. Without this Act, there would be no legal obligation for persons to pay tax on their earnings.

The purpose of the Tax Administration Act is to simplify the administrative provisions regarding income tax. The Act provides clear rules regarding the timing and format of returns, objections and appeals. It also covers non-compliance and non-submission, which helps to ensure better compliance by taxpayers with the income tax rules and regulations.

(iii) Farhaan’s freelance and dividends income will be included in his gross income as revenue receipts. However, the dividends income will be exempt from tax under the Income Tax Act, as it has already been subject to dividends withholding tax (i.e. the South African company has paid the tax to SARS before distributing the net amount to Farhaan).

The profit on disposal of design equipment will not be included in gross income, as this is a capital receipt. Instead, the taxable capital gain arising from this disposal will be included when calculating his taxable income.

Farhaan incurred R 120 000 in expenses in order to earn his freelance income. Of this amount, R 100 000 (R 120 000 less R 20 000) is considered non-capital in nature, and it will be allowed as a tax deduction. The remaining R 20 000, which relates to the purchase of computer equipment, is a capital expense, and it cannot be claimed in full. However, Farhaan is entitled to a capital allowance of R 6 000 on this equipment.

These expenses and capital allowance will reduce Farhaan’s taxable income.

(iv) The basic formula for the calculation of taxable is as follows:

Gross income – Exempt income – Allowable deductions + Taxable capital gain = Taxable income

Solution to Exercise 2E

(i) John Jackson is ordinarily a resident in South Africa. Therefore, John is a resident of the Republic (as defined), although he will spend most of the year outside of South Africa. Therefore, John is subject to taxation in South Africa on his worldwide receipts and accruals. He will be taxed on the profit that he makes on this Mozambique contract. No exemption is available to him in terms of section 10(1)(o), because he is self-employed and not an employee.

(ii) John’s nine employees are ordinarily residents in South Africa. Therefore, they are considered tax residents and are subject to tax in South Africa on their worldwide income. Therefore, the salaries that they earn while working in Mozambique are included in gross income. This means that the salaries that they earn while working in Mozambique must be included in their gross income and are taxable in South Africa, unless an exemption applies.

(iii) Section 10(1)(o)(ii) allows a tax exemption of up to R 1.25 million for foreign employment income, provided that all of the conditions in terms of this section are met. In order to qualify for this exemption an employee:

• must render their services outside South Africa;

• these services must be for or on behalf of an employer;

• must be outside of South Africa for more than 183 full days in total during a 12-month period;

• must be outside of South Africa for a continuous period that exceeds 60 full days during this 12- month period; and

(i) Gross income refers to the total amount, in cash or otherwise, that is received by or accrued to a taxpayer during a year of assessment, excluding receipts or accruals of a capital nature, unless they are included specifically by legislation.
