A kind, accommodating and welcoming voice over a phone builds rapport with a customer – if a
connection cannot be established, your customer may be inclined to seek assistance elsewhere. Be
courteous and build up a reputation for unmatched service, so that your customers always return to you.
1. Greet
Your greeting should be professional and pleasant. Be sure to include your and your company's name,
as well as your department's name, if applicable. It is good practice to end the salutation with a helpful
statement or question.
For example: Xhosa Teaching Solutions, you're speaking with Thando from the Research Department –
how may I be of assistance?

2. Listen
Listening to your customer is vitally important when it comes to telephone etiquette. Make sure that you
listen to what is said, as well as what the customer wants or intends. In most cases, the customer will tell
you what they require straightaway. Once you have discerned what the customer has said, respond so
that the customer knows that you heard what they said.
For example:
Customer: Hi there, I am not sure how to replace the LED in my guitar pedal – could you help me out?
Service provider: Unfortunately, that isn't my area of expertise, but I can connect you with Zaaid in our
technical department. He is just finishing his conversation with another customer. Would you mind holding
the line for two minutes while I redirect your call and inform him that he has another customer on the line?
3. Empathise
This means considering your customer and looking at things from their point of view.
For example (continuing with the previous example):
Customer: Actually no, I really don't have much time this morning – besides, I called yesterday, and my
call was dropped. I really need help, and have been a loyal customer for years. You are making this
process really difficult for me.
Service provider: (Wait a second or two to be emphatic.) I'm so sorry about that, we don’t want this
process to be difficult for you. If you don’t have some time to spare right now, then I can arrange that
Zaaid calls you back at a more convenient time for you. What time would be ideal for you?
4. Probe
While this may take practise for some people, probing is necessary when it comes to helping customers
over the phone. Be direct and keep the basic open questions in mind (Who? What? When? Where?
How?). Using the phrase, ‘Could you tell me more about...’ goes a long way in trying to find out important
details.
For example, in the event that a customer cannot or will not wait any longer, offer to have a consultant
call them back when it is best for the customer. Commit to this, and make sure that the consultant makes
good on your promise. If the consultant fails to call the customer, then your business has lost credibility,
as well as possible future opportunities for business.
5. Common courtesies
Ask the caller whether you can put them on hold or not, and when you return, politely regain the caller's
attention. Have you ever been put on hold for a long time, causing you to feel frustrated and disregarded?
With this in mind, remember the following basic rule: refer to the customer by their name when you come
back, and wait for them to respond before carrying on.

For example:
Service provider: May I place you on hold while I quickly check with a consultant?
Customer: No problem at all.

Service provider: Mrs Connelly? (Pause for response.) Thank you for your patience – it looks like we do

6. Avoid company jargon and rules
Every business has its own regulations and technical/business terms. These can confuse and irritate
customers who may not understand, or even care about, what your business can and cannot do for them
and the reasons why. Customers do not want to feel alienated, or that they are unimportant/insignificant
to the business. If they do feel this way, it may drive them toward competitors and affect your company's
bottom line
7. Offer solutions or alternatives
If you are unable to carry out what the customer needs, inform them of what you can actually do for them.
In most cases, customers will be open to other options, but if you do not put these options on the table,
they will be none the wiser.
8. Tone
A phone conversation does not take place in person, so it is important to keep your voice tone even,
positive and professional. Keep in mind that the customer's image of the person answering the call forms
the image they have of the business. Is the call impersonal and abrupt? Is it flat and uninterested? Is it
enthusiastic and energised? It is important that individuals with good communication and people skills
are hired to answer phones, dealing politely and professionally with customers.
9. Appreciation
Ensure that you thank the customer for their time and make them feel appreciated before they terminate
the call.
For example: Thank you for choosing Xhosa Teaching Solutions – we really appreciate your time and
business, Mrs Boi.

10. Go the distance
Always do your best to go above and beyond for your customers. Take a little extra time and energy to
leave a lasting, positive impact on your clients. For example, when looking at a customer's file, you realise
they are a longstanding customer, and that they recently moved houses. You could therefore offer to
send them a change of address card, or even send them a thank-you card for their years of valued
support. Act as an ambassador for your company, and help it to grow and thrive. Excellent telephone
service is one of the keys to creating a business that is famous for its attention to detail and genuine care
for its clients.
(ii) The key to dealing with a very upset caller is to try to understand their emotions and distress. In
essence, these kinds of callers have two problems: the issue that is troubling them, and the fact
that they are unhappy. Your main issue is the second problem. Giving your caller space to express
their unhappiness is a great start to helping them.
Step one
Give the caller time to vent – allow them to scream, swear, cry, or do whatever they want – and simply
listen graciously and patiently to what upsets them. Eventually, the caller will stop and listen to you,
because they will expect you to take care of their issue. It is unlikely that they will have anything else to
say, unless it is a response to any questions you may ask them.
Step two
Discuss the caller's problem, and put it into your own words if possible. This shows the caller that you
have listened and absorbed everything. Take the gravity and length of the caller's problem into account,
and do your best to appreciate their plight. While the issue may be minor to you, it may be a significant
problem for the caller, so treat the problem with the respect it deserves.
Step three
Do not apologise or say sorry unless the problem is a result of something you did or failed to do. If the
problem is not within your control or caused by you and/or your company, there is no need to apologise.
Besides, apologising fixes nothing and does not really help anyone.
Step four
Do your best to empathise with the caller – this is akin to patching up a wound, but in this case, it is an
emotional one. Showing, or trying to show, that you care and understand should calm the caller down.
Assure the caller that you will do your best to assist them. Completing this step will show the caller that
you do actually care about their issue, and they will usually respect you for this. If you do a very good job,
you may receive a special commendation or thank you for your excellent service and patience. However,
be prepared for instances in which the caller may still be upset with you and the business, due to being
upset that they had to deal the with the problem in the first place.

The recommended layout for a reception area
The following basic guidelines should be followed when laying out a reception area:
• There should be enough space for staff to move around easily while attending to visitors.
• Furniture and equipment should be effectively positioned to promote the efficiency of the staff.
• Cupboards and cabinets should be arranged against walls or pillars to avoid blocking the view and
light.
• Noise levels should be kept as low as possible, so that a professional atmosphere can be created.
• The layout in the office should allow the receptionist to see the front entrance clearly.
If the receptionist doubles as the management assistant, the following additional requirements should be
met:
• The management assistant’s office should be adjacent to that of the senior. This is to ensure that
the assistant and the manager can communicate easily and so that visitors can easily be seen
through to the manager’s office.
• The layout and decorations of the office must be tasteful, but simple.
• There must be a waiting area for visitors. This area should be visible to the management assistant
so that she can attend to visitors when necessary. There should be comfortable chairs and a variety
of current magazines. It is recommended to have the area air-conditioned.
(v) There are a number of qualities that distinguish a good receptionist from a mediocre one:
• A receptionist should be friendly and not intimidate visitors.
• A receptionist should be tactful and put people at ease, regardless of the circumstances.
• A receptionist should be discreet and not involve her or himself in office gossip.
• A receptionist must be a person with integrity, ethical values and honesty who can maintain
confidentiality.
• Good listening skills are an important attribute of a good receptionist.
• Loyalty is a very important quality and a receptionist should try and be loyal as far as possible
to both senior and junior staff members.
• A receptionist should be dependable and have good timekeeping skills.
• Irrespective of the circumstances a receptionist should remain composed under pressure.
• Good organisational skills are essential in being a good receptionist.
• A receptionist should have thorough knowledge of the organisation and the functions of the
people working there. This will enable the receptionist to direct calls to the correct persons.
• Receptionist must know and understand the policies and procedures of the organisation to
be able to assist customers with queries, for example on returns, refunds or terms and
conditions of sales agreements.
• A receptionist must be responsible and should take control of the surroundings in the
reception area. When promises are made to a customer they must be kept.
• A good receptionist will show initiative, and the ability to make decisions and take action
without referring it to others.
• The attitude of a receptionist is one of enthusiasm, being positive and caring.
• A receptionist should be well groomed, neat and tidy.
• Since they are often the first encounter with the company they should speak well and fluently.

Solution to Activity 1b
(i) Any five of the following practices would be considered correct.
• Make extensive use of filtering: Only emails that are sent directly to you should not be filtered.
Everything else – from organisational memos, to mailing list emails and emails that you have
been Cc'd on – must be filtered. This will prevent your inbox from becoming cluttered, and
you can decide whether you actually want to read certain emails or not.
• Filter particular senders out of the inbox: If one or more persons send you mails that are often
not relevant to you, redirect their mails to another folder. You can always review them at a
later stage if you so desire.
• Schedule a regular time to review your folders: Keep your folders organised, and have folders
for more critical mails, as well as less important ones. For example, informational mails and
memos do not require replies, and can be directed elsewhere. You can read these less critical
emails if you have the time and/or opportunity to do so.
• Read emails as a thread: As a general rule of thumb, the more ‘To’ and ‘Cc’ recipients an
email has, the more replies it gets. When viewed as a thread, you can see the conversation
and important information at the same time.
• Do not respond to all emails: This is particularly true if you are Cc'd on an email. Most emails
are relatively brief and quick to read, but many emails need to be replied to, and this can take
time. You should therefore only reply if you really have to; if you are Cc'd in, then you are
simply being informed.
• If an email cannot be replied to right now, transfer it to a ‘Reply’ folder: If you read an email
and cannot reply immediately, then flag it by transferring it into another ‘priority’ folder. Failing
to transfer the mail could cause you to forget, or have to spend more time looking for the
emails among your other messages.
• If an email cannot be immediately read, transfer it to a ‘Read’ folder: You may skim over a
mail, but not have time to read it in full at that moment. In this case, the mail should be
transferred to a priority folder labelled ‘To read’, or something similar. Ensure that you
schedule a time to read the email if it is, in fact, important and needs your attention.

(ii)
• Compose with care: Take the time to think of what you want to say before writing a message.
Moreover, avoid replies that are wordy and take a long time to get to the point, as well as
replies that are too short and unclear. Make sure that your reader clearly understands exactly
what you are communicating.
• Be mindful of your tone: Make sure that your tone is friendly and professional. The mail must
be easy to understand and almost impossible to misinterpret. It is important not to write an
email if you are upset or angry. If you are not sure whether your response is appropriate, get
someone you trust to review it first.
• Proof your email: Ensure that you have written everything you need to communicate. Also
note that an incorrectly spelled word or lack of punctuation can change the meaning of your
message.
• Keep it confidential: Never send private information in an email. This encompasses private
details like bank account and credit card details, as well as sensitive information such as
performance appraisals – these should be delivered personally and more formally. Emails
can be forwarded, so do not say anything that you do not want repeated to others.
• Corporate emails: Check your email again and try to imagine what your senior would say if
they read it. Email is hardly private, and a corporate business has the right to read your
communications in a professional environment. For personal emails, use Gmail or Yahoo
Mail.
• Do not shout: Refrain from writing in all caps. This is seen as shouting in the digital world.
• Check the ‘To:’ field before you send your email: It is very easy to accidentally input the
incorrect address, or to click on ‘Reply to all’ instead of just ‘Reply’. Being careful should help
you to avoid embarrassment and possible ridicule.
• Bcc, not Cc: If an email is being delivered to a group of people, use the Bcc field to keep the
emails of individuals private – do not accidentally give out another person's email address.
Many people want to keep their addresses private in order to avoid spam, viruses and
unsolicited emails. This is why Bcc exists: it allows multiple people to receive an email without
showing the addresses of other recipients. The only email your recipient will see is yours and
their own.
• Fonts, colours, bolding and italics: Avoid strange font colours, excessive bolding, unusual
fonts and/or italics. A heavily formatted email often translates in unpredictable ways on
different email platforms, and may deter recipients from acknowledging and reading your
message.
• No subject: Every email must have a subject. With so many emails being sent and received,
only those with strong, pertinent subject lines will be read. Your subject should therefore
explain what your email is about, and should encourage your recipient(s) to read your
message.

(vi) You will need an email address from which to send email , and you will need an address in order

Each separate network connected to the Internet uses its own email address that is issued by the
Internet service provider you choose to use. Your email program uses Internet mail access to
transfer the message from the mail server to your computer. It is essential that you know your
email address and the email address of the person to whom you want to send an email message.
Your email address is your user identity plus the domain name of the computer to which you
connect to gain access to the Internet, separated by ‘at’ types as @.
You can store frequently used email addresses in your computer on the email program and can
refer to it as your email ‘address book’ or ‘my contacts’.

(i) Answers may vary. The following serves as a guide.
A piece of mail can be regarded as suspicious when it:
• shows no evidence of a return address;
• is for a person who no longer works for your business;
• is received from an unfamiliar or strange source;
• is awkwardly shaped and oddly packaged;
• has instructions that state something like ‘confidential’, ‘private’ or ‘hand-deliver only to’;
• arrives unexpectedly, and is from a company/individual you know little about;
• is heavier or lighter than it should be with regard to its size;
• displays a city/province in the postmark that fails to correlate with the return address; or
• smells odd, is stained, is leaking something powdery and/or has wires or parts protruding
from it.
(ii) Answers may vary. The following serves as a guide
The following steps should be followed when a person encounters a suspicious substance from a
mail:
1. Stay calm and do not panic – most substances are not harmful at all.
2. If the package is leaking a substance, under no circumstances should you smell, touch or
taste the substance Put the mail or package down and vacate the immediate vicinity.
3. Contact your supervisor or a trusted senior.
4. Vigorously wash your hands with soap and warm water, if you have touched substances that
leaked from a package. Furthermore, stay in an isolated area away from the package/letter,
try not to venture far, and limit your contact with anyone else until management arrives to
investigate further.
