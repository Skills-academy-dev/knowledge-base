(ii) Suppose that, for some reason, the price of distilled water drops to R 10 per litre. This is exceptionally cheap, and many people will subsequently like to buy the product at this price. In our example,10 kilolitres are demanded at the price of R 10 (point A on the graph). However, at this price, there simply is not enough distilled water supplied to meet the demand. Remember the law of supply: the lower the price, the fewer producers will supply in the market. At a price of R 10, only 2 kilolitres of distilled water will be supplied to the market (point B on the graph). At this price there is thus a demand for 10 kilolitres, but a supply of only 2 kilolitres. There is thus an excess demand of 8 kilolitres. One could also say there is a shortage of the product.

The question now arises: What can be expected in the case of a shortage? Since there is simply not nearly enough distilled water for everybody at the low price, some of the potential buyers will be willing to offer a higher price to get hold of the product. Say, for example, some buyers are willing to offer R 20 per kilolitre. Not all the buyers who are willing to buy at R 10 are willing to buy at R 20: at this price, there is a demand for 8 kilolitres (point C). At this price, suppliers can produce more and are willing to supply 4 kilolitres (point D). There is thus still and excess demand, but at these prices it has decreased: the excess demand is now 4 kilolitres (i.e. the 8 kilolitres that are demanded minus the 4 kilolitres that are supplied).

However, because there is still a large excess demand, some buyers are willing to pay even more to get distilled water. Some of them are even prepared to pay R 30 per litre (we say that buyers ‘bid up’ the price to R 30).Together, the number of buyers who are willing to pay R 30 per litre demand 6 kilolitres of distilled water. When the price is R 30, more suppliers are willing to supply distilled water, and collectively the suppliers offer 6 kilolitres to the market. Supply and demand are now in equilibrium: at R 30, 6 kilolitres are demanded and 6 kilolitres are supplied. There is now also no excess demand, and no excess supply, and the price is set at R 30.

Of course, what may also happen is that the suppliers, who will see that many people are eager to get the product, may raise the price. Either the buyers or the sellers, or both of them together, can thus cause the price to increase.

We can thus draw the following conclusion:

When prices are low and there is excess demand or shortage, buyers will tend to bid up the price, and/or suppliers will raise the price, until it reaches the equilibrium point.

We have now seen what happens when prices start out at a lower point than the equilibrium point. What happens in the opposite situation, when prices are very high, i.e. much higher that the equilibrium point?

Of course, suppliers want to sell at as high a price as possible, and (in terms of the law of supply) the higher the prices, the more they are willing to supply. Let’s say, then, that suppliers decide that they will charge R 50 per litre of distilled water, and at this price they can supply 10 kilolitres (point F in the graph). However, very few buyers are willing to pay this price, because they simply cannot afford it. At R 50, only 2 kilolitres of distilled water are demanded (point G). Here we therefore have an excess supply, also called a surplus, of 8 kilolitres: 10 kilolitres are supplied, but only 2 kilolitres are demanded. Suppliers are thus left with a huge surplus of distilled water, and will make severe losses if they do nothing further about the situation. In such a case, suppliers tend to lower the price of the product, so

Exchange rate comparisons

Between 1921 and 2016, a period of 95 years, the cost in United States currency of a South African rand has fallen from $ 2.43 to marginally below $ 0.07. The movements from 1921 to 1949, when the level of $ 1,40 to the rand was established, occurred under circumstances that limited the South African authorities' influence on the exchange rate policy severely. With the exchange rate fixed at $ 1.40, these constraining circumstances continued until the Bretton Woods system came to an end in the early 1970s. In the subsequent 45 years, the South African authorities adopted various foreign exchange regimes, without the limitations from Bretton Woods rules. For most of this period, the rand has been floating with various degrees of official intervention – and has generally floated downward.

Figure 1.11: Rand vs. US dollar exchange rate

What explains the downward trend of the rand? There are a number of factors that have been presented to explain each movement, every day, month or year in the foreign currency market. Many of these analyses are quite valid. However, there is typically, among the various factors, one significant contributor. The main cause of the depreciation of the rand over the 95-year period is the existence of higher inflation in South Africa than its main trading partner countries, including the United States. Prices in South Africa are today approximately 70 times as high as in 1921. Over the same period, prices in the United States have only increased tenfold. Higher domestic inflation greatly explains the exchange rate weakness of the rand. The depreciation of the rand also benefited the country. A stronger rand could have meant that South African exports would have been priced out of the world markets.

Focusing on the last 57 years only, we will now compare South Africa's inflation rate with that of the United States, Germany and the United Kingdom. Up to about 1980, South Africa's inflation rate was roughly in line with those of the United States and the United Kingdom. However, local inflation has been generally high while price increases were firmly brought under control in the two overseas economies. This is the reason why the exchange rate of the rand has been under downward pressure. A positive development that should be highlighted is that the inflation differential has decreased because of disciplined monetary and fiscal policies, and currently stands at roughly 3 percentage points.

It becomes important to understand why South Africa had inflation. Some economists firmly believe that a rapidly expanding money supply causes inflation. Others feel that an expanding money supply merely accommodates inflation that was initiated by other factors. However, a process of inflation cannot be sustained unless accompanied by an excessive increase in the money supply. And of that South Africa has had its fair share. When the Reserve Bank opened its doors for business 80 years ago, the total value of notes and coin in South Africa was less than R 30 million. Between 1965 and 2018, it has been averaging R 33 billion. For wider monetary aggregates, roughly the same order of magnitude for growth as for notes and coin applies over the period. This thousand fold increase in the money supply could not be absorbed by the twentyfold increase in the quantity of goods and services produced over the same period, but largely fed inflation.

However, various other activities and processes in the economy also reflect the origins of inflation. Among these are excess demand as reflected by the output gap (i.e. the difference between potential and actual real gross domestic product), supply shortages, cost increases (i.e. increasing unit labour costs) and inflation expectations. Although it is not easy to provide empirical evidence of the precise magnitude of the economic costs of inflation, recent studies indicate that inflation is not generally helpful for countries in their efforts to promote economic growth. With so little to be gained from continuing inflation, and the unavoidable reduction in current national income because of it, the cumulative gains in future income should be sufficient motivation for governments to reduce inflation to levels where it no longer has an influence on the decisions that individuals and businesses make.
