Feedback on prior knowledge

Economists use graphs to illustrate the relationships between different quantities and qualities. To understand any theory about economics and to reason in economic terms, you must therefore be able to use graphs. Graphs are a very useful tool, because they enable one to see quite complicated relationships at a glance.

The first step in understanding graphs is to become familiar with what we call a ‘number line’. Such a line is shown in Figure A below.

Figure A

There are several things you should notice about the number line. As you can see in Figure A, there is a small line above the zero point, ‘0’, that is at right angles to the number line. This line divides the number line into two parts. The part to the right of the zero point contains positive numbers, and is called the positive segment of the line. The part to the left of the zero point contains negative numbers (‘minus’ numbers, like -5), and is called the negative segment. Each number represents a point on the line. The numbers increase in value from left to right. For example, 10 is to the right of 5, and it is bigger

than 5. The same is true of the negative segment of the line: -5 is to the right of -10, and it is a bigger number than -10. (This sometimes confuses people, but remember that when negative numbers get ‘bigger’, they are in fact smaller: when you have -R 10 in your bank account, you have less money than when you have -R 5.). The spaces between the numbers on the number line are called intervals. In Figure A, the intervals are all equal. Every interval is 5 units long. In graphs, we use the numbers on the number line to stand for quantities of things. The distance between 0 and 5 might represent 5 bottles of milk, and the distance between 0 and 20 might represent 20 bottles of milk. On different number lines, the scale may differ. For instance, a distance of 1 centimetre could represent 5 units on one number line, but 5 000 units on another. The number line in Figure A is horizontal – it runs from left to right. (‘Horizontal’ actually means ‘in line with the surface of the earth’.) We can also draw a vertical line, that is, one that runs ‘up and down’, or at right angles to the horizontal line. Consider the one in Figure B. As we move up the vertical line, the numbers increase in value. Below zero the numbers are negative and above zero the numbers are positive. As was the case with our horizontal number line, the intervals are the same.

(ii) Suppose that, for some reason, the price of distilled water drops to R 10 per litre. This is exceptionally cheap, and many people will subsequently like to buy the product at this price. In our example, 10 kilolitres are demanded at the price of R 10 (point A on the graph). However, at this price, there simply is not enough distilled water supplied to meet the demand. Remember the law of supply: the lower the price, the less producers will supply in the market. At a price of R 10, only 2 kilolitres of distilled water will be supplied to the market (point B on the graph). At this price there is thus a demand for 10 kilolitres, but a supply of only 2 kilolitres. There is thus an excess demand of 8 kilolitres. One could also say there is a shortage of the product.

The question now arises: What can be expected in the case of a shortage? Since there is simply not nearly enough distilled water for everybody at the low price, some of the potential buyers will be willing to offer a higher price to get hold of the product. Say, for example, some buyers are willing to offer R 20 per kilolitre. Not all the buyers who are willing to buy at R 10 are willing to buy at R 20: at this price, there is a demand for 8 kilolitres (point C). At this price, suppliers can produce more and are willing to supply 4 kilolitres (point D). There is thus still an excess demand, but at these prices it has decreased: the excess demand is now 4 kilolitres (i.e. the 8 kilolitres that are demanded minus the 4 kilolitres that are supplied).

11 However, because there is still a large excess demand, some buyers are willing to pay even more to get distilled water. Some of them are even prepared to pay R 30 per litre (we say that buyers ‘bid up’ the price to R 30).Together, the number of buyers who are willing to pay R 30 per litre demand 6 kilolitres of distilled water. When the price is R 30, more suppliers are willing to supply distilled water, and collectively the suppliers offer 6 kilolitres to the market. Supply and demand are now in equilibrium: at R 30, 6 kilolitres are demanded and 6 kilolitres are supplied. There is now also no excess demand, and no excess supply, and the price is set at R 30.

Of course, what may also happen is that the suppliers, who will see that many people are eager to get the product, may raise the price. Either the buyers or the sellers, or both of them together, can thus cause the price to increase.

We can thus draw the following conclusion:

When prices are low and there is excess demand or shortage, buyers will tend to bid up the price, and/or suppliers will raise the price, until it reaches the equilibrium point.

We have now seen what happens when prices start out at a lower point than the equilibrium point. What happens in the opposite situation, when prices are very high, i.e. much higher that the equilibrium point?

Of course, suppliers want to sell at as high a price as possible, and (in terms of the law of supply) the higher the prices, the more they are willing to supply. Let’s say, then, that suppliers decide that they will charge R 50 per litre of distilled water, and at this price they can supply 10 kilolitres (point F in the graph). However, very few buyers are willing to pay this price, because they simply cannot afford it. At R 50, only 2 kilolitres of distilled water are demanded (point G). Here we therefore have an excess supply, also called a surplus, of 8 kilolitres: 10 kilolitres are supplied, but only 2 kilolitres are demanded. Suppliers are thus left with a huge surplus of distilled water, and will make severe losses if they do nothing further about the situation. In such a case, suppliers tend to lower the price of the product, so that they can sell more (or buyers persuade them to lower the price by offering to buy the product at a lower price). Let’s say the suppliers lower the price to R 40. At these prices, they are able to supply 8 kilolitres of distilled water; however, demand at this price is only 4 kilolitres. There is thus still a surplus of distilled water, although the surplus is now smaller (4 kilolitres). Eventually, the suppliers will lower the price still further, or “bid down” the price (or the buyers will persuade them to lower the price by offering lower prices). When the price reaches R 30, equilibrium point will once again have been reached: 6 kilolitres will be supplied, and 6 kilolitres demanded.

We can therefore draw a similar conclusion to the one we stated above:

When prices are high and there is an excess supply or surplus, suppliers will tend to bid down the price and/or buyers will offer to buy at lower prices, until the price reaches equilibrium point.

Our starting point before the strike is E1, where S1 intersects with D1 Due to the strike, production is adversely affected and the supply curve S1 shifts to the left (S2). Due to excess demand, the price for steel increases to P2 (refer to new equilibrium point E2). Now, should there be a wage increase, supply will be adversely affected once again, moving the S2 curve further to the left to S3. The new price level is P3. The reason for the price increase is two-fold, though:

Firstly, due to the strike, there is a shortage of supply (or excess demand). When demand exceeds supply, this causes price increases. Secondly, wages form part of production costs. Thus, wage increases cause an increase in such production costs. Producers tend to transfer the added cost burden to consumers by raising their prices. Inflationary pressures in the cost of production are often referred to as ‘cost push inflation’.

There is one more possible (belated) effect here. The workers that received the wage increase are also consumers in the market. Steel forms part of many household and other consumer products. There is thus a distinct possibility that the demand curve could also shift to the right at some point in the near future – due to increased income of consumers (D1 moves to D2). Inflationary pressures on the demand side of the economy are often referred to as ‘demand-pull inflation. The extent of this shift is not necessarily depicted accurately on this graph, but what is evident is that it will cause prices to increase even further. Whereas the equilibrium point in this market was initially at point E, it has no ended up at point E3 due to the strike.

This case study shows the detrimental effect that strikes have on the economy. The only real way in which the inflation rate can be brought down again, is by creating a rightward shift in the supply curve through productivity (i.e. not through overtime worked or through the employment of more workers).

No, it is not good for an economy to have deflation. The reason is quite simple. If the prices of products and services are continually on the decline, this will be extremely detrimental to demand. People would rather wait for the next price decrease before buying such products or services. Without demand in an economy, there is no economy. It is healthy for an economy to have controlled inflation. An inflation rate of between 3 and 6 percent is generally regarded as healthy, as long as employment levels are high and economic growth is good.

2001-11-29: The Reserve Bank and the Rand: Some historic reflections

Address by Mr. T.T. Mboweni, Governor of the South African Reserve Bank, at the Alberton Chamber of Commerce and Industry Christmas Lunch

Exchange rate comparisons

Over the past 80 years, the cost in United States currency of a South African rand has fallen from $2.43 to marginally above $0.10 at present. The movements up to 1949, when the level of $1,40 to the rand was established, took place under circumstances which limited the South African authorities' room for maneuver quite severely. These circumstances, and the $1,40 exchange rate, persisted until the Bretton Woods system came to an end in the early 1970s. In the subsequent 30 years, the South African authorities adopted various foreign exchange regimes, no longer constrained by the Bretton Woods rules. For most of this period the rand floated with various degrees of official intervention – and floated downwards.
