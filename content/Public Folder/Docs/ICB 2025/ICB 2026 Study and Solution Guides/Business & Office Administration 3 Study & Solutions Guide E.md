Solution to Activity 2d
(i) The details column of the PCJ is used to record exactly what was bought. The reason why the
exact details are required is to prevent petty cash fraud from taking place. If you are responsible
for petty cash, and you take cash from petty cash and pay Thomas Pule’s wages (see day 22),
rather write down his name, so that no recourse can be taken against you should the transaction
ever be queried.
(ii) No. The petty cash voucher itself is not a valid VAT invoice. The cash slip (the supporting
document) that displays the supplier’s VAT number should be attached to the petty cash voucher.
General practice is to staple these two documents together. When SARS does a VAT audit, the
petty cash voucher will not be accepted as proof that input VAT could be claimed. They will
request the actual cash slip.
(iii) The cooldrinks have been bought for entertainment purposes, and according to the VAT Act, no
input VAT may be claimed on such purchases.
(iv) No. Although petrol and diesel are zero-rated, oil is a standard-rated supply.
(v) A cell phone is an asset that will be used in the business for a period of more than one year. It is
therefore regarded as equipment and is debited as such. The ‘telephone’ account in the petty
cash journal is an expense account, and is only used for telephone expenses such as the cost of
calls made in line rentals. These expenses are immediate ‘losses’ for the business and will not
provide any future economic benefit for the business. In summary, when a cell phone itself is
bought, it is regarded as ‘equipment’, while all expenses relating to the cell phone (such as call
costs) are regarded as expenses and debited to the ‘telephone’ account.
(vi) When using a perpetual inventory (stock) system, all costs involved to get the stock onto the
shelves, ready for sale, are recorded in the trading inventory account. The reason is quite simple
– these costs are bound to become part of ‘cost of sales’. Cost of sales (an expense) is created
out of the inventory account when the stock is sold, and the resulting gross profit is then
determined.
(vii) The imprest system involves restoring the ‘imprest amount’ (in this instance R 2 000) at the start
of every month of trading. R 2 000 was put into the petty cash box on 1 March 20.9. During March
20.9, total purchases from petty cash amounted to R 1 618.62 (see the total of the petty cash
column in the petty cash journal). The key is to ‘put back in what you took out’; thus a cheque for
R 1 618.62 must be drawn to get the petty cash float back to R 2 000 at the start of April 20.9.

Feedback to Integration task 2.1
Answers may vary. The following serves as a guide.
There could be a number of reasons why a company would keep large amounts of petty cash on hand.
Some reasons are legitimate, others are questionable. The questionable reasons could quite easily
outweigh the legitimate ones. Many suppliers prefer to be paid in hard cash, rather than by means of
electronic funds transfer (EFT) or cheque, since the latter leaves an audit trail via a current bank
account. In simple terms, if a supplier receives hard cash and uses the cash to buy assets or pay for
expenses (instead of depositing the money in the bank account), SARS will have no record of the cash
being received. Such suppliers often offer a cash discount for paying in hard cash (their argument is
that it is better to forfeit 10 per cent of income by means of a trade discount, rather than to pay 28 per
cent in taxes). Such businesses often choose not to declare income received from sales when hard
cash is offered as payment. Unfortunately, this could be a futile exercise, since the buyer will normally
request a valid tax invoice from the supplier (on which input VAT may be claimed). SARS can check
whether the supplier has declared the corresponding output VAT by referring to the original tax invoice
retained by the buyer.
However, there are legitimate reasons for keeping large amounts of petty cash, too. Bank charges on
cheques can be astronomical. Cheque payments and EFTs do not always reflect immediately in the
recipient’s bank account. At most banks, a ‘hold’ of seven working days applies to any cheque banked
by the payee. One has to wonder if the security risks involved in keeping large amounts of cash
outweigh the perceived benefits.

Solution to Activity 3a
(i) ‘At risk’ – receipts can be manipulated to relieve accounts receivable and the shortfall in the cash
book can be covered up by another entry.
(ii) ‘Reasonably secure’ – wages clerk has no say over hours posted as this is authorised by his/her
manager.
(iii) ‘At risk’ – the purchasing manager can order goods suitable for their misappropriation and then
use their control over stores to cover up the transaction.
(iv) ‘Reasonably secure’ – the marketing manager has no vested interest in sales.
(v) ‘At risk’ – the factory manager has a vested interest in seeing capital expenditure approved
whereas the financial director or managing director would view the application more objectively.
Solution to Activity 3b
Answers may vary. The following serves as a guide.
Raw materials
The production manager considered raw material usage to be in line with that expected for the number
of bumpers produced. This would have been calculated based on the amount of raw materials recorded
out of the store. However, when physical counts were conducted there were always shortages to what
should theoretically have been in stock. As it was known that the store was left open for the night shift,
and unattended, it is probable that stock was removed without any record. The extra usage of materials
would turn a profit into a loss.
Bumpers invoiced less than 30 per cent of the production record
Either the production records were fabricated or bumpers were being stolen. The problem was that
there was no ‘finished goods’ store in which to book the bumpers produced. If this was in place, then
the discrepancy could be quickly resolved as the bumpers produced would be booked into stock and
the bumpers invoiced would be booked out of stock. A store’s employee could be made responsible,
and this would force the actual facts to come to light, as there would be a clear audit trail. If production
is overstated, the store manager will refuse to sign acceptance for the goods, the fabrication will be
uncovered and the true production level will come to light. If the production figure is correct, it will be
brought into stock, and then a responsible person is in place to prevent, and to be accountable, for any
theft of bumpers.

Feedback to Integration task 3.2
There is a trust level when appointing staff to authorise purchases, and one does not expect them to
abuse the system. The spirit of the authorisation is that the manager limits his/her purchase to
R 30 000. One cannot stop him/her going up to R 90 000 by triplicating purchase orders, but it is a
violation of the relationship of trust. An audit could pick this up and then the manager would be
exposed.
Solution to Activity 3c
(i) Evidence created by the auditor:
• Calculation of ratios and trends in order to examine unusual variations
• Physical inspection or observation, such as attendance at inventory counts
• Reperformance of calculations making up amounts within account balances
(ii) Evidence received from a third party:
• Debtors circularisations or bank confirmations
• Reports from experts (e.g. property valuations, actuarial valuations, legal representation
letters)
• Documents held by the client, but generated by third parties (e.g. supplier statements)
(iii) Evidence sourced from the client:
• Company accounting records and supporting schedules
• Client explanations for unusual fluctuations in account balances
• Information about the internal control system

The Pareto rule states that 20 per cent of transactions/balances make up 80 per cent of the overall
balance value.
The Pareto rule is a useful aid in maximising effectiveness for minimum effort.
In this example, given the 80/20 rule, you would expect:

Feedback to Integration task 3.4
It is essential to have a disaster recovery plan. What needs to be in place?
• Software programmes should be backed up off-site and must be downloadable on alternative
platforms. Arrange this with the licenser ahead of time.
• Data files should be backed up off-site.
• Alternative hardware should be in place for this kind of emergency.
• Construct a written plan (providing step-by-step guidance) on what to do in such a situation. With
pressure and stress, you do not want to have to think – it is better to do that ahead of time in a
relaxed atmosphere.
• Try a simulated run (i.e. practise run) to see if the recovery plan actually works. Identify weak
areas and correct them.
The plan will pay off massive dividends if there is ever a disaster, as your information technology (IT)
will continue more or less uninterrupted.
