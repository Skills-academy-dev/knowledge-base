• Unemployment: Information technology makes business processes far more efficient, which

Computer care includes the hardware components of computers need to be protected from dust.
The monitor and the keyboard should be dusted regularly. There are specific cleaning aids
available to clean computers with. All computer systems should be protected from direct sunlight

One example is when British American Tobacco (BAT) was being sued in Australia by Rolah McCabe.
McCabe was seeking recompense for her lung cancer, arguing that BAT had been negligent in
manufacturing and marketing its cigarettes (Information Enterprises Australia, n.d.). However, BAT
destroyed documents that would have been useful in the trial. Crown Counsel for Victoria, Peter
Sallman, suggested that the destruction of documents before, during or after legal proceedings be
named a criminal offence. His argument was that an organisation could avoid facing consequences for
unethical practices simply by destroying relevant documents in anticipation of being sued or facing
other forms of litigation.
Another example from Australia is Virgin Airlines, which failed to keep its aircraft maintenance records
up to date. Despite insisting that the airline faced no safety risks, Virgin was forced to keep its planes
within an hour of airports at all times, until it had overhauled its maintenance systems.

Answers may vary. The following serves as a guide
The two common causes for inefficient filing are insufficient storage as well as loss or destruction of
records. Other possible reasons for inefficient filing may include the following:
• A lack of proper training: the value of formal training is often underestimated. It is too often
presumed that personnel know what a filing system entails.
• Insufficient control: when no one is given ultimate responsibility for a filing system, chaos can
result. Control must be exercised particularly over the loan and return of files. Too many people
should not have access to the system.
• Irregular filing: the ideal is to file daily. Staff will often spend time looking for documents which still
have to be filed.
• Insufficient classification: a system should be classified as soon as possible according to the
needs of the users. It should be as complete and comprehensive as possible, and should include,
for example cross references.
• Static systems: filing systems must never be static. Rather, they should be adapted according to
the changing needs of the organisation.

(ii) Any two of the following can be listed:
• Numerical: Numbers are allocated sequentially (e.g. to client accounts and the documents are
filed according to the numbers allocated, from no. 1 to the end).
• Alphabetical: Filing is done according to the name of the person or company. If filing by name,
this is usually by surname, as opposed to the first name of the person.
• Alphanumerical: This type of arrangement is a combination of the alphabetical and the numeric
systems. The files are arranged in an alphabetical order, but not in strict alphabetical sequence. A
number is also assigned to a file.
• Chronological: In the order of date received usually with the latest date being the first document.
• Geographical: According to the geographical area (e.g. The provinces of South Africa). Note,
however, that there would in all likelihood be a subsystem of alphabetical or numerical filing within
the geographical files.
• Subject order: According to the subject of the file (e.g. Claims, payments, etc.). Again, this system
would probably use an alphabetical or numerical system within each subject.
• Colour: A system could be set up where an alphanumerical system is the basic system but each
file is then colour-coded (e.g. a customer’s file could contain correspondence, invoices and credit
applications). Each of these would be filed in a different coloured file (e.g. red for
correspondence, blue for invoices and yellow for credit applications) under the customer’s name
or number. Then, if someone is looking for a letter to the customer, they could simply go to the
red file and then look for the relevant letter which, in a good filing system, would be filed in date
order.

(a) Alphabetical filing system is used to describe the filing of documents under a proper name
i.e. a person’s name or the name of a company or organisation, according to the first letter.
This is probably still the most common type of classification and it does have the advantage
of being directly accessible in that it does not require an index and it is easy to understand
given that everybody knows the alphabet. However, other than in very small systems it can
become difficult to manage in that it is not easy to estimate space requirements for active
files and there can be problems when there are many files with the same or similar names.
(b) Pre-sorting documentation prior to filing has the benefit in that if you sort the documentation
the filing time will be reduced, you will be able to determine whether or not a new file needs
to be opened or created, you pre-sort documents into precise alphabetical order if the filing
system is alphabetical. By pre-sorting you also inspect the documents to ensure it is indeed
ready to be filed.
(c) The term chronological order means filing is done according to date. While this system is
rarely the sole classification criterion or the main one in operation, a chronological element
will exist within all other systems with the most recent communication or information always
appearing on top of the file. Chronological systems are useful when the date has major
significance for example, a departure date for a business trip and the date is the key factor
in triggering off some necessary action.
