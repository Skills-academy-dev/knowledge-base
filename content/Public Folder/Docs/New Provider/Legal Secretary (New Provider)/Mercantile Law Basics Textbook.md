Introduction
Law is a set of rules that governs how people behave in society. In South Africa, the legal system is
uncodified, meaning it is not written in a single document but is based on different sources, such as
the Constitution, legislation, Roman-Dutch law, and court decisions. The law is enforced by the
State, making it different from other rules, such as moral or religious rules, which cannot be legally
enforced.
A legal system exists to create order, protect rights, and ensure justice. It consists of different
elements, purposes, and functions that help regulate society. Laws also define real rights and
personal rights, as well as the difference between a natural person and a juristic person.
This module also introduces the sources of South African law, including legislation, customary law,
and judicial precedent, as well as the classification of law into public and private law. You will also
explore the judicial system, which includes different types of courts, such as the Constitutional
Court, High Courts, and Magistrates' Courts.
By understanding these fundamental concepts, you will develop a strong foundation in how the legal
system operates in South Africa and how it affects individuals and businesses.

A well-functioning legal system maintains order, ensures justice, and promotes fairness among
citizens. It consists of laws, courts, judges, lawyers, police, and enforcement agencies that help in
interpreting, enforcing, and applying the law.

1.3 Elements of Law
Every person has certain rights and duties under the law. A right is a legal or moral entitlement to do
something, while a duty is a legal or moral obligation to act in a certain way.
There are two main types of legal rights:
1. Real Rights – Rights over physical objects (things).
2. Personal Rights – Rights against specific individuals.
1.3.1 A Real Fight
A real right is a right in a thing (jus in rem), meaning the owner can enforce this right against
everyone in society. Real rights are legally protected against interference by anyone.
Example:
 X owns a house. X has the right to enjoy possession of the house, and every member of the
public must respect this right.
 A person who owns a car has the right to sell, rent, or use it as they please.
Key Features of Real Rights:
 A real right applies to movable (e.g., a car) and immovable (e.g., a house) property.
 The owner can use, sell, or rent the property as they wish.
 The right is protected against the whole world (meaning everyone must respect it).
 The right can be transferred from one person to another.
1.3.2 A Personal Fight
A personal right is a right against a person (jus in personam), meaning it allows one person to
demand an action or payment from another specific person.
16 | Page

Example:
 A owes B R100. B has the personal right to demand repayment from A.
 If you sign a contract to rent a house, the landlord has a personal right to receive rent from
you.
Key Features of Personal Rights:
 A personal right only applies to a specific person (not the whole world).
 The right can be enforced through contracts and agreements.
 The right is transferred through legal agreements (e.g., contracts).

1.4 The Concept of a Legal Entity Elements of Law
A legal entity is any person or organisation that has rights and responsibilities under the law. Legal
entities can be classified into two types:
1. Natural Persons – Human beings with legal rights and duties.
2. Juristic Persons – Businesses, organisations, or other legal entities that have legal rights
similar to a person.
Having a legal personality means that an entity can own property, enter into contracts, and be taken
to court. It is important to understand the difference between these two types of legal entities.

1.4.1 Natural Person
A natural person is a real human being. In legal terms, natural persons automatically receive
fundamental human rights such as freedom, equality, and ownership rights.
Key Features of a Natural Person:
 A natural person is a human being.
 They have legal rights and responsibilities.
 They can own property, enter contracts, and be sued.
 They can be part of a marriage, a partnership, or a business.
Example:
 A person can own land, sign an employment contract, or start a business.
 If someone fails to pay a debt, they can be taken to court as an individual.
1.4.2 Juristic Person
A juristic person is a legal entity that is not a human being but is recognised by law as having rights
and duties. Juristic persons allow groups of people to act as a single unit for legal purposes.
Types of Juristic Persons:
 Companies (e.g., Woolworths, Pick n Pay)
 Trusts (e.g., family trusts managing property)
 Partnerships (e.g., law firms, accounting firms)
 Non-profit organisations (e.g., charities)
Key Features of a Juristic Person:
 A business or organisation that exists separately from its owners.
 Can own property, enter contracts, and be sued.
 Protects owners from personal liability (in most cases).
 Operates continuously even if the owners change.

Example:
  A company can sign contracts, borrow money, and own property.
  If a company breaks the law, it can be sued in court without affecting the personal assets of its

1.5 Sources of the South African Law
South Africa has an uncodified legal system, meaning that laws come from multiple sources rather
than one single legal code. Before 1994, the legal system was based on Parliamentary supremacy,
but today, it is based on the supremacy of the Constitution.
The different sources of law help guide legal decisions and ensure fairness and justice. The main
sources of South African law are:
 The Constitution
 Legislation (National, Provincial, and Local)
 Roman-Dutch Law
 Customary Law
 Court Decisions (Judicial Precedent)
 African Customary Law
 Recordings of Modern Writers
1.5.1 The Constitution
The Constitution of South Africa (1996) is the supreme law of the country. It replaced the Interim
Constitution of 1993 and became effective on 4 February 1997.
Key Features of the Constitution:
 Defines the structure of the government and the country.
 Establishes the Bill of Rights, which protects the rights of all citizens.
 Guides legislation, ensuring that all laws align with the Constitution.
 Courts can strike down laws that do not comply with the Constitution.
Example:
If Parliament passes a law that violates the Bill of Rights, the Constitutional Court can declare it
invalid.

Role of the Constitution in Law-Making
1.5.2 Legislation
Legislation refers to laws made by the government. These laws are created at three levels:
Type of Legislation Who Makes the Law? Example
National Legislation Parliament (National C r im i n a l l aws, tax laws
Government)
Provincial Legislation Each Provincial Government Liquor licensing, provincial
roads
Local Government Legislation Municipalities Water restrictions, traffic
(By-laws) regulations
The Law-Making Process:
 A policy proposal is introduced (Green Paper).
 The government revises it and creates a draft law (White Paper).
 The draft is debated in Parliament and refined.
 Once approved, it is signed by the President and becomes law.
1.5.3 Roman-Dutch Law
South African law is based on Roman-Dutch legal principles, which combine Roman law and
Germanic law. These laws were brought to South Africa by Dutch settlers in the 1600s and 1700s.
21 | Page

Key Features of Roman-Dutch Law:
 Influences contract law, property law, and criminal law.
 It is part of South African common law.
 Some principles have been replaced by modern legislation.
Example:
Many contract laws in South Africa still follow Roman-Dutch principles.
1.5.4 Customary Law
Customary law refers to unwritten traditional laws practiced by various South African communities.
Key Features of Customary Law:
 Based on tradition and cultural practices.
 Recognised under the Constitution but must comply with the Bill of Rights.
 Used mainly in family law, inheritance, and traditional leadership disputes.
Example:
Lobola (bride price) is a traditional practice recognised under customary law.
Requirements for Customary Law to Be Recognised:
 It must be a reasonable request.
 It must be widely accepted by the community.
 Its meaning must be clear and enforceable.
1.5.5 Court Decisions/Judicial Precedent
Court decisions play a major role in shaping the law. Judges use previous rulings (precedents) to
decide on new cases.
Key Features of Case Law:
 Stare decisis – Lower courts must follow higher court decisions.
 Courts create legal precedents that influence future cases.
 Ratio decidendi – The reason for a court’s decision becomes binding.
 Obiter dicta – Extra comments made by a judge that are not binding.

Hierarchy of Court Decisions:

Example:
If the Constitutional Court rules that corporal punishment in schools is illegal, all schools must follow

1.5.6 African Customary Law
African Customary Law is a system of traditional rules and customs followed by different African
communities.
Key Features of African Customary Law:
 Based on oral traditions rather than written laws.
 Used in family law, inheritance, and community governance.
 Must comply with the Constitution and Bill of Rights.
Example:
Traditional courts in rural areas handle inheritance disputes under African Customary Law.
1.5.7 Recordings of Modern Writers
Legal experts, such as judges, academics, and lawyers, write books and articles about law. These
writings help courts interpret complex legal issues.

1.6 Classification of the South African Law
The South African legal system is divided into national law (laws that apply within South Africa) and
international law (laws governing relationships between countries).
National law is further divided into Public Law and Private Law.
1.6.1 Public Law
Public law, also known as ius publicum, governs the relationship between the state and individuals
or organisations. It includes laws that define government powers and responsibilities.
