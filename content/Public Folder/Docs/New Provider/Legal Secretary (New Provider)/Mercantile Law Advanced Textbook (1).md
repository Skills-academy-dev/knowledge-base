INTRODUCTION
The law of agency is an important concept that helps people and businesses get things done through
others. This module explains how an agency relationship works, where one person (the agent) is allowed
to act on behalf of another person (the principal). It also looks at the roles, rights, and duties of both
principals and agents, as well as how their relationship works in real life. By understanding agency,
students will learn how it makes everyday tasks like buying, selling, or signing agreements much easier.
In this module, students will explore the basics of agency, including how it is created and how it ends.
They will also learn about the different types of agents, such as agents who work on specific tasks or
those who manage ongoing responsibilities. Real-life examples will be used to show how agency works in
business and personal situations.
By the end of the module, students will understand why agency is so important, especially in business.
They will also learn about how principals and agents must work together honestly and responsibly to
make their relationship successful.
Unit 1: Definition and Nature of Agency
Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorized to act on behalf of another
(the principal) in business or legal transactions. This relationship is essential in commercial law as it
allows businesses to operate efficiently without direct involvement in every transaction. Understanding the
concept of agency helps individuals and businesses determine their rights, responsibilities, and liabilities
in contractual agreements. This unit explores the meaning of agency, its legal significance, and how it
differs from other legal relationships.
Example: A homeowner hires a real estate agent to sell their house. The agent talks to buyers
and arranges the sale for the owner.
Characteristics
 Consent: Both the principal and the agent must agree to the relationship. Consent can be
explicit (written or verbal) or implied from the actions of the parties.
 Example: if a business hires a delivery driver to deliver goods, the consent is shown in the
employment contract.
 Control: The principal has the authority to direct and control the agent’s actions within the
scope of their agreement. This ensures the agent’s actions align with the principal’s objectives.
 Representation: The agent’s actions, if within their authority, legally bind the principal. For
example, when an agent signs a contract with a third party, the principal must honor it.
 Good Faith: Both the principal and the agent must act honestly and treat each other fairly. If
the agent acts dishonestly, the principal can take legal action.
These features make agency a trusted and organized way of completing tasks through others.

Types of agency relationships
Agents perform different roles based on the authority granted by their principals. Some agents have
broad decision-making power, while others are limited to specific tasks. The type of agency
relationship determines the agent’s rights, responsibilities, and level of authority in business
transactions. This subunit examines the main types of agents in South African law and their specific
functions.
General Agent vs. Special Agent
A general agent has broad authority to perform various tasks on behalf of the principal within a
specific business or field. For example, a store manager may be given general authority to order
stock, hire employees, and handle customer disputes. A special agent, on the other hand, is
appointed for a specific purpose with limited authority. For instance, a real estate agent hired to sell a
house can only negotiate the sale price and sign contracts but cannot make other business decisions
for the homeowner.
Universal Agent
A universal agent has the broadest scope of authority, allowing them to act on behalf of the principal
in all legal, financial, and business matters. This level of authority is rare and is typically established
through a general power of attorney, where the principal grants extensive decision-making power to
the agent. Universal agents are often appointed in cases where the principal is unable to manage their
affairs due to travel, illness, or legal incapacity.
For example, a business owner who frequently works overseas may authorize their lawyer or
financial advisor to handle contracts, banking transactions, and property management on their behalf.
Because of the significant trust and responsibility involved, universal agents are often highly qualified
professionals such as attorneys or financial managers.

Del Credere Agent
A del credere agent functions as a sales agent who not only facilitates transactions but also assumes
additional financial risk by guaranteeing payment to the principal. This means that if the buyer fails to
pay for goods or services, the del credere agent personally covers the loss. Due to this added
responsibility, they typically charge a higher commission than a regular agent. This type of agency
relationship is common in international trade, wholesale markets, and auction sales, where there is a
risk of non-payment.
Example: a wholesaler might appoint a del credere agent to sell goods to retailers. If a retailer
fails to pay, the agent must cover the loss. This type of agency is common in industries where credit
sales are involved.
Commission Agent
A commission agent buys and sells goods on behalf of a principal while earning a commission on
each transaction. Unlike a factor, a commission agent does not take ownership of the goods. Travel
agents, for example, book flights and accommodations for clients and earn a commission from airlines
and hotels.
Example: Imagine a car manufacturer appoints a commission agent to sell vehicles in a
different city. The agent finds buyers, negotiates prices, and earns a percentage of each sale.
However, they never own the cars themselves. On the other hand, if the manufacturer appointed a
factor, the factor would take physical possession of the cars, store them, and sell them as if they were
their own stock.
Understanding these types of agents helps explain how different levels of authority and responsibility
work in agency relationships.
Importance of Agency in Business Transactions
Agency is important because it allows people and businesses to save time and focus on their
strengths while delegating other tasks. For example, businesses can hire sales agents to reach new
customers instead of handling all sales themselves.
Agency relationships also bring expertise to tasks. For example, a lawyer acting as an agent for a
client ensures that contracts are negotiated properly and legal issues are avoided.
Without agency, businesses would need to do all tasks themselves, which could slow operations and
limit growth.
Example: A company hires shipping agents to handle deliveries, ensuring goods reach
customers on time while the company focuses on production.
Businesses can appoint sales agents to reach wider markets without having to establish direct
relationships with every customer.
Legal representation by attorneys ensures that businesses comply with laws while negotiating
contracts or settling disputes.

Unit 2: Creation of an Agency Relationship
Agency relationships play a crucial role in business and legal affairs, allowing individuals or entities to
delegate tasks and responsibilities to an authorized party. These relationships enable principals
(businesses or individuals) to appoint agents to act on their behalf, ensuring that transactions and
decisions are made efficiently. The formation of an agency relationship must be legally recognized and
clearly defined, as it determines the scope of authority and obligations of both parties. This unit explores
the various ways in which agency relationships are created, including express agreements, implied
agreements, agency by necessity, and agency through the operation of law.
Methods of creating agency relationships
Agency relationships form the backbone of many business and legal interactions, allowing
individuals or businesses to delegate tasks to others for efficient operation. The creation of an agency
relationship involves establishing a clear understanding between the principal and the agent regarding
their roles and responsibilities. This ensures that the agent can act on behalf of the principal in a way that
aligns with the principal’s objectives. Understanding the different ways in which agency relationships can
be created is vital to avoiding misunderstandings and ensuring legal recognition.
Express Agreement
An express agreement occurs when a principal directly appoints an agent, either verbally or in writing.
In this type of agency relationship, the principal and the agent explicitly agree on the terms of their
relationship. The appointment can be made verbally, in writing, or through a formal contract, depending
on the nature of the agreement. Express agreements provide clarity, as both parties understand their
roles, responsibilities, and limits of authority.
Written agreements, such as contracts, clearly outline the agent's responsibilities, ensuring both
parties understand their obligations.
A written contract is the most secure and enforceable method of forming an agency relationship. It
includes specific details such as the agent’s duties, duration of agency, limitations, and compensation.

This ensures legal protection for both parties and provides evidence in case of disputes. Contracts may
also specify whether the agency relationship is exclusive or non-exclusive, ensuring that both parties
adhere to agreed-upon terms.
Verbal agreements, while valid, are less formal and may lead to disputes if there is no clear
record.
Although a verbal agreement can legally establish an agency relationship, it is less secure than a
written contract. Without documentation, disputes may arise if the principal and agent have different
interpretations of the agreement. In cases where high-value transactions or long-term agency
relationships are involved, a written contract is highly recommended to prevent misunderstandings.
Example: A company hires a manager and provides a written contract outlining their
responsibilities and scope of authority.
This method ensures transparency and avoids confusion, as all terms are explicitly stated.’

Implied Agreement
An implied agency relationship arises from the actions of the principal and the agent, or from the
circumstances surrounding their interaction.
Unlike express agreements, implied agreements do not require formal communication—instead, the
relationship is formed based on conduct. If a principal acts in a way that suggests an individual is their
agent, the law may recognize the relationship even if no formal agreement exists. This type of agency is
common in business and employment settings, where certain responsibilities are assumed rather than
explicitly stated.
The relationship is inferred based on how the parties behave or the roles they assume. This is
common in everyday business scenarios where roles are naturally understood.
In many workplaces, employees automatically act as agents of their employers, handling tasks without
formal authorization.
16 | Page

For example, a cashier in a store is implicitly authorized to collect payments from customers even if no
written contract explicitly states this authority. Such relationships rely on common industry practices and
reasonable expectations.
Example: A shop assistant is assumed to have the authority to sell products and interact with
customers, even without a formal agreement.
If a shop assistant is allowed to handle customer transactions, answer inquiries, and process payments,
they are acting as an agent of the business. The absence of a written agreement does not negate the
agency relationship, as their role is understood based on business operations and employer
expectations. Implied agreements rely on common sense and the reasonable expectations of the
parties involved. Courts recognize implied agency relationships based on standard industry practices,
the conduct of the principal and agent, and the reasonable expectations of third parties. This ensures
that business operations remain functional, even when formal contracts are absent.
Agency by Necessity
This type of agency occurs in emergency situations where the agent must act without the principal’s
prior approval to protect the principal's interests.
In some situations, an agent may have to act on behalf of the principal without explicit consent due to
urgent or unforeseen circumstances. This occurs when a decision must be made immediately to
prevent loss or damage to the principal’s assets or interests.
The agent's actions are justified by the urgency of the situation and the potential harm that
could arise if no action is taken.
