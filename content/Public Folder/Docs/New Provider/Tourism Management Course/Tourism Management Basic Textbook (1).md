INTRODUCTION
Welcome to your journey through the fascinating world of tourism management! This textbook
has been specially designed for South African distance learning students like you. It aims to
provide a comprehensive overview of the tourism industry, its various components, and the
important role it plays in our economy and society. Let's dive in and explore what tourism
management is all about.
Why Study Tourism Management?
Tourism is one of the fastest-growing industries in the world, and South Africa is a popular
destination for tourists from around the globe. By studying tourism management, you will learn
how to effectively manage tourist attractions, accommodations, transport, events, and more.
This knowledge is essential for ensuring that tourists have a safe, enjoyable, and memorable
experience.
What Will You Learn?
This textbook is divided into two main parts, each containing several modules that cover
different aspects of tourism management. Here's a brief overview of what you can expect to
learn in each part:
PART 1: Tourism Overview
 Module 1: Introduction to Tourism
You will learn what tourism is, who tourists are, and why tourism is significant. This
module will also cover how to measure tourism flows and the importance of tourism
management.
 Module 2: The History of Tourism
Discover the history of tourism and how transportation has developed over time. You will
also learn about the history and development of the South African tourism industry.
 Module 3: The Geography of Tourism
This module explores the relationship between tourism and geography. You will learn
about core tourism resources and major tourist attractions in South Africa.

Introduction
In this module, we will explore tourism and its impact on our lives. Tourism involves people
travelling for leisure, business, or other purposes. We will define tourists and understand the
importance of tourism in economic, social, and environmental contexts.
We will study tourism flows, the movement patterns of tourists, and discuss why certain places
attract more visitors. Challenges with collecting accurate tourism data will be identified, and ways to
address these issues will be discussed.
Tourism is a multifaceted field that integrates disciplines like geography, economics, and sociology.
We will examine different definitions of tourism and understand its diverse nature. Additionally, we
will learn about the tourism system model, which shows how various components work together to
create a seamless experience for tourists.
Studying tourism management helps us effectively manage and develop tourism activities,
benefiting both tourists and host communities. We will also explore the structure of the South
African tourism industry, gaining insights into how tourism functions in our country.
By the end of this module, you will have a solid understanding of tourism concepts and be ready to

1.1 Defining tourists
According to the United Nations World Tourism Organization (UNWTO), a tourist is someone who
travels for less than a year to a destination outside their regular living area for any main reason
other than work. These reasons might include business, leisure, or other personal purposes.
Types of tourists:
1. Excursionists: These tourists visit a destination for less than 24 hours without staying
overnight. They might visit for a day trip to a nearby city or attraction.
2. Overnight tourists: These visitors stay at least one night at their destination. They can be
categorised into:
 Short-haul tourists: Typically travel within the same continent or to nearby regions. For
example, a tourist from South Africa visiting Zimbabwe would be considered a short-
haul tourist.
 Long-haul tourists: Travel across continents or to far-off destinations, such as a South
African travelling to Canada.

1.1.1 Categories of Tourists
Spatial Aspects:
Tourists are often classified based on where they travel:
 Domestic tourists: These tourists explore new areas within their own country but outside
their everyday surroundings.
 International tourists: These include:
 Inbound tourists: Travellers coming from abroad into the host country.
 Outbound tourists: Residents of a country travelling to international destinations.
Temporal Aspects:
The duration of the visit helps further categorise tourists:
 Stayovers: Tourists who spend at least one night in a destination. They may stay in hotels,
guest houses, or other accommodations.
 Excursionists: Also known as day trippers, they visit a location for the day and return home
without an overnight stay.
1.1.2 Understanding Tourism
Types of Tourism:
 Leisure and recreation: This type involves travelling for pleasure, such as vacations at the
beach, adventures in nature, or city breaks for relaxation and entertainment.
 Cultural and religious tourism: This type includes visits to historical sites, festivals, religious
places, or events that offer insight into other cultures and beliefs.
 Business and professional: These trips are taken for professional reasons, including
attending workshops, conferences, or industry events, often linked to the traveller’s
occupation.
Why it matters:
Tourism has a profound impact on local economies by creating jobs, generating income, and
fostering infrastructure development. It also promotes cultural awareness and global connectivity.
UNWTO’s Role:
The UNWTO compiles and distributes global tourism statistics, providing essential insights into
travel trends and economic impacts. This information helps countries plan better for future tourism
development.

1.2 The significance of tourism
Global Impact:
Tourism stands as a powerful global economic force. In 2015, international travel saw 1.184 billion
people crossing borders, reflecting a 4.4% increase from the previous year. This growth
underscores tourism's vital role in international commerce and cultural exchange, demonstrating its
capacity to connect diverse cultures and boost economic sectors worldwide.
South Africa's Tourism Industry:
Tourism is a cornerstone of South Africa's economy, supporting over 1.2 million jobs and generating
more than R100 billion annually. Despite a decrease in 2021 to 3.2% from 6.4% of the GDP in 2019,
largely due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the sector remains robust. In 2015, the direct contribution of
tourism to the GDP was particularly significant, suggesting its potential to become the country's top
economic sector.
Tourist Arrivals:
Tourist arrivals in South Africa have seen significant fluctuations over the years, influenced by
global and domestic events. Notably, from 2012 to 2019, tourist numbers generally increased each
year, except for minor drops in 2015 and 2019. However, in 2020 and 2021, there was a sharp
decline in international overnight arrivals due to the global impacts of the coronavirus pandemic.
Despite these challenges, South Africa remained a leading African destination in 2021, closely
following Tunisia by about 220,000 visitors.
15 | Page

Major International Visitors:
In 2021, the largest group of non-African tourists came from the United States, with 82,020 visitors,
highlighting the strong appeal of South Africa’s diverse attractions such as wildlife safaris and
vibrant cultural experiences.
Economic Impact and Employment:
The economic contribution of tourism significantly decreased due to pandemic-related disruptions,
reflecting in both GDP contributions and employment figures. The sector provided jobs to
approximately 1.1 million people in 2021, down from over 1.5 million in 2019.
Historical Context and Tourism Trends:
The history of tourism in South Africa reveals how key events like the end of apartheid, Nelson
Mandela's release, and major sports events such as the Rugby and FIFA World Cups have
dynamically influenced tourist numbers. Additionally, factors like increased global mobility and
advances in technology have driven tourism growth, facilitating easier travel planning through online
platforms.
Understanding the dynamics of tourism growth, both globally and within South Africa, is crucial for
effective planning and development of this vital industry. It highlights the need for agility in adapting
to changing global trends to maintain and enhance South Africa's position as a premier tourist
destination.
1.3 Measuring tourism flows
Tourism consists of movements or 'flows' of people between different places. Tracking these
movements helps us grasp the scale of tourism and its impact. The most reliable sources of data on
tourism flows are provided by the United Nations World Tourism Organization (UNWTO) and the
World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC). These organisations gather and share information that
helps countries and businesses make informed decisions about marketing, infrastructure
development, and more.
Why it Matters:
Accurate tourism data are crucial for designing effective marketing strategies, improving
international relationships, and enhancing the overall management and measurement of tourism
activities within the national economy.

1.3.1 Global Tourism Statistics
Top Tourist Destinations and Spendings (2015):
 Top Destinations: France led global international tourist arrivals with 84.5 million visitors,
followed by the USA, Spain, China, and Italy. These countries are favoured for their rich
cultural heritage, vibrant cities, and stunning natural landscapes.
 Top Spenders: China was the largest spender on international tourism in 2015, reflecting its

countries also featured prominently on this list, showcasing their citizens' high engagement
with global travel.
 Top Earners: The United States earned the most from tourism, with significant receipts from
international visitors, indicating the strong pull of its tourist attractions and services.
1.3.2 South Africa's Position in Global Tourism
Challenges and Opportunities:
Despite its rich cultural and natural resources, South Africa ranked 36th in terms of international
tourist arrivals in 2015. The government aims to position South Africa among the top 20 destinations
by 2020, targeting 18 million arrivals. Factors like the country's beautiful landscapes, wildlife, and
efforts to improve safety and visa processes contribute to its tourism appeal.
These statistics and discussions highlight the importance of understanding tourism flows and
economic impacts. They serve as a foundation for strategic planning in South Africa’s tourism
sector, aiming to enhance its global position and maximise economic benefits from this vital
industry.
1.4 Tourist data problems
Challenges with Inbound Tourist Data:
When evaluating inbound tourist arrival statistics, a degree of caution is necessary, especially if
these statistics are used to identify historical trends. The United Nations World Tourism
Organization (UNWTO) has made efforts to standardise definitions and data collection
methodologies worldwide. Despite these efforts, discrepancies arise due to varied data collection
and reporting practices across countries, impacting the reliability of data on tourist arrivals,
expenditures, and other tourism-related metrics. Consequently, the UNWTO frequently revises
country-level data to provide more accurate year-to-year comparisons.
17 | Page

For example, statistics from South African Tourism in 2015 show that 87% of tourists from Lesotho
were on holiday, with the remainder visiting for business or other reasons. Despite Lesotho's
population being about 1.9 million, South Africa reported hosting over 1.6 million holidaymakers
from Lesotho in the same year. This suggests that when 100 people from Lesotho travel to South
Africa, they might be counted multiple times, such as when they cross the border multiple times in a
year.
Complexities in Transit and Multiple Entries:
Transit travellers, particularly from countries like China and Brazil, significantly impact South
Africa's tourism statistics. For instance, 28.9% of all Chinese tourists and 44% of Brazilian tourists
are transit visitors, potentially skewing overall arrival figures. Furthermore, many American tourists
use South Africa as a stopover en route to other East African destinations like Kenya or Tanzania.
This results in these tourists being counted more than once – upon their arrival in South Africa and
again when they return during the same trip.
Domestic Tourism Tracking Issues:
Tracking domestic tourism movements poses additional challenges, as it is harder to monitor
compared to international tourism. Often, data on domestic tourism is collected through surveys or
responses to tourism boards, which can sometimes rely on random sampling techniques. These
methods may introduce further uncertainties in understanding the complete picture of tourism
dynamics within South Africa.
Understanding the intricacies and potential errors in tourism data is crucial for effective
management and planning in the tourism sector. It highlights the need for continuous improvement
in data collection practices and the importance of interpreting this data with an understanding of its
limitations.
1.5 The nature of tourism
Complexity in the Tourism Sector:
Tourism is a complex, multifaceted industry that spans various sectors of the economy. It involves a
broad network of businesses, from small bed and breakfasts to large multinational hotel groups, and
from curio shops to major casino operations. No tourism business operates in isolation; each one
interacts with various other services like meals, accommodation, and sightseeing, which together
create the holiday experience.
18 | Page

Fragmentation and Diversity:
In South Africa, the tourism industry is diverse and fragmented, dominated by a mix of large,
well-established companies and a multitude of smaller, locally-owned enterprises. About 60% to
70% of the industry comprises small, medium, and micro enterprises (SMMEs), which play a crucial
role in sustaining local economies and offering unique, personalised experiences.
Major Players:
Significant players in the market include major tour agencies, hotel groups, and transport
operations. Notable companies such as Tsogo Sun, Sun International, and Protea Hotels (acquired
by Marriott International) are pivotal in shaping the industry. Air carriers like South African Airways
(SAA) and kulula.com also contribute significantly to tourism logistics.
1.5.1 Tourism's Economic Implications
Fixed and Variable Costs:
The costs associated with tourism, such as operating a train or managing a hotel, often remain
constant regardless of the number of passengers or guests. This economic characteristic means
that profitability can heavily depend on achieving high levels of occupancy and usage.
Vulnerability and Resilience:
The tourism industry is highly vulnerable to external events, including natural disasters, economic
recessions, and health crises like the Ebola or Zika virus outbreaks. Despite these challenges, the
industry has shown remarkable resilience, recovering from setbacks such as the 9/11 attacks and
the 2004 tsunami in Southeast Asia.
Managing Tourism:
Effective tourism management involves taking into account the interests of various stakeholders and
aims to maximise economic benefits for both the community and the country. This management
occurs at different levels:
 Individual business level
 Destination level
 National level, involving ministries or departments of tourism.

Example 3: Myanmar: The Next Cultural Treasure?
With the gradual political and economic opening of Myanmar since 2011, there is growing optimism
about the revival of its tourism sector. The country is home to some of the most breathtaking
historical sites in Southeast Asia, including the ancient city of Bagan, where over 2,000 Buddhist
monuments tower over the green plains.
Myanmar's tourist attractions, previously obscure due to decades of strict military rule and limited
external contact, are now becoming more accessible to international visitors. The country's tourism
industry is poised for a major transformation, needing significant improvements in infrastructure and
services to accommodate an expected surge in visitors.
The lifting of many travel restrictions and the easing of visa regulations have made it easier for
tourists to explore this once-hidden gem. The ancient temples and vibrant local cultures are largely
unspoiled by modern developments, offering an authentic experience that is increasingly rare in a
globalised world.
Tourists are expected to flock to Myanmar's historical sites in large numbers, drawn by its unique
cultural heritage and the allure of exploring a country that has only recently opened its doors to the
wider world.
1.6 Why study tourism management?
Tourism management is not only a dynamic academic field but also a critical area of study that
prepares students to understand and manage the intricacies of one of the world's fastest-growing
sectors. As tourism expands globally, the demand for well-informed, strategically minded
professionals continues to rise. Academic institutions worldwide, such as the Centre International de
Recherches et d'Études Touristiques (CIRET), highlight the importance of this field with their
extensive network of research centres—841 in 112 countries as of 2016—emphasising the global
scope and significance of tourism studies.

also involves navigating diverse regulatory environments and cultural landscapes. For instance,
managing a tour that spans multiple countries requires understanding international travel
regulations, cultural sensitivities, and logistical coordination between numerous service providers.
