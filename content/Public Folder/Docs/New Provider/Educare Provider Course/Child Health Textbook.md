THEME 1: GENERAL IMPORTANCE OF HEALTH

Health is a broad idea that's not easily pinned down. It can mean various things to different people and can be influenced by each person’s background and the society they live in.

1.1 Concept of health

A universally agreed definition of health hasn't been established, but the World Health Organisation's (WHO) definition is often used. WHO defines health as a state of total physical, mental, and social well-being, not just the absence of disease or weakness.

1.2 Characteristics of a healthy child (birth to 6 years)

A healthy child typically presents certain characteristics:

• They appear bright and alert.

• They are usually happy and content.

• They feel secure and show confidence.

• They enjoy playtime and engage actively.

• Their growth is appropriate for their age.

• They eat well and have a healthy appetite.

• They are clean, with neat hair and clear skin.

• Their teeth are well cared for, and their eyes are bright.

• They have a good posture and strong muscles.

• Their legs are robust, supporting their activity.

Understanding Health More Deeply

The concept of health includes several aspects:

i) We all have to make our own health choices, which means we need to know enough about

health to make good decisions.

ii) Health is complex and linked to every aspect of a person's life.

iii) Good health should be a tool that enables us to live our lives more fully, not just an end in

itself.

iv) Health is very personal – each of us has our own understanding of what it means to be truly

healthy.

v) Our perception of health is always evolving, and achieving perfect health in every aspect

might not be possible, but we strive for the best state of well-being we can.

1.3 Factors which may affect the health of the child (from the time of

conception)

A child's health is shaped by three key elements:

• Heredity: This is the set of traits passed down from parents to their child. These traits are decided at conception and can include things like hair colour, potential height, and certain health risks.

• Environment: The surroundings where a child grows up, including their home, school, and community, can significantly affect their health.

• Behaviour: The habits and actions of a child, often learned from their family and friends, also play a part in their health.

1.3.1 Heredity

Heredity is about the genes we inherit from our parents. These genes set the stage for our individual characteristics at the very start, when conception occurs.

Each child comes into the world with their unique features. For example, one might have black hair while another might have brown.

9 | Page

As you interact with children, you might notice that some have quicker development or a more pronounced sense of rhythm than others. These inborn qualities are the result of the genetic inheritance from their parents.

Heredity outlines what a child might potentially achieve. Yet, it's the environment that often shapes these genetic potentials into the person the child becomes.

Examples of disorders linked to genetic differences:

• Cleft palate: A condition where the roof of the mouth is not fully closed.

• Cleft lip (hare lip): A split in the upper lip.

• Haemophilia: A disorder that affects the blood's ability to clot.

• Down's Syndrome (Trisomy 21): A genetic condition resulting from an extra chromosome 21.

• Albinism: A lack of pigment in the skin, hair, and eyes due to a genetic variation.

• Turner Syndrome: A chromosomal disorder affecting females, where one of the X chromosomes is missing or incomplete.

These conditions are present from birth and can influence various aspects of a child's life and health.

1.3.2 Environment

A child's growth and development can be influenced by many environmental factors, which can come into play during the foetal stage or at any time after birth.

Environmental factors fall into three categories:

(a) Physical Factors:

These include elements such as:

• Climate: Extreme weather events like hurricanes and floods.

• Soil: Presence of contaminants like worm eggs or waste from animals and humans.

• Water: The quality of water, whether it is clean or polluted.

• Noise Levels: Sound pollution from traffic or airplanes.

• Air Quality: The presence of smoke, carbon dioxide, and other harmful gases in the air.

• Food Supplies: The availability of fresh versus rotten or contaminated food.

(b) Biological Factors:

This category covers:

• Germs: Harmful microorganisms that can prevent a body from fighting off illnesses.

• Plants: Some plants are toxic and can pose a risk if ingested by children.

• Animals: Certain animals can transmit diseases.

• People: Individuals can also be carriers of diseases that may spread.

10 | Page

(c) Social Factors:

These factors include:

• Family Attitudes and Values: Beliefs and practices regarding child-rearing, nutrition, or discipline.

• Lifestyles: Habits like smoking or substance misuse.

• Religious Practices: Dietary restrictions based on religious beliefs that could affect nutrition.

• Education: The role you play, as an educator, in promoting healthy or unhealthy practices.

• Community Customs: The traditions and norms within the school and broader community can impact health.

• Social Problems: Issues like divorce, death, single-parent families, teenage pregnancy, and community violence can affect a child’s well-being.

• Traditional Health Practices: Some cultural medical practices might pose health risks if not performed hygienically.

1.3.3 Behaviour

Behaviour is likely the most significant factor influencing children's health.

Children form their health-related choices and decisions from the knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs they learn, which you as a carer have a substantial influence on. The decisions and actions they take while under your supervision can set a precedent for their future.

Good health habits, when established early, tend to be maintained throughout a person's life.

It’s essential to instil positive health habits in children from a young age. Doing so lays a strong foundation for later life, upon which further healthy practices can be built.

1.3.4 Keeping Children Healthy

Keeping children healthy includes:

• Providing a safe and healthy environment both indoors and outdoors.

• Maintaining a positive attitude towards health and well-being.

• Observing and supervising children closely to ensure their safety and welfare.

• Teaching children valuable health habits – your own behaviour is a powerful example for them.

• Collaborating with parents and healthcare professionals to support the child's health.

• Ensuring the Educare setting is clean and meets hygiene standards.

1.4 Influence of health on the total development of the child

A child's health status has a profound impact on their overall development. It affects not only their physical growth but also their energy levels and their eagerness to engage in activities.

11 | Page

Example:

Consider a child with a heart condition who has spent a lot of time in the hospital and undergone surgery. This child may not be as physically robust as other children their age and might show less interest in exploring and learning new things.

An unhealthy child might find it challenging:

• To focus their attention, which is crucial for learning (cognitive development).

• To be physically active, which is necessary for building strength and coordination (physical development).

• To make friends and play with others, which is essential for learning social skills (social development).

• To feel motivated or joyful, which can influence their mood and outlook (emotional development).

• If they are frequently absent from school due to illness, their language skills and other areas of learning may fall behind (academic development).

1.5 A team approach to the health of the young child (health team)

An educarer collaborates with a diverse group of professionals to ensure comprehensive health objectives are achieved and to deliver a full health programme within the Educare Centre.

A Health Team is made up of doctors, dentists, Educare Centre staff, the child’s parents, and various organisations. This team plays a crucial role in upholding and enhancing the health and development of young children.

1.5.1 The Persons Who Make up This Health Team

Anyone who can contribute positively to a child’s health could be a member of this Health Team. It’s important to understand that the team composition might vary depending on the Educare Centre and the specific health needs that arise in a particular case.

(a) Prominent Members of the Health Team:

Key individuals or entities often form a part of the Health Team. This could be due to their special connection with the child or the unique services they provide. These team members are essential in maintaining high health standards. They include:

• The child themselves.

• The child’s parents or guardians.

• The child’s general practitioner, hospital, or local clinic.

• Municipal and school health services.

• Social workers and dental services.

• Welfare organisations and health inspectors.

• The educarer themselves.

12 | Page

(b) Other Members of the Health Team:

• There are also specialist professionals who may join the Health Team, including:

• Speech Therapists, who assist with communication challenges.

• Occupational Therapists, who support the development of daily living skills.

• Physiotherapists, who help with movement and physical function.

• Optometrists, who ensure the child's vision is properly cared for.

1.5.2 The Role of the Prominent Members of the Health Team

(a) The Child:

• The very existence of a health team is for the child; without the child, there would be no need for such a team.

• A child who practises good health habits is less likely to fall ill frequently.

• It is important for the child to follow health guidelines.

• The child should communicate openly about their health and adhere to the profile of a healthy child as described earlier.

(b) The Parents:

• Parents have the primary responsibility for their child's health and well-being.

• The health education provided in the Centre should align with what is taught at home.

• Parents must keep in regular contact with the educarer to report and discuss any health concerns, such as an upset stomach, headaches, or bedwetting.

(c) The Family Doctor - General Practitioner (G.P.) and/or Clinic:

• Children often become comfortable with their family doctor, making it essential to consult the doctor if the child is unwell.

• General Practitioners offer key preventive health services, including immunisations.

• Educators should have the contact details of the child's doctor to hand for any urgent situations.

(d) Municipal and School Health Services:

• Local authorities provide essential health services for young children, such as well-child clinics, immunisation programmes, and screening for conditions like tuberculosis (TB).

• The nursing staff from local health authorities will visit the Centre to offer these health services for children.

• They play a vital role in ensuring environmental health standards are met within their jurisdiction.

• They are also in charge of implementing health regulations and by-laws to maintain high standards of health and hygiene, including sanitation and refuse removal.

13 | Page

(e) Social Workers:

• Social workers possess specialised knowledge that equips them to support and guide families struggling with issues such as alcoholism, drug abuse, and challenging home environments.

• If there are concerns about a family's ability to provide a safe environment, the parents may be directed to a social worker or a relevant welfare organisation.

• Protecting the child's welfare is a critical responsibility of social workers. If necessary, they may intervene to place the child in a secure environment.

• Social workers play a crucial role in situations where child abuse is suspected. In such cases, it may be appropriate to involve the authorities without initially informing the parents.

• Typically, social workers are employed by welfare organisations, state departments, and sometimes churches.

(f) Dental Services:

• Dental health is managed by Oral Hygienists and Dentists.

• An Oral Hygienist focuses on preventive care, including teeth cleaning.

• Dentists are responsible for more complex procedures, such as filling cavities and extracting teeth.

• Dentists work in both private practice and public health sectors, sometimes directly for the government.

• Dental professionals may visit Educare Centres to educate both parents and children about dental health and to screen for any dental issues.

(g) Welfare Organisations:

• Welfare organisations serve as crucial health allies for Educare Centres.

• They provide supportive services to the community, including specialised health initiatives and assistance for various social issues.

• Examples include the South African National Cancer Association (CANSA), the South African National Tuberculosis Association (SANTA), and The Salvation Army.

• The South African Association for Child and Family Welfare is another key organisation in this network.

• It is important for Educare Centres to be informed about these organisations and to utilise their services when needed.

(h) Health Inspectors:

• Municipalities and the Department of Health employ Health Inspectors to ensure that health standards are upheld.

• These inspectors monitor environmental health and hygiene within businesses and at all times in the community.

• Regular inspections by Health Inspectors ensure that businesses comply with health regulations.

14 | Page

• For Educare Centres to operate, they must be licensed, meeting specific health and safety standards.

• Health Inspectors have the authority to revoke a centre's licence if it does not meet health requirements.

• They also provide valuable advice to help maintain high health standards in the centre.

(i) The Role of the Educare Worker:

• As an Educare Worker, your aim is to ensure each child reaches their best health potential.

• You act as the key link between the children and the health team, overseeing the direct implementation of health policies.

• Your role is crucial in equipping the Educare Centre with necessary health and safety measures.

• Coordinating the health programme is part of your responsibilities.

• You should be familiar with all possible members of the health team and have their contact information for emergencies.

• Providing valuable input is part of your duty to ensure the health programme is properly implemented.

• Vigilant observation of each child's health is essential, not only when they arrive at the centre but consistently throughout their time there.

• You should have a thorough understanding of how diseases spread.

• Continuous observation of the children is needed to quickly identify any health issues.

• Guiding children in developing good health habits is a fundamental part of your role.

1.5.3 Some Indicators that the Child is Not Well Could Be:

• Unexplained Crying: If the child is crying and there's no clear reason, it could signal discomfort or distress.

• Loss of Appetite: A child who is not eating well might be experiencing health issues.

• Change in Activity Levels: A child who is normally lively but suddenly becomes less active may not be feeling well.

• Increased Bathroom Visits: Going to the bathroom more often than usual could indicate a health problem.

• Signs of Abuse: Any signs of harm, such as bruises or evidence of physical injury, should be taken seriously as potential indicators of abuse.

THEME 2: HEALTH AND THE ENVIRONMENT

2.1 Community and environmental health

2.1.1 Community health

A holistic approach to health recognises the interconnectedness of the body, mind, and spirit, acknowledging that each plays a crucial role in an individual's overall well-being.

In this view, health is considered in its totality, incorporating physical (bodily), psychological (mental), and social aspects. The well-being of the community is also essential, as individual health cannot be optimised if the community is not involved.

The Role of the Day Care Centre:

(a) Day Care Centres are tasked with:

• Ensuring health rules are correctly followed.

• Providing health education to parents.

• Offering guidance to children on their care and overall health.

(b) Consequently, the Educare Centre must address:

• Physical Needs: Such as nutritious food, sufficient exercise, and good hygiene.

• Psychological Needs: Through stimulating activities like singing, puzzles, and painting.

• Social Needs: By facilitating group games and events with parents, like concerts.

Gathering more information about the children's backgrounds can enhance social support.

(c) Understanding the child's background is beneficial for:

• The prevention of illness.

• The promotion of good health.

• Addressing healthcare needs tailored to children with disabilities.

• Referring to specialised health team members for children with specific health concerns.

While community efforts focus on preventing diseases, it ultimately falls to individuals to make their own health-related choices, such as decisions about smoking, dietary habits, and lifestyle behaviours.

2.1.2 Environment health

Environmental health recognises that a person's well-being is not only about their personal health but also includes the health of the environment around them.

16 | Page

The World Around Us Consists of Three Divisions, Namely:

(i) Physical Environment:

a) Internal Physical Environment:

• The inherited genes that a baby receives at conception play a role in their future health and strength.

• These genes can also predispose the child to certain hereditary diseases such as cleft palate and Down Syndrome.

b) External Physical Environment:

This encompasses the broader factors affecting a child's health and is often the most significant in shaping their well-being.

(ii) Social and Political Environment

• The social structures and government policies in place can greatly influence the health of a child.

(iii) Emotional Environment:

• A child's emotional surroundings, including relationships and emotional support, are crucial for their health.

General Physical Environment:

• Climate: Extreme temperatures, whether very hot or icy cold, impact our health and require appropriate adjustments to maintain well-being.

• Rainfall: Variations in rainfall, from abundant to none, also necessitate changes in how we live and protect our health.

A person must adapt to these environmental conditions to survive and thrive.

(a) Refuse and Sewage Removal:

• If waste isn't properly managed, bacteria can grow and pose health risks to the community.

• Educators play a critical role in promoting proper waste disposal.

• Encouraging habits like hand washing and the safe disposal of waste protects community health.

• Properly functioning toilets are vital for sanitation. Educators must ensure that they are maintained and that blockages are prevented.

(b) Housing:

• Adequate ventilation in homes is essential to prevent the spread of viruses and bacteria.

• Overcrowding should be avoided as it can contribute to the spread of infectious diseases like tuberculosis (TB).

• Cleanliness in housing is fundamental to health and must be upheld.

17 | Page

(c) Food and Milk Hygiene:

• All food, including milk, must be handled with strict hygiene to prevent contamination.

• Awareness of proper refrigeration and foodborne diseases is important for educators.

• Educators should be informed about common illnesses related to food and milk, which will be discussed in further detail later.

(d) Pollution:

(i) Ground Pollution:

• The soil can be contaminated by insecticide sprays and excreta that contain worm eggs.

• When children play in contaminated soil, there is a risk they could become ill.

(ii) Water Pollution:

• Lack of proper sanitation facilities means human and animal waste can contaminate water sources.

• Communities using polluted water for washing, drinking, or cooking might face serious health issues, including food poisoning.

• Waterborne diseases such as Cholera, Gastro, and Bilharzia can arise from contaminated water.

• In case of diarrhoea outbreaks, the water supply should be tested and treated.

(iii) Air Pollution:

• Breathing polluted air, filled with smoke and gases from vehicles and industrial activities, can lead to respiratory conditions.

(iv) Noise Pollution:

• Excessive noise can cause hearing problems, reduce the ability to concentrate, and lead to irritability and aggression.

As an Educare professional, it's crucial to prioritise the prevention of accidents and uphold a high standard of hygiene and safety at all times. This means being vigilant and maintaining a clean and secure environment where children can learn and grow. Supervision and observation should be constant to ensure that any risks are immediately identified and addressed. Moreover, the setting should be designed to foster not only physical safety but also social and emotional development, providing a holistic space for children to develop healthily and happily.

(ii) Social and Political Environment:

(a) Social Awareness of a Baby:

From before birth, a baby is already tuned into social cues, particularly those coming from the mother. A baby can sense and respond to the mother's emotional state, laying the groundwork for early social interactions.

(b) The Family as the First Social Environment:

A child's first experiences of society begin within the family, which is a fundamental source of security and familial connection. The family's norms and values can significantly impact the child's development and their interaction with the wider community.

(c) Impact of Social Misbehaviour:

Behaviours such as alcohol and drug abuse in the community can disrupt social structures and negatively influence children.

(d) 'Culture' and Its Role:

Culture encompasses the shared practices, beliefs, and values of a group. It shapes community behaviour and individual identity, providing a sense of belonging and guiding social interactions.

(e) Influence of Cultural Habits:

Cultural practices, including dietary restrictions based on religious beliefs, can affect a child's health and social experiences. For example, certain communities may have specific traditions regarding food consumption that are integral to their identity.

19 | Page

(f) Health Customs:

Cultural health practices, like the use of traditional medicines and acupuncture, play a role in health management within communities and must be understood in the context of the broader cultural background.

(g) Communication in Community Health:

Open and healthy communication is crucial for community well-being. A breakdown in communication can lead to a sense of disconnection and contribute to a wider societal malaise.

(h) The Importance of Recreation:

Recreation is a vital aspect of health, offering a way to relax, engage with others, and enjoy a sense of freedom. Healthy recreational spaces are those that are open, allowing for fresh air and social interaction, such as a park or a sports court.

(iii) Emotional Environment:

Emotional well-being is shaped by a combination of internal and external factors:

(a) Internal Factors:

These are influenced by genetic traits, including physical attributes governed by our body’s glandular functions, which can affect our appearance and behaviour. Internal factors also encompass the emotional capacity to give and receive love, the ability to respect and feel confident, and to maintain a positive life perspective. Conversely, a lack of these can lead to a negative self-image, self-doubt, and feelings of aggression or dissatisfaction.

(b) External Factors:

The environment around us can significantly influence our emotional state. Elements such as the level of security, privacy, acknowledgment from others, and having access to necessities like shelter and food contribute to a healthy emotional environment. A positive and secure self-image is critical for mental well-being. Conversely, inconsistent care, exposure to stress, and lack of recognition can contribute to feelings of anxiety, impatience, aggression, and a generally negative view of life.

2.2 Spreading and prevention of diseases

2.2.1 Terminology and concepts

Carriers:

Individuals who carry pathogens but may not show symptoms themselves. They can, however, transmit these pathogens to others.

Contacts:

These are people who have been exposed to others with an infectious disease and may potentially contract that disease.

Disease-causing Organism:

The specific microorganism responsible for causing a disease, such as the bacteria that causes tuberculosis.

20 | Page

Epidemic:

An occurrence where a disease affects a large number of people within a community or region over a short period.

Immunisation:

A preventive measure, often in the form of a vaccine, that protects individuals from certain diseases.

Immunity:

• Passive Immunity: This is immunity passed from mother to child, often through breastfeeding.

• Active Immunity: Developed when a person is vaccinated or has recovered from a disease.

Incubation Period:

The time span between the initial infection and the onset of symptoms.

Infection:

This happens when disease-causing germs enter the body and multiply, potentially leading to illness.

Isolation:

A measure to separate sick individuals from healthy ones to prevent the spread of infections.

Quarantine:

Restricting the movement of people who may have been exposed to a contagious disease to prevent its spread.

Risk Group:

People who are more likely to be affected by a disease, often due to certain health conditions or age.

Signs and Symptoms:

Observable effects of an illness, such as rashes or fever, that indicate someone may be sick.

Source of Contamination:

The place or substance where infectious agents can be found, such as contaminated water or food.

2.2.2 Methods by which diseases spread

In health education, it's important to be aware of the various ways diseases can be transmitted. Here are three key methods:

(a) Spread of diseases by means of air (droplets):

Diseases can spread through tiny moisture droplets in the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes. People nearby may inhale these droplets, which contain harmful microorganisms, leading to infections such as the flu, chickenpox, measles, pneumonia, tuberculosis, whooping cough, and mumps.

(b) Spread of diseases by means of contaminated water:

Water sources polluted with harmful microorganisms can cause diseases if the water is consumed or comes into contact with the skin. Illnesses like diarrhoea, bilharzia, cholera, jaundice, and typhoid fever can result from using contaminated water.

(c) Spread of disease by direct contact:

Direct physical contact with an infected individual or with contaminated objects can transmit diseases. For example, touching items soiled by someone with an infectious disease, using their personal belongings like combs, or lying on bedding with lice can lead to the spread of infections.
