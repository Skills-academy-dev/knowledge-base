The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Learners’ Memory: A Systematic Review

Siavash Rokhsari1\* 1University Canada West, Canada

Abstract Memory plays a fundamental role in learning through encoding, retention, and subsequent retrieval. The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) in education may reconfigure these processes, yet the field lacks a consolidated account of its impact on learners’ memory. This PRISMA-2020 systematic review synthesizes 26 empirical studies spanning K–12, higher education, and adult learning. Interventions primarily involved large-language-model chatbots and conversational agents, AI-generated instructional media (teaching videos, mnemonic images), and adaptive analytics-based feedback. Across designs, outcomes were predominantly positive: multiple studies improved immediate retention and demonstrated advantages at delay (one week or course examinations). Benefits were greatest when AI introduced structure (retrieval practice, mnemonic supports), targeted metacognitive or affective feedback, or personalized prompts; negligible effects arose with strong comparison resources or generic, low-presence support. Working memory was seldom assessed, and sensory memory was absent. The evidence synthesized in this study bears implications for educators, policymakers, and learners to adopt AI, align with cognitive principles, monitor delayed outcomes, and ensure ethical implementation.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cognition, Memory, Retrieval practice, Educational technology

Instructional design theories explain why technology-enhanced environments can either support or hinder these memory processes. The Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning and Cognitive Load Theory emphasize aligning modality and presentation to avoid extraneous processing, focusing limited capacity working memory on essential and germane processing, and providing worked examples or cues that scaffold integration of new information with prior knowledge (Mayer, 2020; Sweller et al., 2011). Under these theories, designs that reduce split attention and redundancy, while preserving opportunities for effortful processing, are expected to benefit encoding and consolidation and to set the conditions for later retrieval. In parallel, research on cognitive offloading and transactive memory shows that people may remember where to find information rather than the information itself when external access is easy. This has clear implications for study practices that rely on immediate assistance, since overreliance can suppress retrieval opportunities and foster illusions of competence (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Sparrow et al., 2011).

Within this theoretical landscape, artificial intelligence (AI) has become increasingly embedded in educational practice. Intelligent tutoring systems adapt problem sequences and feedback based on learner performance and, on average, have outperformed conventional instruction across domains and study designs, although effect sizes vary by context and implementation quality (Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013). Augmented reality (AR) applications often report medium learning gains in meta-analyses, but effectiveness depends on how AR is integrated pedagogically and, on the demands, placed on attention and working memory (Akchayir & Akchayir, 2017; Garzón & Acevedo, 2019). More recently, conversational agents and large language model-based (LLM) systems have been reviewed as tools that can scaffold explanation, generate feedback, and personalize support. Systematic reviews in this area report promising effects on study processes and various performance measures, but they also highlight variability in design quality, task alignment, and assessment choices (Labadze et al., 2023; Okonkwo & Ade-Ibijola, 2021; Wollny et al., 2021). Together, the literature suggests multiple pathways by which AI could influence memory. Adaptive systems can implement spacing and interleaving policies and can schedule tests as retrieval practice (Thompson & Hughes, 2023). Chatbots can prompt elaboration and self-explanation that deepen semantic processing (Neumann et al., 2021). Multimodal or immersive systems can provide spatial and contextual cues that may aid recognition and associative links (Essoe et al., 2022). At the same time, tools that provide answers on demand can displace retrieval, reduce desirable difficulty, and risk shallow encoding (Donet et al., 2025).

Despite the rapid growth of AI in education and several broad syntheses of effectiveness, a focused account of memory outcomes remains underdeveloped. Evidence synthesis was hindered in prior reviews as they typically aggregate heterogeneous measures under general labels such as achievement or learning, or they emphasize usability and engagement rather than cognitive outcomes per se (Shanmugasundaram & Tamilarasu, 2023). Immediate post-instruction tests are often the sole outcome, which makes it difficult to separate short-term performance from durable retention. Moreover, studies rarely classify effects by the specific memory process targeted. Gains may reflect more effective encoding, better scheduled retrieval, reduced extraneous load that frees working memory, or some combination of these mechanisms, yet most syntheses do not map results to those constructs. Reviews of chatbots and LLM supports emphasize promise for study help, but also note short follow-up intervals and inconsistent assessment formats, which limit inferences about delayed retention. Meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring and augmented reality demonstrate mean benefits for learning, but they seldom differentiate immediate recall from delayed retention or identify which design features most consistently support memory processes across educational levels (Akchayir & Akchayir, 2017; Garzón & Acevedo, 2019; Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Okonkwo & Ade-Ibijola, 2021; Wollny et al., 2021). This gap motivates a synthesis that treats memory as the primary outcome and that links observed effects to theoretically grounded mechanisms.

The review has three objectives. The first objective is to estimate and describe the effects of AI-enabled tools on memory across immediate and delayed assessments, with attention to educational level and subject domain. The second objective is to classify findings by the memory process plausibly targeted by each intervention, namely encoding, storage, or retrieval, and to relate those classifications to design features such as feedback type, adaptivity, spacing or interleaving schedules, modality, and the presence of complementary strategies such as self-testing or structured note-taking. The third objective is to appraise methodological features that influence the credibility and interpretation of memory outcomes, including the alignment between intended mechanisms and outcome measures, the presence and timing of delayed tests, and design characteristics such as randomization, attrition, and implementation fidelity. These objectives translate into the following research questions, stated to support transparent synthesis.

1\. How do AI-enabled educational tools affect learners’ memory across short-term performance,

immediate retention, and delayed retention in formal educational settings? 2. Which memory processes are most consistently supported by different AI designs, and under what

conditions do benefits, null effects, or apparent trade-offs occur? 3. Which design and implementation features moderate memory outcomes, and to what extent do

complementary study strategies account for observed differences?

By centering memory as the primary outcome and by grounding analysis in established cognitive theory, this review aims to produce actionable guidance for instructional designers and educators who are deciding how to integrate AI while safeguarding the cognitive outcomes that matter for long-term learning. The contribution is twofold. It separates immediate from delayed outcomes so that claims about effectiveness are not conflated with short-term performance, and it maps effects to specific memory mechanisms so that recommendations can be aligned with the design features that plausibly drive durable retention.

2\. Methodology

2.1. Review Design

This review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The choice of a systematic review design was made to provide a transparent, replicable, and comprehensive synthesis of the empirical literature examining the impact of AI tools on learners’ memory. By adopting a systematic approach, the review sought to move beyond anecdotal or selective evidence and instead provide a structured overview of the existing body of work. The study was guided by a protocol that specified research questions, eligibility criteria, search strategies, data extraction procedures, and quality appraisal methods. The review paid particular attention to memory processes (encoding, storage, retrieval) and memory stages (working, long-term, and immediate vs. delayed retention), given their centrality in cognitive theories of learning and their importance for educational practice.

2.2. Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in consultation with the existing literature and best practices in systematic reviewing. Electronic searches were carried out across seven major databases: Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, PsycINFO, ScienceDirect, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar. The time frame was restricted to publications between January 2022 and December 2025 to reflect the rapid emergence of AI tools in education during the past decade. Boolean operators and truncations were employed to maximize coverage and capture variations of the central concepts. The search string

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established a priori. To be eligible, studies had to:

1\. Report empirical research, whether quantitative (randomized controlled trials, quasi-experiments,

experiments, or observational studies) or mixed-methods designs. 2. Focus explicitly on the relationship between the use of AI tools and learners’ memory outcomes,

Exclusion criteria eliminated studies that were conceptual or opinion-based, those that investigated AI without measuring memory outcomes, non-peer-reviewed materials (such as reports or dissertations), or studies that addressed general learning performance without a specific memory component.

2.4. Study Selection Process

All retrieved records were exported into Mendeley for reference management and duplicate removal. The selection was conducted in two stages. First, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, with clearly irrelevant studies excluded at this stage. Second, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were obtained and assessed in detail to confirm their inclusion. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, ensuring reliability in the selection process.

2.5. Data Extraction

A structured data extraction form was developed to ensure consistency and completeness in capturing study characteristics. The form included: author(s) and year, country, AI tool or system investigated, educational setting and level, study design and sample size, targeted memory process or stage, outcome type (objective or subjective), outcome timing (immediate or delayed), key findings, and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) quality score. Extraction was conducted manually by the lead reviewer and independently verified by a second reviewer to reduce errors.

2.6. Quality Appraisal

The MMAT, 2018 version (Hong et al., 2018) was applied to all included studies. MMAT was chosen for its flexibility in appraising different study designs within a single framework. Each study was scored from 0 to 5 depending on how many of the five criteria were met. Studies were not excluded on the basis of quality scores; instead, the ratings informed the interpretation of evidence, particularly in highlighting methodological strengths and weaknesses in the literature.

2.7. Data Synthesis

Because the included studies were heterogeneous in their AI interventions, research designs, outcome measures, and educational contexts, a narrative synthesis was adopted rather than a meta-analysis. Findings were organized by (1) study design, (2) AI tool type, (3) targeted memory process or stage, and (4) educational level. Attention was also given to immediate versus delayed outcomes and to objective versus subjective measures. This approach allowed for the identification of patterns, divergences, and thematic insights across the 26 studies.

3\. Results

3.1. Overview of Included Studies

A total of 26 empirical records were included in the synthesis, representing 25 unique studies (one randomized trial is reported twice). Research was conducted across China, Turkey, the United States, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Malaysia, Pakistan/UK, Greece, Ethiopia, India, Australia, and New Zealand, reflecting varied educational settings. Most studies were situated in higher education, with two in K– 12 (a Grade-6 social studies chatbot study and a K–3 dialogic reading study) and one adult laboratory study. Study designs ranged from randomized trials and quasi-experiments to controlled or within- subjects classroom experiments, surveys, and learning analytics evaluations. Sample sizes varied considerably, from n = 16 in a laboratory experiment to approximately n = 700 in a learning-analytics evaluation, with the largest single randomized trial at n = 187.

Interventions most commonly featured large-language-model chatbots or conversational agents that delivered prompts, feedback, or dialogic support in course contexts, especially in language learning and medical/STEM instruction. Classic intelligent tutoring systems, VR/simulation platforms, and algorithmic practice-scheduling tools were not characteristic of this 26- study set.

3.3. Memory Processes and Stages

Outcomes most often indexed retention (immediate and delayed), with several studies also examining transfer. Working memory was rarely assessed directly, and sensory memory was not targeted. Many interventions combined processes, for example, scaffolding encoding and then assessing retention, or measuring both retention and transfer after instruction.

3.4. Educational Contexts

The evidence base is concentrated in higher education, spanning EFL/ESL, medical and health sciences, engineering, and teacher education. The two K–12 studies focused on vocabulary/reading and social studies, with one including a dialogic reading agent for early primary learners and the other using a course chatbot with a delayed “permanence” test. One adult laboratory study investigated writing and recall while manipulating access to an LLM.

3.5. Impacts on Learners’ Memory

Across designs and contexts, effects on memory-relevant outcomes are predominantly positive, with several randomized and quasi-experimental classroom studies showing improvements in immediate retention and, crucially, evidence of durability at delayed assessments (Abdelhalim & Alsehibany, 2025; Gan et al., 2024; Yetishensoy & Karaduman, 2024; Zhang & Huang, 2024). AI-generated instructional videos matched or exceeded recorded lectures on learning performance and produced advantages on delayed retention in some cases (Pellas, 2025; Xu et al., 2024). AI-generated mnemonic images helped maintain performance when control groups showed decline over time, consistent with improved long-term retention (Agnes & Srinivasan, 2024; Alomar et al., 2025).

Chatbot feedback mattered qualitatively: affective feedback yielded better retention than neutral feedback, and metacognitive feedback supported transfer (Yin et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2024). Course- level integrations of LLM support were associated with higher short-term test scores and, in some cases, downstream course-exam performance, while dialogic reading with a bilingual conversational agent improved comprehension with benefits that held at delay (Gan et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025; Yusof, 2025). Vocabulary learning studies consistently reported gains in immediate and delayed retention when chatbots or AI-generated mnemonic supports were used (Abdelhalim & Alsehibany, 2025; Agnes & Srinivasan, 2024; Chen, 2025; Maghsoudi, 2025; Zhang & Huang, 2024).

At the same time, mixed findings appeared when the comparison involved already effective human- curated resources: for example, one three-arm randomized trial found no clear one-week retention advantage for ChatGPT-supported study relative to other high-quality resources despite higher initial scores (Kalam et al., 2025). 3.6. Methodological Quality, Gaps, and Limitations

Methodological appraisal using MMAT (2018) indicates that randomized trials and well-designed quasi-experiments generally showed moderate to high quality, while several classroom experiments and observational designs exhibited common limitations such as small samples, incomplete reporting of allocation procedures, short follow-up windows (often one to two weeks or a single course cycle), and reliance on proximal academic assessments rather than standardized memory batteries. The evidence is geographically concentrated and higher-education-heavy, with relatively few K–12 investigations and limited representation from regions such as Africa and Latin America. Process coverage is uneven: retention and, to a lesser degree, transfer are well represented, whereas working memory is rarely examined, and sensory memory is absent. Heterogeneity in outcome measures, delay intervals, and task domains complicates cross-study comparisons and precludes formal aggregation without strong assumptions.

4\. Discussion

The present systematic review synthesized empirical evidence on how AI tools influence learners’ memory processes and stages in educational contexts. Drawing on 26 core empirical studies, the discussion integrates the findings with established theories of memory and learning. This approach not only clarifies where empirical outcomes align with theoretical expectations but also illuminates points of divergence that warrant further scrutiny. In doing so, the review highlights both the pedagogical opportunities of AI, such as its potential to enhance encoding, storage, and retrieval, and the ethical challenges that arise when technologies alter traditional learning dynamics.

Overall, the evidence suggests that AI applications can enhance memory encoding, storage, and retrieval through adaptive scaffolding, multimodal presentation, and real-time feedback (Ma & Chen, 2025; Naseer & Khawaja, 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2025). At the same time, the findings reveal contradictions: while some studies show robust long-term memory gains (Abdelhalim & Alsehibany, 2025; Alomar et al., 2025; Yusof, 2025), others highlight risks of cognitive offloading, over-reliance, or superficial learning (Abbas, 2024; Kalam et al., 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025). Situating these findings within theoretical traditions of cognitive psychology, educational technology, and neuroscience helps to clarify the mechanisms at play and chart future research directions.

At the level of encoding, the reviewed studies consistently indicate that AI tools can enrich learners’ attentional and perceptual engagement (Dai & Wu, 2025; Sayed et al., 2024; Vidanaralage et al., 2022). The classic Atkinson–Shiffrin (1968) multi-store model emphasized that encoding requires effective transfer from sensory memory to short-term working memory. AI platforms, particularly those employing natural language processing and multimodal interfaces, appear to optimize this transfer by tailoring stimuli to learners’ prior knowledge and preferred modes of representation. For instance, adaptive intelligent tutoring systems provide personalized prompts that align with Mayer’s (2020) cognitive theory of multimedia learning, where dual coding of visual and verbal inputs fosters deeper encoding. Several of the empirical studies in the dataset (e.g., Bai et al., 2023) demonstrated that chatbots and AI-driven visualizations improved learners’ ability to encode complex material, especially when combined with retrieval-based questioning. Parallel insights from review work on embodied AI (Pataranutaporn et al., 2022) reinforce the idea that rich sensory engagement aids initial encoding.

Working memory theories provide further explanatory power for understanding these effects. Baddeley’s (1974, 2000) model, with its central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and episodic buffer, suggests that instructional tools must balance rather than overload working memory

This suggests that AI is not inherently beneficial; rather, its alignment with cognitive architecture determines its effectiveness.

Long-term retention is perhaps the most contested domain. The levels-of-processing framework (Craik & Lockhart, 1972) posits that deeper semantic processing yields more durable memory traces. Several studies in the dataset confirm that AI tools can foster deeper elaboration through personalized questioning, reflection prompts, or Socratic dialogue (Chen et al., 2025; Younis et al., 2024). Dergaa et al. (2024) similarly reported that AI-supported medical simulations promoted conceptual integration that translated into long-term retention. On the other hand, Bond et al. (2024) and Jose et al. (2025) warn that constant AI scaffolding may encourage superficial reliance, with learners failing to internalize information in a way that supports independent retrieval. This aligns with Sparrow et al.’s (2011) concept of the “Google effect,” whereby information stored externally reduces the learner’s motivation to consolidate knowledge internally. Integrating these insights suggests that the boundary condition is the extent to which AI promotes generative processing rather than passive consumption.

Theories of retrieval practice further contextualize these findings. Roediger and Karpicke’s (2006) “testing effect” emphasizes that active recall strengthens long-term memory more than passive review. Multiple studies in the review (e.g., Kang et al., 2025; Mustafa et al., 2024) illustrate how AI-driven quiz engines and chat interfaces can operationalize this principle, repeatedly challenging learners to retrieve knowledge in varying contexts. Similarly, the findings of Wang and Fan (2025) and Lan (2025) align with Bjork’s theory of desirable difficulties (Bjork, 1994), showing that AI systems introducing slightly more challenging retrieval conditions—such as interleaving or delayed testing—enhanced retention relative to simpler drills. Importantly, though, not all AI systems incorporate such pedagogical sophistication; as Shanmugasundaram and Tamilarasu (2023) and Khalid et al. (2024) observed, some chatbot-based tools default to surface-level repetition, which risks creating illusions of competence without strengthening retrieval pathways.

Beyond these cognitive models, social and constructivist theories shed light on the interactive dynamics between learners and AI. Vygotsky’s (1978) concept of the zone of proximal development suggests that effective learning occurs when scaffolding targets just beyond current competence. AI systems employing adaptive feedback exemplify this principle, as in the studies by Martin et al. (2025) and Li and Balinas (2025), which demonstrated that personalized hinting and pacing improved both performance and self-efficacy. Likewise, collaborative AI environments examined by Gkintoni et al. (2025) and Oakley et al. (2025) illustrate how dialogic interaction fosters co-construction of knowledge. These findings suggest that AI, when designed as a responsive peer or tutor, can instantiate social constructivist learning processes. However, Moroianu et al. (2023) and Murphy et al. (2025) caution that reliance on AI interlocutors may reduce opportunities for genuine human collaboration, raising questions about the social dimension of memory building.

Dual-coding theory (Paivio, 1986) and Mayer’s multimedia principles (2020) are particularly relevant for understanding how AI generates multimodal learning experiences. Multiple studies in the dataset (e.g., Chen et al., 2025; Nizamani, 2024) showed that AI-generated visuals paired with verbal explanations strengthened both immediate recall and delayed transfer tasks, consistent with dual- channel processing. Similarly, Granda et al. (2024) demonstrated that AI-produced augmented reality environments supported medical students’ integration of visuospatial and semantic information, enhancing storage and retrieval. Yet, as Kohnke et al. (2023) and Halkiopoulos and Gkintoni (2025) emphasize, over-reliance on flashy visuals without integration into coherent instructional design can produce cognitive overload, diluting rather than enhancing memory effects. Thus, the relationship between multimodality and memory hinges on thoughtful pedagogical orchestration.
