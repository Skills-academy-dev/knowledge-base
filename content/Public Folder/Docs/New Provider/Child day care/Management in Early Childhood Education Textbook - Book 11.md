1.1 Introduction
The landscape of Early Childhood Education (ECE) in South Africa is diverse, with institutions
varying widely in terms of resources and challenges. While some have ample support, others
manage with basic amenities and may experience difficulties such as power shortages and lack of
connectivity. Addressing these disparities is crucial for improving education management within
ECE institutions. The focus here is not only on elevating teaching and learning experiences but also
on the collaborative efforts of all stakeholders, including staff, children, teachers, and parents, to
foster an environment conducive to effective ECE.
An examination of national and provincial policies and laws suggests a need for a refreshed
approach to governance and management across all levels of the educational system. This chapter
provides a foundational understanding of education management theories, the various management
styles, and the role of a school principal as a leader and an administrator. Information in this chapter
is pertinent to all three ECE stages: Infants and Toddlers, Pre-Grade R (pre-primary phase), and the
Foundation Phase (grades R to 3). The discussion includes the management responsibilities of ECE
centre managers, school principals, and department heads, all of whom share the common goal of
managerial excellence in education.
Education in the ECE sector is distinct, shaped by the leadership abilities of its managers and
principals. These roles are guided by a philosophy and ethos that aim to achieve educational
objectives through planning, organising, leading, and overseeing all facets of education, in
accordance with established policies and professional standards.
This chapter introduces key management concepts within the ECE framework, aiming not to be
exhaustive but to offer a solid starting point for understanding managerial functions. The intent is to
enable educators and students to apply these management principles throughout their engagement
with this book and to refine their expertise through practical application.
1.2 Management and education management in ECE
Hersey, Blanchard, and Johnson define management as the art of effectively utilising resources,
including personnel and funding, to achieve the goals of an organisation. The term 'management' in
the educational context is applicable to various organisational structures, ranging from business
enterprises to educational and social entities. As outlined by the Department of Education, in the
realm of Early Childhood Education, management involves an inclusive strategy that addresses
policy creation and program implementation for children from their infancy until nine years old. This
approach requires the active involvement of parents and guardians to ensure the protection and
development of the child's cognitive, emotional, social, and physical capacities.
Within Early Childhood Education settings, management is a sequence of coordinated activities
undertaken by ECE centre managers, school principals, heads of departments, or classroom
teachers. These educators collaborate with individuals and groups, leveraging various resources, to
fulfil educational objectives for children up to nine years of age. Achieving these educational goals
requires a collective effort, involving educators, students, administrative staff, parents, and the
community.
Therefore, the autonomous management of ECE centres and schools is crucial. This autonomy is
key to realising educational goals effectively and fostering an environment that supports the holistic
development of children.

1.3 Self-management of ECE centres and schools
According to the South African Schools Act of 1996, educational institutions are increasingly
expected to take charge of their internal management. The Act enables schools to make
independent decisions, particularly concerning resource allocation, while emphasising that these
self-managed schools must still answer to a higher educational authority regarding their
administrative practices.
Caldwell and Spinks describe a self-managed school as one that has been granted the authority to
make its own decisions, especially in the distribution of resources. Despite this autonomy, the
school must ensure transparency and maintain accountability to the overseeing educational bodies.
The movement towards autonomous management within schools does not inherently guarantee
successful outcomes. The effectiveness of such a management approach is contingent upon the
internal management's quality and nature. It is crucial for leaders in the ECE sector, including
directors, principals, and department heads, to be well-versed in management styles that serve the
best interests of all children and learners within the South African educational context.
To navigate the complexities of autonomous ECE management successfully, it is essential for those
in leadership positions, such as ECE directors and school principals, to cultivate a distinctive and
effective management style that aligns with the goals of providing quality education.
1.4 Management styles
In the context of management within Early Childhood Education (ECE), different leadership styles
are employed, each with its own approach and impact on the organisation's culture and
effectiveness. This section discusses management/leadership styles such as autocratic,
laissez-faire, and democratic, along with their potential outcomes within educational settings.
An autocratic leadership style is characterised by decision-making concentrated in the hands of
the leader, with little to no input from others. This approach can lead to a well-ordered environment
but may also result in a lack of creative input and low morale among staff who may feel undervalued
and unheard.
The laissez-faire leadership style is quite the opposite, offering complete freedom to staff to make
decisions and manage their responsibilities with minimal guidance. While this can foster
independence and personal growth, it may also lead to inconsistencies and lack of direction if not
managed carefully.
Democratic leadership, on the other hand, involves all stakeholders in the decision-making
process, including educators, staff, parents, and sometimes even the children. This style
encourages shared responsibility and collective input, which can lead to a more motivated staff and
innovative solutions to problems. However, the challenge arises when too many opinions lead to
indecision or slow progress.
No single management style is perfect; each has its own set of advantages and challenges.
Effective management in ECE settings often requires a blend of styles, tailored to the situation and
the individuals involved. School principals and ECE directors play a pivotal role in choosing and
adapting these styles to create a productive and nurturing environment for both staff and children.
The success of this blended approach depends on various factors, including the specific needs of
the ECE centre, the individuals involved, and the broader educational goals.

These management styles are intricately linked with all the roles and functions of the ECE school
principal or teacher, influencing the daily operations and long-term success of the educational
institution.
1.5 The managerial functions of the manager/school principal
A school principal must adeptly coordinate, direct, and oversee the myriad activities within a school
to ensure order and address disciplinary issues, or to pivot strategies if initial approaches prove
ineffective. It's evident that educational management tasks such as planning, organising, and
directing are integral to the daily functions of educators.
Rarely do school principals enter a position where all decisions have been resolved and challenges
completely addressed. They are often at the helm of ongoing decision-making processes, crucial to
the fluid operation of an Early Childhood Education (ECE) centre or school. Principals are expected
to master and apply four core management functions:
 Planning: Establishing a roadmap for the school's future actions.
 Organising: Structuring the school's resources effectively to execute plans.
 Leading: Inspiring and motivating the educational community to achieve school goals.
 Controlling: Monitoring progress and making necessary adjustments to stay on track.
These functions are indispensable in maintaining a dynamic and responsive educational
environment where both children and teachers can thrive.
1.5.1 The school principal as a planner
In the realm of educational management, the school principal's role is multifaceted, encompassing
the comprehensive observation of the school's environment, including its people, resources, and
activities. Strategic planning stands at the forefront of these responsibilities, serving as the
foundational layer upon which further management tasks such as problem-solving and policy
development rest. Effective planning is instrumental, particularly for navigating the complexities of
an ever-evolving educational landscape and for setting a course towards the school's long-term
success.
Practical planning within a school may take several forms, including:
 Detailed and documented planning that aligns with established policy guidelines.
 Forward-looking strategies that encompass immediate actions (daily or weekly plans) and
more extended forecasts (annual planning), ensuring the school's objectives are met.
 Comprehensive oversight of projects, ensuring they integrate smoothly with the school's
mission and contribute to its overarching goals.
In the intricate process of decision-making, a principal must engage in thorough analysis, weighing
various alternatives and their potential impacts. This involves not only identifying and resolving
problems but also continually assessing the effectiveness of decisions and making adjustments as
needed. A principal's distinctive decision-making approach is often a reflection of their personal
leadership style, tailored to the unique dynamics of their educational environment. The skill of
decision-making is not static; it requires adaptation and flexibility to ensure that every solution aligns
with the school's commitment to providing quality education.

1.5.2 The school principal as an organiser
A school principal's role transcends instructional leadership to include the crucial task of organising.
This encompasses structuring the school's operations and delineating how resources and personnel
are to be allocated to achieve desired outcomes. This task demands strategic foresight in planning,
coordinating team efforts, establishing effective communication channels, and setting rules and
regulations to guide the school's day-to-day and long-term functions.
Effective organisation requires putting plans into action, which involves detailing the tasks, assigning
the right individuals to those tasks, and setting timelines. This structure is not only about maintaining
order but also about optimising the school's operations to enhance efficiency and productivity.
A key aspect of organising is delegation, which involves assigning authority and responsibility to
other staff members. Delegation serves several purposes: it allows principals to focus on critical
management tasks, it can increase the volume of work that can be accomplished, and it can
empower staff, leading to heightened job satisfaction and motivation.
However, delegation can be challenging for some managers due to various concerns, such as
reluctance to relinquish control, fear of underperformance, or concerns about the quality of
delegated work. These challenges, ranging from perfectionism to biases about staff capabilities, can
hinder effective delegation. It is crucial for school principals to recognize these potential obstacles
and develop strategies to overcome them, thereby fostering a more collaborative and empowered
school environment.
1.5.3 The school principal as a leader
Leadership is the third pillar in the multifaceted role of the school principal. This involves guiding the
entire school community—staff and students alike—toward the achievement of common educational
goals. The process includes forming a solid organisational infrastructure, entrusting responsibilities,
and synchronising various school functions. Leadership transcends managerial duties; it entails
embodying certain intrinsic qualities that set leaders apart. Effective leaders are not only navigators
but also pioneers and participants in the school's journey. They unite their team with a shared sense
of purpose and direction, earning trust and commitment through supportive and motivational
leadership.

Defining Leadership
In the context of South African ECE, the challenge of leadership is the cultivation of inherent
leadership qualities within the educational framework. Sterling and Davidoff emphasise ten
leadership tenets vital to this sector. These tenets include:
 Embracing a holistic view of the educational landscape.
 Vitalising the school's core values.
 Cultivating and communicating a shared vision.

 Reframing challenges as opportunities for growth and learning.
 Recognising and valuing the contributions of the entire school community.
 Leading with agility and adaptability to meet various needs.
 Maintaining a balanced and efficient operational flow.
 Valuing creative thought and open dialogue.
 Upholding integrity as a non-negotiable principle.
 Exemplifying the school's aspirations and standards.
These principles form the essence of effective leadership within the school setting, guiding the
principal's approach to their role.
The school principal as an implementer of official documents
In the realm of Early Childhood Education (ECE), the role of the school principal extends beyond
administrative duties to include the vital task of enacting policies and strategic plans. A principal's
leadership is comprehensive, encompassing every aspect of a school's operation. They provide
expert guidance to teachers, mentor students in their educational journey, and foster active
engagement with families and the wider community. Despite potential diversification of roles within
the school, the principal maintains an overarching supervisory responsibility, ensuring that the
institution's objectives are pursued with commitment and educational integrity.
Ten fundamental ideas on leadership
Drawing from the insights of renowned ECE advocate Horswell, ten core leadership principles
emerge as critical to effective governance across the various stages of ECE:
 Influence: True leaders wield the power to reshape perspectives and guide new courses of
action.
 Communication: A leader's ability to forge connections hinges on clear, impactful
communication.
 Time Management: Effective leaders are masters of time, prioritising tasks to maximise
efficiency.
 Relational: Leaders foster deep, authentic connections, showing sincere care beyond mere
formalities.
 Visionary: The capacity to envision and articulate future goals is the hallmark of a visionary
leader.
 Exemplary: Leaders lead by example, demonstrating personal growth and the pursuit of
excellence.
 Resilience: A tenacious spirit enables leaders to withstand challenges and persist with
determination.
 Team-Building: Recognising that collective talents exceed individual capabilities, leaders
build strong teams.
 Performance-Oriented: Such leaders inspire high standards, celebrating achievements and
fostering ambition.
 Self-Improvement: A commitment to continual self-development keeps leaders at the
forefront of progress.
These principles serve as a foundation for principals who seek to inspire and guide their schools
through the ever-changing landscape of education.

Core interpersonal skills that leaders need
Interpersonal skills are the fundamental tools that leaders use to engage and collaborate with others
effectively. Possessing strong interpersonal skills, leaders are adept at fostering team cohesion and
engaging in meaningful one-on-one interactions. Typically, leaders with robust interpersonal abilities
are perceived as optimistic, serene, and confident, radiating positivity that can invigorate an entire
educational community.
Key interpersonal competencies for leaders include:
 Communication: Effective leaders are skilled communicators, capable of clearly articulating
ideas and concepts both in writing and speech. They excel in transforming complex ideas into
understandable messages, facilitating understanding and driving forward school initiatives.
 Mentorship: Quality mentorship involves offering guidance and support precisely when it's
needed. Leaders in education are responsible for instituting mentorship programs that
cultivate the growth of new staff, sharing expertise that bolsters professional development and
fosters confidence.
 Collaboration: Teamwork is central to a leader's ability to unite a group around shared goals.
It involves harnessing the collective strengths of team members and directing them towards
collaborative success. Leaders must navigate team dynamics, resolve misunderstandings,
and sustain motivation, ensuring that the team's collective efforts are goal-oriented and
inspired.
 Decision-Making: Leadership demands the capacity to make informed decisions, often
amidst complex and challenging circumstances. The ability to discern and choose the most
beneficial path forward is critical, and each decision must be weighed with the broader school
objectives in mind. A leader's decision-making is informed by a thorough understanding of the
situation at hand, the potential impact of the decision, and the needs of those involved.
 Inspiration and Motivation: Leaders merge communication prowess with motivational ability,
inspiring educators to strive for excellence. They understand that motivation is deeply
personal and varies from individual to individual. By fostering a culture of achievement and
positivity, leaders encourage staff to seek innovative solutions and pursue quality in their work,
leading to a more engaged, dynamic, and productive educational environment.
School principals as ethical leaders
School principals are often seen as the moral compasses of their educational communities. Their
commitment to ethical leadership has become increasingly significant in light of concerns over the
conduct within the teaching profession. Acknowledging the damage caused by unethical practices,
as noted by former Minister of Basic Education Angie Motshekga, underscores the need for
principals to lead with integrity.
In the fabric of society, leadership is not just about direction and achievement; it's about embodying
a mission that reflects shared values. Ethical leaders lay the groundwork for the realisation of
collective goals and shape the policies that guide societal conduct.
An ethical leader stands as a pillar of trustworthiness and empathy, engaging with others in a
manner that fosters mutual respect and openness to diverse perspectives. Such leaders are seen
as exemplars who not only set standards but also live by them, inspiring others to adopt a shared
ethical vision. Their leadership goes beyond transactions and tasks; it's about cultivating an
environment where confidence and job satisfaction flourish through genuine encouragement and
support.

As ethical leaders, school principals are expected to integrate their values into every aspect of their
leadership. This involves making decisions that are not only effective but also morally sound,
reflecting the virtues of honesty, justice, and integrity. They are charged with the responsibility of
being role models, demonstrating steadfast commitment to their ethical convictions in all
professional interactions.
The role of a principal as an ethical leader is to consistently demonstrate through actions and
choices that they are guided by a moral compass. They strive to make choices that align with the
greater good, aspiring to ideals of nobility and purity, even in the face of challenges. Their
leadership is characterised by a steadfast dedication to the principles they cherish, manifesting a
commitment to truth and ethical standards in every facet of their role.
Africanisation and leadership: servant leadership
The concept of servant leadership, with roots extending back over millennia, views the leader's role
as that of a service provider first and foremost. Kent (2016) articulates that servant leaders prioritise
the needs of their team, ensuring that the highest priority needs of colleagues and the community
are addressed. This leadership approach is more than a style; it is a philosophy that shapes the
management of organisations.
In the African context, servant leadership resonates deeply, emphasising the cultural value placed
on community and service. It's an approach where the leader assumes a supportive role, facilitating
a collaborative environment that empowers others. Key traits of servant leadership include:
 Attentive Listening: Servant leaders are committed to understanding the perspectives of
others, demonstrating active listening as a foundational leadership skill.
 Empathy: By empathising, servant leaders connect with their team on a profound level,
understanding and sharing their experiences and concerns.
 Healing: They are dedicated to resolving interpersonal strife and promoting a healthy work
environment, contributing to the overall wellness of their team.
 Persuasion Over Coercion: Persuasion is preferred over authoritative commands, facilitating
a consensus-driven approach that respects individual voices.
 Visionary Outlook: A servant leader is visionary, laying out a clear path forward for the
organisation that aligns with collective goals.
 Foresight: They use past experiences to anticipate future outcomes, planning proactively to
meet upcoming challenges.
 Stewardship: Servant leaders are stewards of their organisation, earning trust and respect by
prioritising the interests of the team.
 Disciplined Approach: They exemplify self-discipline in their actions, promoting a culture of
integrity and trustworthiness.
 Ethical Mindfulness: A servant leader remains constantly aware of ethical standards and
professional conduct, ensuring that their actions reflect these values.
In summary, authentic leadership, as outlined by Van Deventer (2003), encapsulates a series of
impactful traits. An educational leader is not only a strategic guide and an inspirer but also a unifier,
a role model, and a catalyst for change. They embody the principles they stand for, influencing the
school culture and the broader community.
While leadership and management can be seen as two sides of the same coin, with their distinct
features, they are often intertwined. Managers are tasked with ensuring that operations are
executed correctly, while leaders are charged with setting the right direction and inspiring progress.

1.5.4 The school principal as a controller
The role of the school principal encompasses the crucial responsibility of quality control within
educational activities. This involves an array of methods to monitor and ensure that the institution's
operations align with its strategic objectives. The principal is key in verifying that the school remains
on its intended course and, when necessary, implementing corrective actions. This oversight is a
core aspect of the principal's role, ensuring that all planned actions contribute positively to the
school's mission.
Principals are tasked with guaranteeing that:
 Established standards and quality benchmarks for educational activities are achieved.
 Student performances and staff achievements are evaluated against these set standards.
 Appropriate and timely corrective strategies are enacted when deviations are identified.
