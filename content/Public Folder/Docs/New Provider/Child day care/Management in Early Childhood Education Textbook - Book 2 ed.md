9.1 Introduction
Effective teaching begins with careful and thoughtful planning. This chapter will explore key issues
to consider when creating educational plans for young children, whether in a nursery setting for
babies and toddlers, an Early Childhood Education (ECE) centre, or the more structured
environment of the Foundation Phase in primary schools.
The first thing to think about is the children you're teaching. Without a deep understanding of young
children, you won't be able to engage them in meaningful learning, and they won't be able to grasp
the lessons successfully. Knowing what activities are right for their age and stage of development is
vital.
9.2 Planning for learning and development: developmentally appropriate
practices
There's been much discussion about whether we should strictly follow specific experts when
planning for children's development and learning. But one thing is clear: the theories on child
development give us valuable insights that guide many decisions we make in teaching young
children.
To create the best learning environment, you must consider the individual needs of each child. By
understanding how children grow and learn at different stages, you can ensure they have the right
space, equipment, and activities to make the most of their education.
9.2.1 How knowledge of child development specifically helps teachers
Understanding child development is key for teachers to create effective learning environments.
Here's why it's so beneficial:
 Spotting and Preventing Risks: Teachers can identify potential dangers specific to
children's ages. For example, a two-year-old might try to climb everything, so teachers need to
make their spaces safe for adventurous little explorers but still challenging enough for older
children.
 Observing Growth: Teachers can monitor kids more closely, recognising if a child is
progressing as expected or if their behaviour is unusual. This helps them to support each
child's unique journey.
 Setting Realistic Expectations: When teachers understand what children typically do at
certain ages, they can set appropriate activities. It wouldn't be fair, for instance, to expect an
18-month-old to share toys all the time. Teachers learn what to expect at each stage, which
helps them plan better.
 Tailoring Activities: Teachers can design activities that match the different interests and
needs of children at various stages of development.
 Effective Communication: Teachers can talk to parents with confidence about their
children's progress and behaviours, providing clear reasons and understanding.
Knowing about child development means teachers can support each child to learn and grow in the
best way possible.

9.2.2 Principles of development which underpin planning for learning
When planning how to teach children, it's important to understand some key terms about their
growth and learning:
 Growth: This is all about the physical changes that you can see and measure, like how tall a
child gets or how much they weigh. During infancy and again during puberty, children can
grow quickly. This growth is part of their genetic plan but can be affected by things like not
getting enough food or being sick.
 Development: This term means getting better at things both in the mind and body. As children
grow, they learn to move, think, feel, and interact with others in more complex ways. This
development starts from the time they are born and continues as they grow up.
 Maturation: This word describes how children's behaviour changes naturally over time. These
changes let them learn new and more complicated skills and are part of what they inherit from
their parents.
 Learning: Learning can mean different things, but here it's about gaining knowledge through
trying new things and having experiences. It's connected to how the brain grows and
develops, especially the parts that let us think deeply. Learning isn't just studying; it's an active
process that happens while children play and interact with the world.
When creating learning programmes for young children, we use important principles from child
development theories. Here’s how they help us:
 Integrated Development: A child’s growth in social, emotional, physical, and intellectual
areas are all linked. What they learn in one area can help them in another. For example, as
little ones learn to walk and move, they can explore more. This new exploration helps their
brain grow because they’re seeing and doing new things.
 Orderly Development: Children develop in a sequence that we can expect. They start with
simple skills and gradually move to more complex ones. For instance, their language develops
from making simple sounds to saying full words. They also learn to use their hands better,
moving from holding things with their whole hand to using just their thumb and first finger.
 Cephalocaudal Pattern: This is just a fancy term that means as babies grow, they develop
control of their head, neck, and arms before their lower body. So, when a baby learns to hold
their head up, sit, and stand, they're following this head-to-toe pattern.
 Proximodistal Pattern: Children develop motor skills from the centre of their body outwards.
That's why a young child will learn to use their arms before they can manage more detailed
movements with their hands and fingers.
 Uniform Pattern: Each child grows at their own pace, but the sequence of development is
usually quite similar across all children. However, kids of the same age can still be very
different from each other, so it's important to look at each child as an individual.
 Hierarchical Integration: This means that simple skills develop first, which form the basis for
more complex skills. Just like building blocks, the simple skills come before the more
advanced ones.
 Independence of Systems: Different parts of a child's body develop at different rates. For
example, their physical abilities might advance faster than their language skills.
 Plasticity: Kids' brains are adaptable, especially when they're young. This means if a part of
the brain is damaged, other parts might learn to take over its job. The younger the child, the
more flexible the brain is.

 Practice Makes Perfect: When children learn new things, they need lots of chances to
practise to get better at these skills. But remember, pushing them too much or too quickly isn't
good – it's just as bad as not giving them enough practice.
 Just-Right Challenges: Kids grow the most when they're given tasks that are a bit harder
than what they're used to but not too difficult. This helps them succeed and feel good about
learning, which encourages them to keep trying new things.
 Safe Spaces for Best Learning: Children learn best when they feel safe and cared for. This
means they need a place where their physical needs (like being warm and not hungry) and
emotional needs (like feeling loved and understood) are met.
If these principles aren't followed, it's tough for teaching and learning to be effective. So, teachers
and principals should always use these guidelines to make sure the children have the best chance
to learn and develop.

9.2.3 Major characteristics of development from birth to the end of the Foundation
Phase
The first nine years of a child's life are filled with fast-paced growth across physical, social,
cognitive, and emotional areas. Although each child develops at their own pace, it's important to
recognise that challenges like poverty, malnutrition, or lack of care can affect their development.
Infant and Toddler Development
Babies start their lives depending entirely on adults. The first three years are a whirlwind of growth
and learning. By their first birthday, most have tripled their birth weight and are starting to move and
interact with the world. By two and a half years, they're walking and talking, and they're keen to play
with other children. Brain development is especially rapid during these years, with countless
connections forming as they learn and grow.
Preschool Development
As children reach three, their growth rate slows down, but their motor skills and social abilities keep
expanding. They love being active and learning to coordinate their movements more precisely.
Preschoolers also thrive on playing with friends and using language more fluently. Their thinking
becomes more advanced, allowing for imaginative play. This is also when they start to build their
self-identity, understanding more about their feelings, their relationships with others, and the wider
world.
Foundation Phase Development
During the Foundation Phase, which includes the first nine years of life, children show a remarkable
desire to learn and master new skills. This drive is evident in their enthusiasm for games and sports,
where they start to understand and follow more complex rules. At this stage, children's ability to
work together and be part of a group becomes more refined. They also gain greater control over
their bodies, allowing them to sit still and focus for longer periods, which is essential for more
structured learning tasks.
Remember, children's competence and readiness for school can differ greatly, often due to the
variety of experiences they've had in their early years. Therefore, teachers should provide a range of
activities to cater to the diverse needs of Grade R and Grade 1 students.

9.2.4 Characteristics of developmentally appropriate practices
When setting up learning environments, it’s important to base our approach on a deep
understanding of child development. This helps to ensure that our teaching methods meet the
unique needs of every child.
 Planning Learning with the Child in Mind: The first step in planning learning activities is to
consider the behavioural and developmental stage of the child. Developmentally appropriate
planning starts with the child as the focus, meaning that all decisions about the learning
content should be suited to their physical, cognitive, social, cultural, and language
development. It's not helpful, and can even be damaging, to push children to learn things
before they are physically and psychologically ready. Instead, teachers should design learning
activities that match what the children are ready to learn and grow into more challenging tasks
as the children develop.
 Individual and Culturally Sensitive Learning: Every child is unique, and so are their
abilities. A good learning programme respects these differences and doesn't set unrealistic
goals. It should also consider the cultural and social contexts of the children's lives, making
learning relevant and meaningful to children from all backgrounds.
 Flexible and Responsive Teaching: Learning should be flexible, allowing teachers to seize
'teachable moments' that arise from children's spontaneous interests. This makes learning
more engaging and connects new information to what children already know.
 Encouraging Active Involvement: Children should be encouraged to be physically and
mentally active. A balanced learning programme includes a mix of lively and quiet activities. A
play-oriented approach should be used to foster learning through exploration, questioning, and
creativity. This means children should be involved in hands-on activities that reflect real-life
experiences rather than relying solely on workbooks.
By considering these principles, educators can create an enriching learning environment that
supports children's natural curiosity and fosters a love for learning.
9.3 Managing the learning environment of infants and toddlers (birth to
three years)
This section is designed to illustrate how the developmental stages of babies and toddlers should
guide the organisation and planning of their learning environments. It’s important to create spaces
that support the growth and learning needs of children in this critical age range.
9.3.1 Planning for babies
When planning for children under one year old, it's essential to understand their developmental
needs and characteristics. This understanding helps us to shape the learning programmes and the
environment for the infants.
 Babies are learning to trust people around them. It’s crucial for those who care for babies to
respond quickly to their needs and cries, building a sense of security and trust.
11 | Page

 A baby's learning space should help them form a strong bond with their caregivers. This
means having a setting with comfortable chairs and soft spaces for cuddles and feeding. It’s
also about having routines that feel familiar and safe for the babies.
 Babies explore the world through their senses. Their environment should provide a variety of
sights, sounds, and textures to touch, without overwhelming them. Safe toys that make noise,
like rattles, and objects they can look at, like mirrors and colourful pictures, help stimulate their
development.
 As babies learn by putting things in their mouths, it's important to ensure all toys and objects
are safe and non-toxic. Regular checks for hazards and keeping everything clean are key to
keeping babies safe as they learn through taste and touch.
 Infants naturally learn to move their bodies independently. By their first year, they may begin
to crawl or even walk. Early Childhood Education (ECE) settings should ensure there's
enough open space with stable, uncluttered furniture to support infants as they develop im-
portant physical skills like rolling, reaching, crawling, and pulling themselves up to stand.
Caregivers should remember to limit how long infants stay in baby chairs or cots since too
much time in them can hinder their physical development. Instead, babies should have plenty
of 'tummy time' on the floor to strengthen their muscles and improve their ability to move
around. Walking rings are not recommended in ECE centres as they may affect the natural
development of infants' movement skills.

 Around the nine-month mark, babies start to grab and explore objects with precision, using
their index finger and thumb. It's important to have a range of clean, safe toys for them to
examine. Be sure there are no small, dangerous items within their reach that they could put in
their mouth.
 Communication begins early. Babies express themselves through cries, facial expressions,
and body movements, along with early attempts at language. Caregivers should engage with
these attempts by imitating the sounds babies make and interacting with them through actions
like blowing kisses or changing facial expressions.
 Babies are unique individuals who deserve respect. Even though they can't talk, caregivers
should try to understand and anticipate their needs, helping them try to solve problems before
stepping in. After assisting, explain what you did, like cleaning their face after eating.
 Babies grow and change quickly. To support their rapid development, caregivers need to
provide suitable activities, toys, and equipment that match their evolving abilities and interests
in their first year.
 By seven to eight months, babies realise that people and things still exist even when they can't
see them. Caregivers should introduce activities that encourage understanding of object
permanence, like peek-a-boo or interactive books. It's also a key time for recognising stranger
anxiety and separation distress, so a consistent and familiar caregiver can be a great comfort.
 As they grow, older infants take an interest in others and may accidentally hurt younger ones
or take their toys. It's crucial for caregivers to model and encourage gentle interactions,
praising good behaviour, and ensuring younger babies are safe from unintended harm.
 Lastly, older babies value the approval of adults. Caregivers should show their delight in the
baby's activities and achievements to build their confidence and self-esteem.
 Babies start reaching for and manipulating objects early on, and by about nine months, they
can pick up small items with their index finger and thumb. A variety of safe, washable toys
should be provided for babies to explore. Ensure there are no small, unsafe items that they
could swallow or choke on.
 Babies communicate through crying, facial expressions, body movements, and making early
sounds. Caregivers should respond to all communication efforts by babies, mimicking the
sounds they make, and waiting for them to respond back, creating a back-and-forth
interaction.
 Babies are individuals who need to be respected. Caregivers should try to interpret what
babies want while giving them a chance to solve their own problems before help is provided.
Tell them what will happen next, like wiping their face after a meal.
 Babies grow and develop quickly. Caregivers must ensure that the programme's activities,
toys, and equipment keep pace with the changes in the babies' growth and development.
 By about seven to eight months, babies start to understand that objects and people still exist
even when out of sight. Caregivers should plan activities that focus on object permanence and
be aware that separation anxiety may become evident at this age.

 Older babies are curious about others and may unintentionally hurt younger ones. Caregivers
should model gentle behaviour and ensure that younger infants are out of harm's way.
 Older babies need adult approval. Caregivers should show positive reactions to the babies'
activities, encouraging their development.
An effective infant programme ensures the following:
 Each baby has a personalised schedule that aligns with their home routine, allowing for
individual needs during sleep, mealtimes, and play, instead of a one-size-fits-all plan.
 Caregivers are warm-hearted and consistent, maintaining a low adult-to-child ratio. This allows
for the formation of strong, secure relationships between infants and one or two main
caregivers.
 Caregivers are attentive and respond promptly to crying, dedicating quality time to each baby
to foster a nurturing bond.
 A range of engaging sensory experiences is offered to stimulate learning in an environment
that feels like home.
 The surroundings and toys provided are safe and clean, and there's ample room for play. This
ensures children have the space and resources they need for well-rounded development.
9.3.2 Planning for toddlers
The toddler stage (13 months to about three years) is when children make great strides in motor,
language, and cognitive skills. To support their growth:
 Toddlers need ample space for movement. Provide safe equipment like low climbing frames,
slides, and soft balls for running, jumping, and climbing activities.
 Consistent and predictable routines help toddlers feel secure. Changes in the environment
should be minimal to avoid upsetting them.
 Their ability to communicate grows from about 20 words at 15 months to up to 950 words by
three years. Encourage language development with descriptive language and by
acknowledging their communication efforts. Help them express feelings with words, not
aggression.
 Toddlers are egocentric, viewing themselves as the centre of the universe. Teach empathy by
modelling caring behaviour and using positive instructions.
 Sharing can be challenging for young toddlers. They’re better at handing out items they don't
need at the moment. Introduce the concept of taking turns and provide multiple popular toys to
reduce conflict.
 Toddlers are still mastering self-control. They might not always follow rules, especially if
they're new. Keep safety rules clear and minimal, and ensure their play area is free from
hazards.
 Testing boundaries is common. Avoid power struggles by consistently enforcing limits and
redirecting their attention.
 As they get older, toddlers begin to understand symbolism through hands-on experiences.
Provide real items for them to explore and learn from.
 Toddlers prefer to do things themselves and can get upset if they're helped unnecessarily.
Allow them to try tasks within their ability and provide protective measures for potentially
messy activities.
14 | Page

 A toddler's need for independence can conflict with their need for help. Be patient and offer
comfort when they're stressed, sick, or tired.
 Toddlers’ attention spans can be short but extend when they're absorbed in an activity.
Choose engaging and suitable tasks for their age.
 Older toddlers are skilled at imitation. Ensure that they have good role models and provide
them with play materials like clothes and dolls for imaginative play.
 Sensory experiences are vital. Offer various materials like sand and play-dough for creative
and sensory play, both indoors and outdoors.
An effective toddler programme ensures:
 While there's a structured group plan for daily activities, caregivers also provide flexibility for
individual sleep, meal, and playtimes to accommodate each toddler's needs.
 Staff are patient and attentive, understanding the unique needs of toddlers, balancing their
need for support with their desire for independence.
 Any instances of aggressive behaviour are addressed promptly, with staff demonstrating and
encouraging positive interactions and communication.
 There are plenty of activities planned to nurture language development, including talking,
reading, and storytelling.
 A diverse range of sensory and motor activities is available to help toddlers learn with their
entire bodies, both indoors and outdoors.
 The environment is kept safe and clean, allowing toddlers to explore and learn without risk.
 There is ample space and a variety of play materials to support toddlers' development across
all areas.
 Toddlers have access to enough toys and materials, reducing the pressure to share before
they are developmentally ready to do so.
9.3.3 Checklist of the main environmental considerations required for planning a
baby and toddler unit
Creating a nurturing environment for babies and toddlers is crucial. It should not merely be a
downscaled preschool setting but tailored to their specific developmental stages. The planning
should include:
 Distinct areas for different types of play: spaces for active play, imaginative scenarios, messy
play, and quiet time to cater to all aspects of a toddler's growth.
 Soft furnishings and decorations that offer a homely feel while providing sensory stimuli.
 A stimulating setup that encourages curiosity and exploration with a range of items to see,
touch, hear, and smell.
 A stable and consistent setting where toddlers can find familiar toys and resources, helping
them feel secure.
 Stringent safety measures and vigilant supervision to prevent accidents.
 High standards of cleanliness to maintain a healthy environment for the children.
This framework ensures that the physical space meets the diverse needs of children under three
years old, supporting their well-rounded development.

When planning spaces for babies and toddlers, it's essential to create an environment that's not just
a scaled-down classroom but one that meets their specific developmental needs. Consider the
following:
 The layout should allow caregivers to see all children at all times, ensuring effective
supervision whether children are awake or asleep.
 The indoor area must accommodate routine activities like sleeping, eating, and changing
nappies, with personal belongings and nappy storage close to the relevant areas.
 Support areas are necessary for food preparation, toy cleaning, laundry, and staff breaks, with
food prep areas separate from nappy changing stations.
 Safety is paramount. Check the placement of windows and stairs, the type of glass in
windows, electrical outlets, water source settings, and ensure there are adequate emergency
exits.
 Plan both indoor and outdoor environments to have clear spaces for active play and
developing motor skills.
 There should be plenty of room for storing toys and equipment both indoors and outdoors.
 Consider aesthetic and acoustic factors, like colour, texture, lighting, and sound, to create a
pleasing and functional environment.
 Create cosy, private spaces where children can spend time alone or with a caregiver, both
indoors and outdoors.
 Include easy-to-clean areas for messy play and ensure that these spaces are suitable for both
babies and older toddlers.
 Provide a variety of surfaces outdoors for different types of mobility and play, ensuring there
are open areas for active movement.
 Ensure the environment offers sensory stimulation through different textures, sounds, and
visual experiences.
 Consider security measures such as a secure perimeter fence and protection from the
elements.
 Make sure areas for manipulative, sensory, and motor skills activities are available, along with
quiet zones with books and soft toys.
 For toddlers, include spaces for imitative play, block areas, a book corner, manipulative and
construction toys, and ample space for large motor activities.
 Arrange the environment so children can start to do things for themselves, such as going to
the toilet and washing hands, with equipment scaled to their size.
 Have proper storage and display areas for materials, offering limited choices at any one time
to avoid overwhelming the children.
 Plan for different outdoor experiences every day, and ensure cultural diversity is reflected in
the play materials provided.
 Convey positive messages within the environment, allowing children to explore and choose
play materials freely within a secure space.
 Provide materials that are individually suited to the children's developmental stages, offering
both challenges and opportunities for success.
 Encourage toddlers to help with tidying up, as they are capable of sorting and putting away
toys with guidance.

 When planning a child-friendly space, ensure you allocate areas for displaying children's

If you take all these environmental factors into account and cater to both the individual and collective

9.4 Managing the learning of preschool children (three to five years)
This section highlights the importance of aligning teaching practices with developmental needs in an
Early Childhood Education (ECE) setting for children between three and five years old, including
Pre-Grade R. When planning and managing teaching, the following considerations are crucial:
 The design of the learning environment, such as the playroom.
 The daily schedule of the ECE centre.
 The content of learning, including programmes and activities.
 Documented plans for learning content.
9.4.1 Planning the learning environment
Effective planning of the playroom is essential for facilitating learning. A well-organised playroom
includes various areas for play, offering children the opportunity to engage in different activities and
to make choices freely. When planning a playroom, ensure there are ample opportunities for
creative play and decision-making. Here are some areas that should be included:
 A zone for artistic and creative projects.
 A block construction area for building and engineering play.
 An area dedicated to dramatic or fantasy-themed play.
 A space for musical exploration.
 A reading nook with a variety of books.
 A section for science experiments and discovery.
 An area focused on sensory play with materials like water and sand.
 A corner for writing and literacy activities.
 A woodworking or crafting space.
By considering these aspects, the learning environment will cater to a range of interests and
promote comprehensive development in preschool children.
Determining the Number of Areas in a Playroom
When deciding on the layout of a playroom, remember there are no fixed rules. The design should
be tailored to the children's unique needs and may vary based on factors such as:
 The age range of the children - younger children may require fewer, but larger, play areas.
