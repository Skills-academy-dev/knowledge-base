INTRODUCTION
Have you ever wondered how fraud is uncovered in companies, non-profits, or government
departments? Or who investigates when large amounts of money go missing? In the world of
business, there are professionals trained to do exactly that. These specialists are called
forensic auditors. Their job is to find the truth behind financial crimes by collecting evidence
and helping organisations take legal action when necessary.
Forensic auditing goes beyond regular auditing—it focuses on uncovering fraud, tracing
transactions, and reporting findings clearly. Forensic auditors often present their findings in
court or disciplinary hearings, so their reports must be honest, detailed, and legally sound.
In South Africa, this work is critical. Fraud and corruption affect both public and private sectors.
Forensic auditors play a key role in exposing wrongdoing and protecting financial integrity.
In this textbook, you’ll learn how to plan investigations, collect and protect evidence, follow
legal and ethical standards, and report your findings. You’ll also explore real-life South African
cases, relevant laws, and professional expectations to help prepare you for work in this
important field.

Unit 1: What is Forensic Auditing?
Forensic auditing is more than just checking books and balancing figures—it is a powerful tool
used to investigate financial crime. In this unit, you will explore what forensic auditing is, why it
is important, and how it helps uncover the truth when money goes missing. Whether it involves
uncovering fraud in an NGO, tracing corrupt dealings in government, or investigating theft in a
company, forensic auditors play a key role in protecting the public and maintaining trust.
Definition of Forensic Auditing
The word forensic means “suitable for use in a court of law.” So, forensic auditing refers to
audits that involve a legal investigation. These audits often lead to legal action, either in a
courtroom or through disciplinary hearings.
Definition:
Forensic auditing is a special area of auditing that involves examining financial
information for use in legal cases, often linked to fraud, corruption, or theft.
Did You Know?
The term “forensic” comes from the Latin word forensis, meaning “of the forum” – in ancient
Rome, that referred to public debates and court proceedings.

Functions of Forensic Auditing
Forensic auditors perform a wide range of tasks depending on the case at hand. Here’s a
breakdown of common functions:
 Investigating Fraud: They trace missing funds, fake invoices, ghost employees, or
manipulated accounts.
 Collecting Evidence: Auditors gather documents, emails, bank records, and
interview witnesses.
 Analysing Financial Information: Using methods like ratio analysis, trend
comparison, and surprise audits to detect red flags.
 Reporting Findings: They write reports that answer the “6Ws”: What, Who, Where,
When, Why, and How.
 Testifying in Court: As expert witnesses, forensic auditors help courts understand
financial evidence.

Real-Life Example: Eskom and Contract Irregularities
In recent years, Eskom, South Africa’s national electricity provider, was involved in
investigations due to irregular contracts awarded to suppliers. A forensic audit uncovered
that certain procurement officials bypassed normal tender processes to favour specific
companies. These findings led to contract cancellations and disciplinary proceedings,
highlighting how forensic auditing uncovers misconduct and supports corrective actions.
This example illustrates the core function of forensic auditing: uncovering financial wrongdoing
and collecting evidence for legal and disciplinary action.
Tip:
Always keep a clear chain of custody when handling evidence. This means documenting who
accessed the information and when. It ensures the evidence remains admissible in court.
Benefits of Forensic Auditing:
 Prevents and detects fraud
 Improves internal controls
 Protects organisational reputation
 Supports accountability and governance
 Recovers lost or stolen funds
Did You Know?
Forensic auditors must be able to explain their findings in a simple way. Judges are not ac-
countants, so complex jargon can weaken a case.

Unit 2: Differences Between Forensic and Traditional Auditing
Auditing plays a key role in protecting financial systems from fraud and mismanagement. But
not all audits are the same. While traditional audits aim to ensure that financial statements are
accurate and follow accounting rules, forensic audits are designed to uncover suspicious
activities and provide evidence that can be used in legal investigations. This unit explores the
differences between forensic and traditional auditing in detail. Understanding these differences
will help you appreciate the specialised nature of forensic auditing and its value in both the
private and public sectors of South Africa.

Real-Life Example: Municipal Audit vs. Forensic Investigation in Tshwane
In the City of Tshwane, a routine municipal audit by the Auditor-General flagged irregular

Unit 3: Importance of Forensic Auditing in Business and Government
Forensic auditing plays an essential role in both business and government. With increasing
cases of financial fraud, corruption, and unethical behaviour, organisations need expert
investigators to uncover wrongdoing and protect their resources. This unit explores why
forensic auditing is important, how it supports businesses and government institutions, and the
impact it has on promoting honesty, transparency, and good decision-making.
3.1 Why Businesses Need Forensic Auditing
Forensic auditing helps businesses identify fraud, recover losses, and improve their operations.
It is especially useful in cases of:
 Employee theft or embezzlement
 Falsified financial records
 Procurement fraud or false invoicing
 Misuse of company funds
Forensic auditors investigate the situation, collect evidence, and help business leaders take
legal action if needed.
Example:
A South African construction company noticed large, unexplained payments to a supplier. A
forensic audit revealed the supplier was fake and the payments were going to a manager’s
personal account. The evidence led to criminal charges and helped the company recover some
of the stolen money.

3.2 Importance in Government and the Public Sector
In South Africa, government departments and state-owned entities (SOEs) manage large
amounts of public money. If funds are misused or stolen, it can hurt service delivery and
damage public trust.
Forensic audits are often ordered when there are signs of:
 Corruption or bribery
 Irregular tenders or contracts
 Unauthorised spending of public funds
 Delays in service delivery without clear reasons
These audits help government officials investigate and take corrective action, including
recovering stolen funds and prosecuting offenders.
Supporting Good Governance
Forensic auditing helps promote good governance, which means:
 Following the law
 Acting ethically
 Being transparent
 Protecting public interests
It also helps departments comply with laws like the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA)
and Municipal Finance Management Act (MFMA), which require strong financial control and
regular reporting.

Unit 4: Role of the Forensic Auditor
Forensic auditors play a crucial role in today’s complex business and government
environments. They are not just accountants—they are investigators, analysts, and expert
witnesses. Their work goes beyond reviewing numbers; it involves uncovering hidden truths
and presenting reliable evidence that can stand up in court. In this unit, we will explore the key
responsibilities of a forensic auditor, the settings in which they operate, and the wide range of
tasks they perform to prevent and detect fraud.
4.1 What Does a Forensic Auditor Do?
A forensic auditor investigates financial records to uncover fraud, corruption, theft, and
mismanagement. Their work is often part of a legal process and may include giving expert
testimony in court.
Key Responsibilities:
 Investigate Fraud and Irregularities: Forensic auditors examine suspicious
financial activities, such as fraudulent invoices, embezzlement, or payroll fraud.
 Analyse Financial Data: They identify patterns, compare data across time periods
or departments, and spot inconsistencies.
 Gather and Preserve Evidence: Auditors collect and protect documents, emails,
transaction logs, and financial records for use in court.
 Write Reports for Legal Use: Their findings are detailed in formal reports that may
be used in disciplinary hearings or legal proceedings.
 Testify as Expert Witnesses: They may explain financial evidence to a court or
tribunal and must be credible and clear communicators.
 Recommend Control Improvements: Based on their findings, auditors often advise
businesses or government departments on how to prevent future fraud.

5.2 Core Ethical Principles
Forensic auditors are trusted professionals. Their ethical behaviour not only protects their
reputation but also ensures the credibility of their investigations. The following principles are
essential:
 Confidentiality
 Keep information secure and only share it with those authorised to know.
 For example, a forensic auditor must not reveal a whistleblower’s identity during the
investigation.
 Integrity
 Always be honest and avoid conflicts of interest.
 For instance, refusing a bribe offered to ignore fraudulent transactions.
 Objectivity
 Maintain neutrality and fairness, even under pressure.
 Avoid bias, especially if auditing someone the auditor personally knows.
 Accountability
 Be responsible for your actions and decisions.
 This includes acknowledging mistakes and correcting them.
 Professional Competence
 Continuously update knowledge and skills.
 For example, attending workshops on changes to South African financial laws.
 Reasonable Care and Diligence
 Take every precaution to verify facts before reporting.
 Avoid using illegal methods to gather evidence.

Ethical Dilemmas and Decision-Making
Forensic auditors often face tough decisions where ethics are tested. For example:
Scenario:
An auditor discovers that a close friend is involved in fraud. Reporting it could ruin the
friendship, but staying silent would be unethical and possibly illegal.
Tip:
Always ask, “What action best serves justice and the truth?”
Ethical Decision-Making Tools
Use tools such as Laura Nash’s 12 Questions to evaluate your actions:
The 12 Questions Explained
Each question in Nash’s model serves as a step to evaluate the ethical dimensions of a
decision. Together, they form a comprehensive tool for ethical analysis:
1. Have you defined the problem accurately? Ensure you have a clear and precise
understanding of the issue at hand.
2. How would you define the problem if you stood on the other side of the fence?
Consider the perspective of other stakeholders involved in the situation.
