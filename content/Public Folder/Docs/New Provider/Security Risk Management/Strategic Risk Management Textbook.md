Unit 1: Understanding Risk and Uncertainty
1.1 Definition of Risk
 Risk refers to the effect of uncertainty on objectives, which may be positive or
negative.
It involves two main aspects:
 Likelihood (probability) that an event will occur.
 Impact (consequence) the event will have on the organisation’s goals.
Figure 2: Depiction of risk
Source: https://www.stakeholdermap.com/risk/risk-definition.html
1.2 Definition of Uncertainty
 Uncertainty refers to a lack of complete knowledge or predictability about future
events.
 Unlike risk, uncertainty is difficult to measure or assign probabilities.
Often arises from:
 Incomplete or imperfect information.
 Rapid changes in the external environment.
 Unexpected events.
10 | Page

1.3 Differences Between Risk and Uncertainty
 Risk can generally be assessed and managed through controls and mitigation
measures.
 Uncertainty is harder to quantify and requires flexible, adaptive strategies.
 Recognising the distinction is critical to choosing appropriate management approach-
es.
1.4 Common Sources of Risk and Uncertainty
Internal sources:
 Operational failures
 Process inefficiencies
 Human errors
External sources:
 Economic fluctuations
 Legal and regulatory changes
 Technological advancements
 Environmental or natural events
Real-life Example
A street food vendor decides to try selling a new spicy dish. They know it might not sell well
(risk), but they don’t know how people will react to the taste or price (uncertainty).

Unit 2: Principles and Objectives of Risk Management
A clear set of principles and well-defined objectives guide all effective risk management activities.
Understanding these elements helps organisations create structured, systematic approaches to
manage risks and achieve their strategic and operational goals.
2.1 Principles of Risk Management
 Risk management should be integrated into all organisational processes and decision-
making.
 It must be structured and comprehensive, ensuring consistency across all areas.
 Should be customised to fit the organisation’s external and internal context.
 Needs to be inclusive, involving key stakeholders at all levels.
 Should be dynamic and responsive to change, continuously improving.
 Must rely on best available information, while acknowledging limitations.
 Requires a focus on human and cultural factors, promoting risk awareness.
 Should facilitate continual improvement and enhancement of systems.
2.2 Objectives of Risk Management
 Protecting value and assets: Minimising losses and safeguarding resources.
 Supporting decision-making: Providing reliable information to guide choices.
 Enhancing resilience: Preparing the organisation to withstand and adapt to changes
and shocks.
 Achieving strategic objectives: Aligning risk responses with overall organisational
goals.
 Improving operational efficiency: Reducing disruptions and increasing stability.
Real-life Example
A farmer saves extra seeds and sets aside emergency money in case of a bad harvest. This
helps protect their business and family.

2.3 Alignment with Organisational Strategy
 Risk management must align with the organisation’s vision, mission, and strategic objectives.
 Ensures that risk responses support growth, innovation, and sustainability.
 Helps integrate risk considerations into planning and performance evaluation.
2.4 Benefits of Effective Risk Management
 Builds stakeholder trust and strengthens reputation.
 Improves compliance with legal and regulatory requirements.
 Enhances resource allocation and cost control.
 Encourages a proactive rather than reactive culture.
2.5 Challenges in Implementing Principles and Objectives
 Resistance to change within organisational culture.
 Limited resources or lack of skilled personnel.
 Complexity in integrating risk processes across departments.
 Difficulty in measuring and communicating risk effectively.

Governance and organisational context shape how risk management is designed and implement-

ations into strategic and operational activities.

 Refers to the framework of rules, practices, and processes by which an organisation is
directed and controlled.
 Establishes clear roles and responsibilities, ensuring that decision-makers are ac-
countable.

Involves understanding the internal and external environment in which the organisation operates.
Factors such as:
 Organisational structure and culture
 Stakeholder expectations
 Market and industry conditions
 Regulatory and legal requirements
3.3 Risk Governance
 A subset of corporate governance focused on identifying, assessing, and managing
risks at all organisational levels.
 Promotes integration of risk management into strategic planning and daily operations.
 Requires oversight from senior leadership and boards to ensure effectiveness.
3.4 Roles and Responsibilities
 Senior management: Provides strategic direction and ensures resources for risk man-
agement.
 Board of directors: Oversees risk policies and overall risk appetite.
 Risk committees and internal auditors: Monitor and report on risk performance and
controls.

 Gradual shift toward enterprise-wide approaches, integrating strategic and operational risks.

 Customisation to specific contexts
 Continuous improvement

 Highlights components such as:
 Governance and culture
 Strategy and objective-setting
 Performance measurement

Case Study (6 questions-6 marks)
Fresh Bites Café is a small, popular local café known for its fresh salads and sandwiches. To
reach more customers and increase sales, they decided to start a delivery service. However,
this decision came with several risks. There was a strategic risk that offering delivery might
change their brand image as a cosy, dine-in spot. Operational risks included the possibility of
late deliveries affecting customer satisfaction. Financial risks arose from the extra costs of
hiring delivery drivers and investing in packaging, which could strain their budget. There were
also compliance risks, as they needed to follow food safety and delivery regulations. To ad-

and clear processes. By carefully planning and integrating risk management into their opera-
tions, they launched the delivery service successfully. Within six months, delivery sales made
up 20% of total revenue and helped the café build stronger connections with new customers,
creating additional value for the business.

Introduction
Risk identification is the process of spotting possible threats or uncertainties that could affect an
organisation’s goals, operations, or reputation. These risks can come from inside the organisa-
tion, like technical issues or staff shortages, or from outside sources like economic changes, new
regulations, or natural disasters. It also involves understanding context. Getting the full picture
around the organisation, including internal factors like its values, structure, and past experiences,
as well as external conditions like the market, competitors, or the weather. Without understanding
the context, risk management becomes guesswork. A risk in one organisation might not be a risk
in another, depending on where and how they operate.
This module explores different ways to find risks, such as brainstorming sessions, interviews, and
checklists. It also introduces key tools like PESTLE and SWOT analyses that help examine the
bigger picture of risk.

 Risks can be grouped into several broad categories:
 Strategic Risks: These relate to long-term goals, such as launching new products or

 Operational Risks: These affect daily functions like supply chain management or staff

 Financial Risks: These involve monetary matters such as budget overruns or sudden

 Organisations are also exposed to environmental and legal risks.
 Environmental risks include natural events such as floods, wildfires, or long-term is-
sues like climate change.
 Legal risks arise when a company fails to comply with laws, such as safety regulations
or data protection laws. A failure in this area can result in lawsuits, fines, or reputation-
al damage.
