Introduction

Operational risk is the chance of loss from failures in processes, people, systems, or external events. It spans various non-financial risks across an organisation. Managing it means identifying, assessing, and controlling risks to reduce losses. A proactive approach protects assets, reputation, and long-term business stability.

This module introduces the foundations of operational risk management.

• Explains key terms and global frameworks, including Basel II/III and COS

• Introduces the Four-Cause Model to identify sources of risk

• Explores the difference between internal and external risk drivers

• Examines how legal risk is integrated into operational risk

• Reviews real-world case studies to show how risks emerge and are managed in practice

Unit 1: International Definitions (Basel II/III, COSO)

This unit provides a foundational understanding of how leading global frameworks define operational risk. By comparing definitions from Basel II, Basel III, and COSO, students will learn why consistent terminology is critical for measuring, reporting, and managing risk across different organisations and jurisdictions.

1.1 Basel Framework Definitions

• The Basel II Accord (2004) formally recognised operational risk as distinct from credit and market risk.

• It defined operational risk as the risk of loss from failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events.

Basel III, introduced after the global financial crisis, expanded on Basel II by:

• Setting stricter capital requirements for operational risk

• Raising qualitative standards for risk management

Under Basel III, banks must hold capital for both expected and unexpected operational losses using methods such as:

• Basic Indicator Approach (BIA)

• Standardized Approach (TSA)

• Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA)

10 | Page

• These approaches help ensure that banks stay solvent during operational disruptions.

• Basel regulations incentivise strong risk management by linking capital requirements to risk exposure.

This motivates banks to invest in:

• Better internal controls

• Stronger systems

• More resilient operational processes

Key Terms:

• Basic Indicator Approach (BIA): A simple method where banks hold capital equal to a fixed percentage of gross income.

• Standardized Approach (TSA): A more granular method that applies different percentages to specific business lines.

• Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA): Banks use their own internal models, subject to regulatory approval, to calculate required capital.

Practical Example:

When a South African utility company like Eskom implements a multi-tiered approval process for software updates—requiring sign-off from both IT and risk management—a robust control environment is demonstrated, reducing the likelihood of unauthorized or faulty system changes.

1.2 COSO Framework Definition

The COSO framework defines operational risk as the risk of loss from failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events.

• Information and communication

The control environment forms the foundation for effective risk management by setting the tone at the top.

Unit 2: Four-Cause Model

The Four-Cause Model categorizes operational risk sources into processes, people, systems, and external events. Understanding these four distinct causes helps organizations pinpoint where failures may occur and tailor controls appropriately.

2.1 Process-Related Drivers

• Unclear or incomplete processes can cause employees to guess and make mistakes.

• Outdated instructions may lead staff to follow incorrect procedures, causing delays or extra work.

• Too many steps or manual handoffs slow down operations and increase the risk of errors.

2.2 People-Related Drivers

• Lack of training can lead to errors, as staff may not know the correct procedures.

• Stress or low morale may cause employees to rush or ignore rules, increasing the risk of problems.

• Staff turnover creates knowledge gaps, making it difficult to maintain consistent operations.

2.3 System-Related Drivers

• Outdated software or equipment can cause system crashes or create security vulnerabilities.

• Unverified updates may disrupt important features and lead to operational downtime.

• Poor system integration can result in data loss or confusion, making reports unreliable.

2.4 External Drivers

• Natural events such as load shedding or floods can halt operations and lead to financial losses.

• New laws or regulations may require rapid adjustments, placing pressure on staff and systems.

• Market shifts or supply disruptions (e.g. price spikes, shipping delays) can catch organisations off guard and require quick responses.

Unit 3: Internal vs. External Risk Drivers

3.1 Internal Risk Drivers

• Internal risks often arise from issues with processes, people, and systems.

• Outdated procedures or unclear guidelines can lead to wrong decisions, delays, and losses.

• Human factors such as poor training or low morale can cause errors or misconduct, affecting trust and performance.

• Technical issues, like software glitches or poor system integration, can interrupt operations and distort data.

• Internal audits and self-assessments reveal risk gaps and help catch problems early.

• Regular workflow reviews and open communication allow teams to report issues before they escalate.

• Strong documentation and knowledge sharing are essential for consistency and quality.

3.2 External Risk Drivers

• External risks can arise suddenly and have major effects on operations.

• Natural emergencies like floods or load shedding can disrupt or relocate services.

• Legal and regulatory changes may force organisations to adjust policies and shift resources quickly.

• Market disruptions, such as supplier failures or economic downturns, can affect supply chains and income.

• Scenario planning and stress testing help organisations identify potential threats and test their readiness.

• Simulating events like cyberattacks or supplier failures promotes a proactive response mindset.

• Resilient partnerships and supply chains reduce the impact of disruptions.

Key strategies include:

• Diversifying vendors

• Having backup suppliers

• Creating collaborative agreements

Unit 4: Legal Risk Integration

Legal risk integration involves understanding how laws, regulations, and contractual obligations intersect with everyday operations. In this unit, we’ll explore what legal risk means, how it can slip into processes, and practical ways to weave legal checks into your risk management framework.

4.1 Understanding Legal Risk

Legal risk occurs when an organisation fails to comply with laws, regulations, or contracts.

Key sources of legal risk include:

• Regulatory non-compliance, which can lead to fines, sanctions, or reputational harm.

• Ambiguous contract terms, potentially causing disputes or financial liability.

• Intellectual property violations, such as using unlicensed software, resulting in legal action.

Legal risk also involves:

• Changes in legislation or court rulings that impact how organisations must operate.

• The need to stay updated on legal developments affecting the industry.

Risk reduction strategies include:

• Regular legal audits to identify compliance gaps.

• Ongoing staff training to ensure understanding of legal responsibilities.

• Cross-functional collaboration between legal, operations, and risk teams.

Clear communication channels are essential to quickly share legal updates and embed compliance into daily decision-making.

Incorporating Legal Controls

Standardised review checkpoints help integrate legal risk controls into operations:

• Include a legal compliance checklist in workflows to ensure alignment with regulations.

• Involve legal advisors in contract drafting and renewals to clarify terms and protect the organisation.

• Use automated regulatory updates (via compliance software or subscription services) to alert teams of changes.

Documentation supports legal compliance by:

• Keeping a well-organised archive of policies, contracts, and compliance records.

• Scheduling regular reviews (e.g. quarterly) to detect issues early and show active oversight.

Training and awareness enhance legal risk management:

• Conduct workshops on key regulations.

• Share easy-to-understand guides to help staff recognise legal risks.

• Embed legal awareness into daily operations and decision-making.

Unit 5: Operational Risk Taxonomy

In this unit, we’ll learn how to organize different types of operational risks into a clear taxonomy. A well-defined risk taxonomy acts like a map, helping you categorize risks consistently and making it easier to monitor, report, and control them.

5.1 Taxonomy Structure

A risk taxonomy starts with broad categories, broken into detailed subcategories.

Key steps include:

• Defining high-level groups (e.g. people, processes, systems, external events)

• Assigning specific risk types under each group

• Providing clear definitions to avoid confusion

• Maintaining a flexible, hierarchical structure for future updates

Benefits of a structured taxonomy:

• Standardises communication across teams

• Makes data comparison and trend spotting easier

• Supports regulatory reporting through clear classifications

Keep the taxonomy relevant by:

• Reviewing it regularly with stakeholders

• Adding new subcategories as operations evolve

• Involving subject matter experts to identify emerging risks

5.2 Key Categories and Definitions

Key categories in an operational risk taxonomy typically include:

• People risk – errors, fraud, and compliance failures

• Process risk – procedural gaps, control breakdowns, documentation flaws

• System risk – technology failures, data integrity issues, cyber threats

• External risk – environmental events, legal changes, market disruptions

Each category should:

• Include clear definitions and

• Provide concrete examples (e.g. phishing attacks under cyber threats)

Align taxonomy with industry standards such as:

• Basel loss event types for consistency and benchmarking

Adapt global frameworks to local contexts for:

• Better compliance

• Improved practical application

5.3 Practical Application of Taxonomy

Applying the taxonomy involves linking real incidents to predefined risk categories.

Key steps include:

• Collecting historical data on operational losses and classifying them

• Using the taxonomy to organise risk registers and dashboards

• Integrating taxonomy labels into incident reporting tools for automatic classification

Staff training is essential:

• Run workshops with sample incident classifications

• Discuss unclear cases and build consistent understanding

• Improve accuracy and confidence over time

Embedding the taxonomy helps:

• Generate useful metrics (e.g. loss frequency by category)

• Target control improvements where risk is concentrated

• Prioritising root causes for immediate or long-term resolution

Unit 7:Case Study Overview

Case Study: South African Bank IT Failure

Background

In March 2019, a major South African bank experienced a critical IT outage that affected its ATM network and online banking systems. The failure stemmed from a software update that inadvertently introduced a bug into the transaction processing core, halting services nationwide for 48 hours.

Risk Identification and Taxonomy

• Process: The bank’s change management procedures lacked a formal rollback plan for software updates, leading to prolonged service downtime.

• People: IT staff were not trained on emergency patch rollbacks, and communication between development and operations teams was insufficient.

• Systems: The update was deployed without testing on a staging environment that mirrored live operations, exposing the production system to unvalidated code.

• External: Customers and regulators imposed pressure, increasing reputational risk and potential fines for service-level agreement breaches.

Assessment and Impact

The bank incurred direct operational losses estimated at R120 million due to halted transactions and manual contingency measures. Customer complaint volumes spiked by 300%, and the bank faced regulatory scrutiny from the South African Reserve Bank for failing to meet uptime requirements.

Root Cause and Controls

A root cause analysis revealed that outdated change management protocols and missing contingency procedures were primary drivers. In response, the bank implemented:

• A formalized software release policy with mandatory pre-production testing.

• Cross-functional “go-live” checklists and rollback mechanisms.

• Enhanced training programs for IT and operations teams on incident management.

Lessons Learned

The incident underscored the importance of robust change control and communication. By integrating control activities—such as dual approval for updates and regular disaster recovery drills—the bank improved system resilience and customer confidence.
