Introduction

Imagine you're holding a puzzle. Each piece is different, but every one is needed to complete the picture.

A child is like that puzzle. At first look, they might seem simple, just like the puzzle seems when the pieces are scattered. But to really understand them, we need to see how all the different pieces fit together. Each piece of their learning, feelings, and relationships is important.

In 'The Whole-Child Approach', we're going to be like puzzle solvers. We'll look at the 11 key parts that make up a child's whole picture. We will discover how to help each part of them grow, just like fitting puzzle pieces together to reveal the whole image.

1\. The whole-child approach

The whole-child approach is a method of considering the child in their entirety. It's about understanding that the child's learning, experiences, and development are all interconnected parts of a single, complete being. Every aspect is crucial and, hence, demands attention and discussion. Neglecting any part of a child's development can affect all other areas.

Consider the interconnectedness of these aspects like the pieces of a domino set. For instance, a headache might seem like just a physical ailment, but it can actually ripple through to how you feel (emotional), interact with others (social), and process information (cognitive).

To cultivate a child as a whole, we need to nurture the following aspects: Physical: Pertaining to the body's health and physical condition.

• Cognitive: Related to mental processes and intellectual capabilities.

• Language: The ability to communicate and express oneself through words.

• Emotional: Involving the ability to understand, express, and manage feelings.

• Self-image: How one perceives oneself, which shapes personal identity.

• Social: Pertains to interactions with others and the ability to form relationships.

• Moral normative: Understanding ethical principles and discerning right from wrong.

• Sexual: Recognition and respect for sexual identity and gender differences.

• Creative: The capacity to generate original ideas and bring them into existence.

• Aesthetic: An appreciation for beauty and artistry in various forms.

• Religious: Holding beliefs in, and reverence for, a higher power or spiritual principles.

In this module, we'll explore strategies to support every aspect of the child's growth, ensuring no piece is left unattended.

2\. Different aspects of the whole-child approach.

In this section, we will explore each aspect of the whole-child approach in greater detail, emphasising the significance of each for a child's comprehensive development.

2.1 Physical aspect

The 'physical' aspect relates to the body's functions, including sensory abilities, body control, motor skills, and muscular strength. The term 'motor skills' is synonymous with movement skills and is integral to physical development.

Here are examples of how physical development manifests through various activities:

• Walking and running are examples of gross motor skills, utilising the body's large muscles for movement.

• Standing on one leg or catching a ball demonstrates body control and balance, engaging both large and small muscle groups along with sensory coordination.

• Threading a needle or drawing illustrates fine motor skills, requiring precise movements and coordination between the eyes and hands.

Conceptual Clarifications:

• Motor skills: Movements executed by muscles, ranging from gross (large muscle movements) to fine (small muscle movements).

• Coordination: The seamless operation of various muscles and senses in tandem.

• Large muscles: Muscles used for sweeping movements, like running or swinging.

• Small muscles: Muscles used for detailed tasks, such as writing or manipulating small objects.

The physical aspect of a child's development is advanced through a variety of activities that utilise large muscles, such as climbing, skipping, engaging in sports like ball games, and dancing. These activities are known as gross motor movements. Similarly, developing fine motor skills involves smaller muscles and intricate movements, like those needed for constructing puzzles, creating art, or tying shoelaces.

As caregivers, it's important to create environments that encourage children to use their physical capabilities, which is vital for their overall motor skill development. Passive activities, such as excessive sitting in front of a television, do not support the advancement of these skills. By the age of six, most children have developed a considerable number of motor skills and can coordinate their muscles and senses effectively—a process known as coordination.

Children also progress towards independence as they grow. They learn to use the toilet, eat without assistance, and can adapt to new environments, such as a school, away from their parents. These experiences are essential for their development, as they expand the child's understanding of the world.

An interesting aspect of physical development is that the parts of the body closer to the head tend to develop motor skills before those closer to the feet. For instance, a crawling infant will use their arms more than their legs. The parts closer to the centre of the body also develop before the parts farther away. Thus, a child will initially use their arms for reaching, and as they develop, gain finer control with their fingers.

6 | Page

The stages of physical development in infants and children are marked by a series of milestones:

• From 1 to 3 months, infants exhibit the stepping reflex and begin to lift their heads.

• At around 5 months, they start to sit without support.

• By 10 months, crawling becomes a common way for infants to explore their surroundings.

• At 9 months, you'll see them walking while holding onto furniture or hands.

• When they reach 12 months, many are walking independently.

• Between 13 to 18 months, children often start walking backwards and sideways.

• From 2 to 4 years, running and climbing stairs are added to their repertoire of movements.

• Between 4 to 7 years, children can jump, throw, and catch balls with better coordination.

During the preschool years, children begin to show a preference for using either their right or left hand. This hand dominance typically starts to emerge by the age of two and is generally well-established by the age of five or six. It's important for educators and parents to recognise and support the child's dominant hand in their activities to promote dexterity.

2.2 Cognitive aspect

The cognitive aspect encompasses the intellectual and mental growth of the child, which includes the capacity to understand and think abstractly. During the early years, a child's cognitive development is closely linked to sensorimotor development, which means they learn through interacting with their environment using their senses (like seeing, hearing, smelling) and motor skills (such as moving and touching).

One key concept in cognitive development is object permanence. This is the understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen, heard, or otherwise sensed. For instance, when a small child sees a spoon hidden, they will learn that it still exists and will search for it. This understanding becomes more refined with age, as older children will know that an object has not vanished simply because it is out of sight.

Another important concept is conservation. Conservation is the understanding that certain properties of objects, like volume or quantity, remain the same despite changes in the object's form or appearance. For example, when a child sees two identical balls of clay, they learn that flattening one does not change the amount of clay. It's the same ball, only its shape has changed.

To stimulate a child's brain and aid their cognitive development, parents and educators can introduce a range of activities that encourage thinking, such as singing, reading, and playing strategic games. These activities can enhance a child's cognitive skills by teaching them concepts like cause and effect, sequence of events, and problem-solving. Engaging in creative activities also helps develop their ability to think abstractly and enhances their creativity.

2.3 Language development

Language development begins from birth, as babies use crying as their initial form of communication. As they grow, they develop their language skills further, first through babbling and later through recognising and imitating the speech they hear around them. This process is essential for building neural pathways that facilitate language acquisition.

An interesting aspect of language learning is the concept of 'motherese', or the instinctive way parents speak to their infants. This simplified, repetitive, and melodic form of speech helps children to grasp the basic sounds and rhythms of their native language. As children hear words repeatedly in context, they begin to associate meanings with them and eventually understand that words are symbols representing objects, actions, and concepts.

Language acquisition is also reinforced when children witness the excitement and positive reinforcement from parents or caregivers upon recognising and repeating words. It is crucial for adults to pay attention to what they say and how they say it, providing clear and consistent examples for the child to imitate.

It's important to note that children process thought in their native language, which means the richness of their language environment directly impacts their cognitive development. Therefore, it's vital for parents and educators to create a language-rich environment that encourages children to listen, speak, and eventually, read and write. Conversations, storytelling, and reading to children are key practices that support language development.

Children can typically understand language long before they can speak. This receptive language skill allows them to follow simple instructions and understand basic concepts before they are able to articulate words and phrases themselves. As language skills develop, children begin to form sentences, expanding their vocabulary and improving their ability to communicate effectively. By the age of six, a child's vocabulary has grown significantly, and their sentence structure becomes more complex, indicating a solid foundation in language development.

Here are the milestones for language development in young children:

• At 6 months, infants typically start babbling, producing repetitive sounds like "da-da-da" or "ba-ba-ba".

• By 1 year, children begin to use single words that refer to what they can hear, see, or feel. These words often denote basic needs or familiar objects, such as "milk", "cat", or expressions like "go" when they wish to move, or "hungry" when they need food.

• When children reach 2 years of age, they start to combine two words to form simple phrases, like "see ball" when they notice a ball or "want cookies" to express a desire for cookies.

• By 4 years old, a child's vocabulary broadens significantly, often exceeding 50 words. They start to construct short sentences ranging from three to eight words. These sentences are usually grammatically simpler than adult speech and are characterised by telegraphic speech, where non-essential words are omitted, as in "there big dog".

• At 5 years and beyond, children have grasped the basic rules of grammar. They begin to combine words into more complex and meaningful sentences, showing a more refined understanding of language structure.

These guidelines provide a framework for the expected progression of language skills, but it's important to remember that each child develops at their own pace. Encouraging a rich language environment with plenty of conversation, reading, and storytelling can greatly support a child's language development.

2.4 Emotional and self-image

Fostering a positive emotional development is anchored in three fundamental aspects:

1\. Sense of Security: A child needs to cultivate a feeling of security, which is the cornerstone for developing self-confidence and an eagerness to explore their environment. Security here implies not just physical safety but also emotional safety, where the child feels consistently supported and understood.

2\. Expression and Management of Emotions: Children need to learn not only how to articulate

what they are feeling, be it joy, sorrow, or excitement, but also how to regulate these emotions. It's important for them to understand appropriate expressions of emotion, such as finding constructive ways to express displeasure instead of having a tantrum, or expressing sadness without letting it overwhelm their day.

Globally, a smile is generally recognised as a sign of happiness. However, emotional expressions can vary culturally. Despite these variations, basic emotional expressions like happiness, surprise, or anger have universal aspects.

Temperament, which is evident from a very early age, plays a significant role in a child's emotional landscape. It forms the base from which a diverse range of emotions will grow. Studies categorise baby temperaments as follows:

• Easy Babies: About 40% of children are typically cheerful and adjust easily to new situations.

• Slow-to-Warm-Up Babies: Making up 15%, these children are more reserved and cautious with change.

• Difficult Babies: Comprising 10%, these infants tend to be more intense and may not soothe easily.

• Variable Category: The remaining 35% of children display a mix of temperamental traits.

Temperament traits are generally observable within the first three months of a baby's life. Understanding these traits helps caregivers tailor their approach to each child's unique emotional needs.

3\. Self-Image Definition:Self-image is the mental representation one has of oneself. It's the

personal narrative that shapes self-perception and influences how an individual interacts with their world.

Fundamental to self-image are the judgements you make about yourself, often reflected in how you interpret reactions from others. For example, if people consistently respond to your actions with positivity, you might develop a more positive self-image. Conversely, if the feedback is negative, it can affect self-perception adversely.

A child's self-image is moulded in the same way. Positive affirmations from adults, such as being told they are smart or kind, can help build a strong self-image. Conversely, negative comments can be damaging.

What does it mean to have a good and positive self-image?

• It means recognising your strengths and acknowledging your worth.

• It involves having a realistic view of your capabilities and seeing yourself in a balanced way.

• It's about accepting yourself while understanding that there is always room for growth and improvement.

9 | Page

What does it mean to have a poor and negative self-image?

• It might involve doubting your abilities and feeling unworthy.

• You might focus on perceived faults rather than strengths.

• It can lead to harmful behaviours, such as neglecting your health or engaging in risky activities.

Self-image profoundly influences behaviour. It can affect the way you dress, your mood, and even your life choices, including career paths and friendships. It also impacts how you expect others to see you—if you don't value yourself, you might question why others would. A healthy self-image is critical for overall well-being.

Why is a positive self-image crucial for a child?

A positive self-image is a cornerstone for resilience. It equips children with the capacity to navigate life's challenges and to view setbacks as opportunities for growth rather than insurmountable obstacles. Children with a healthy self-esteem are better equipped to handle criticism and understand that failure is not a reflection of their worth but a natural part of learning and development.

When guiding children, it's important for adults to address the behaviour, not the child's character. For instance, instead of labelling a child as disruptive, it's more constructive to explain the consequences of the specific behaviour and encourage different choices. Phrasing such as "It's not safe to run indoors because you could get hurt" helps children understand the 'why' behind rules, promoting internal regulation rather than fear of reprimand.

Emotional development is also shaped by a child's primary caregivers—the individuals who play the central role in their care. These relationships are foundational, as they teach children about trust, love, and self-worth. The quality of attachment with caregivers can influence a child's self-image for life.

Definition Primary Caregiver: The primary caregiver is the individual predominantly responsible for the child's care and emotional support

Leaving a child to cry without comfort may inadvertently convey a message of neglect, leading the child to become hesitant to express needs or seek help. Negative experiences, such as abuse or neglect in childhood, can have profound effects on an individual's ability to trust and may result in difficulties in forming healthy relationships later in life. As adults, these individuals may struggle to express their feelings and may not feel entitled to support. However, it's crucial to acknowledge that individuals can choose how they respond to their past and can seek healing and growth, regardless of their early experiences.

2.5 Social development

Social development is the process through which a person forms relationships with others, shapes their sense of self, and integrates into society. Children are not innately equipped with social skills; they acquire these skills through learning and understanding societal norms.

Children learn social skills not only through instruction but also through observation and imitation of the adults in their lives. Thus, it's crucial for adults to embody positive social behaviours—essentially, to practice what they preach, serving as good role models.

In settings such as daycare centres, children have the opportunity to develop a range of social skills:

• Sharing: Learning to share toys and space with others.

• Attention and Cooperation: Engaging in group activities like building puzzles or playing with blocks, which teaches teamwork and patience.

• Turn-taking: Understanding the importance of waiting for one's turn, whether in play or daily routines like using the toilet.

• Empathy and Consideration: Demonstrating care by sharing and waiting, acknowledging the feelings and needs of others.

• Respect: Showing respect for peers and adults alike, which includes listening and valuing their input and presence.

Definition Peers: Peers are individuals who are similar in age, often encountered in educational and social settings, providing a child with relatable models for interaction.

One of the key milestones in social and emotional development is the attachment phase between children and their caregivers, typically intensified from the age of about 7 to 9 months. It's a period when children show a strong preference for familiar figures, which is a normal part of the separation anxiety process. This attachment behaviour peaks at 14 to 20 months and gradually becomes less pronounced as children grow.

Developing a conscience is another aspect of social development. It's the internal guidance system that informs a child of the community's expectations and norms. This understanding stems from multiple sources, including family guidance, cultural background, and personal experiences. It is through these influences that children learn to discern right from wrong and develop their moral compass.

2.6 Moral normative development

Humans are unique in our capacity to discern right from wrong, guided by moral principles. These principles are shaped by the community and culture we grow up in, which sets the standards for what is considered acceptable behaviour. A child learns to navigate societal norms successfully and to adopt the values and expectations of the people around them.

Children should be guided by clear rules within the home and school settings, which help them understand the expectations placed upon them. These rules provide a framework for them to develop self-discipline and order in their lives.

According to Kohlberg's theory, moral development in children progresses through distinct stages:

• Stage 1: Children's sense of right and wrong is initially based on what is punished or rewarded. They comply with rules to avoid punishment or to gain rewards, recognising authority figures as the source of rules.

• Stage 2: Children begin to act in ways that serve their own interests, recognising that there is a benefit to following rules, but largely when it aligns with their own desires.

• Stage 3: As they grow, children understand that being moral is related to the expectations of their family and community. The concept of "being good" is associated with fulfilling these social roles and expectations.

• Stage 4: There is an acknowledgment of societal laws and a sense of duty to uphold them. Children at this stage believe in maintaining social order by obeying laws and respecting authority.

• Stage 5: The focus shifts to upholding the rights, values, and principles agreed upon by the whole society. There's an understanding that laws should further the greater good.

• Stage 6: The final stage involves the development of personal moral guidelines. Individuals at this stage follow self-chosen ethical principles, even if they conflict with laws and rules.

This theory outlines how children's understanding of morals evolves from simple obedience to complex ethical reasoning, ultimately shaping their decisions and behaviours as adults.

2.7 Sexual development

Sexual development is a natural aspect of growing up, beginning from the moment a child is born. As children grow, they start to become aware of gender identities and the differences between male and female roles, as defined by their culture and society. Understanding these roles is a part of normal development, and children may explore what these roles mean to them.

By around the age of three, children recognise that gender is constant – they understand that boys grow into men and girls grow into women. They start to develop their own gender identity and often mimic the behaviours they see in adults and older children.

Children's play often reflects real life, and it is through play that they explore different roles and ideas. For example, they may enact scenes from home life, school, or work, adopting various roles regardless of their gender. It is important for caregivers to provide a variety of play options to allow children to explore freely in a safe and accepting environment.

As children approach adolescence, their bodies begin to change, and they become more conscious of these changes. It's essential for them to receive accurate and age-appropriate information about their bodies and sexual health.

12 | Page

It is crucial to answer children's questions about sexual development frankly and sensitively. Honest and clear communication helps to establish a healthy understanding of sexuality and respect for their own and others' bodies. If a child asks a question like "Where do babies come from?" or "What does being gay mean?", it's important to respond in a way that is age-appropriate and reflects the values and knowledge you wish to impart. Creating an open dialogue can help prevent misinformation and foster a positive attitude towards sexual health.

2.8 Creativity

Creativity is the capacity to generate innovative ideas through imaginative skill, be it devising a novel solution to a problem, a fresh method of expression, or creating a unique object or form.

The term "creativity" derives from the root "to create", which signifies the act of bringing something new and original into existence.

There's a myriad of domains where creativity can manifest. Children can exhibit creativity in diverse settings, such as in their play, when engaging in arts and crafts, through writing, or in the imaginative ways they approach problem-solving. Creativity isn't confined to the arts; it can be seen in how one thinks, solves problems, or even in the way a space is organised or decorated. Originality is key, regardless of the medium.

Educators and parents should remember that the process of creation is as valuable as the end product. The focus should be on nurturing the child's imaginative process rather than on the perfection of the outcome. For children, the act of creating is often as rewarding as what they create, and it's important to encourage this exploratory process. Play, particularly unstructured play, is an excellent avenue for creative expression, providing a space for children to explore and express their ideas freely.

Creativity bolsters a child's sense of self and is fundamental to their cognitive development. When children are encouraged to be creative, they learn to trust their own ideas and are empowered to make decisions. Questions like "What colour should I use? What shall I draw? How can I use these blocks?" stimulate critical thinking and problem-solving skills.

Creative thinking skills encompass:

• The ability to approach tasks from different perspectives.

• Formulating original ideas.

• Valuing the process over just factual outcomes.

• Adapting and applying ideas in various contexts.

To foster creativity in children, adults can:

• Encourage children to think independently and find their own solutions.

• Provide time and space for play without imposing restrictions on time or materials.

• Guide rather than dictate activities, allowing children the freedom to explore.

• Encourage brainstorming and the sharing of ideas, teaching children the value of diverse perspectives.

• Promote 'what if' questions to broaden their thinking horizons.

• Respect and value creative ideas, showing children that their creativity is appreciated.

2.9 Aesthetic developement

Aesthetic development is about cultivating an appreciation for beauty in various forms around us.

It's essential for educators and parents to foster children's aesthetic awareness. Encouraging them to notice and appreciate the beauty in a pristine environment, the intricate patterns of a flower, or the spectrum of colours in a rainbow enhances their ability to see beauty in the world.

However, equating beauty with goodness can be misleading, especially in the context of human appearance. Stories often contrast physical beauty with moral character, such as in the classic tales of 'Beauty and the Beast' or the wicked stepmothers and witches in fairy tales. It's important to guide children to understand that beauty is not synonymous with goodness and that one should not judge others based solely on appearance. It's crucial to teach children to appreciate the inner qualities of people and to understand that beauty has many dimensions.

2.10 Religous development

Research suggests that humans have an innate inclination to seek connections with a Higher Power or greater forces, often termed as the "religion gap". This inherent tendency can be fulfilled through various religious beliefs and practices that individuals choose to adopt.

South Africa is a melting pot of diverse religions including African traditional religions and those introduced by settlers and immigrants from other continents, such as Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism.

2.10.1 Indigenous religions of Africa

The Religion of the San People:

For the San, spirituality is woven into the fabric of everyday life. They hold a belief in a supreme

deity and engage in spiritual practices either individually or through rituals facilitated by a traditional healer, often referred to as a shaman.

A central practice in the San's spiritual life is the trance dance. Women form a circle around a fire and clap and sing songs with spiritual significance, while men dance within the circle. This act of singing and dancing is not only a cultural expression but is also thought to induce a trance state in the shamans, which is associated with healing powers.

Trance States:

Trances, akin to deep meditation or dream-like states, have been documented by archaeologists studying prehistoric peoples. It is believed that shamans entered trance states to create the rock paintings found in caves across Southern Africa.

Followers of African Traditional Religion:

• Adherents of African traditional spirituality typically:

• Practice Ubuntu, which promotes compassion and communal ties.

• Value the continuity of community traditions, including significant life events like birth, initiation, marriage, and death.

• Hold ceremonies and rituals to honor ancestors and the spiritual forces they believe influence daily life.

2.10.2 Important religions to Africa

Islam

Islam is a monotheistic faith founded on the teachings of Prophet Muhammad, who was born in Mecca, now part of Saudi Arabia, over 1400 years ago.

The central religious text of Islam, the Quran, is believed to have been revealed to Muhammad by the angel Gabriel. Muslims consider the Quran to be the verbatim word of God (Allah), and it serves as a comprehensive guide for life. The Quran teaches that Allah is the same God worshipped by Jews and Christians, thus establishing a link among the Abrahamic faiths. The teachings of the Quran include how to live a life in accordance with God's will, and it is written in Arabic.

Muslims gather for worship in mosques, and the crescent moon and star are widely recognised symbols of Islam.

How Islam Came to South Africa

Islam was introduced to South Africa during the time of Dutch colonialism. As the Dutch brought enslaved people from East Indonesia to work in Cape Town, these individuals brought their Islamic faith with them. Muslim community leaders played a crucial role in preserving Islamic traditions and beliefs among the enslaved population. Over time, Islamic religious schools, or madrasas, were established, which provided education about the principles and practices of Islam to the Muslim community in South Africa.

Buddhism

Buddhism was established in India over 2,500 years ago by Prince Siddhartha Gautama.

15 | Page

Gautama's Life Story (Buddha)

Siddhartha Gautama's journey to enlightenment began under extraordinary circumstances. As a newborn, it was foretold by a wise man that he would either become a great ruler or a spiritual leader. Despite his father's efforts to shield him from life's hardships by confining him to the palace, Gautama became profoundly aware of human suffering during rare outings from his sheltered life. He encountered an old man, a sick man, and then a corpse, which was his first confrontation with the realities of aging, illness, and death.

This exposure sparked a deep existential quest within Gautama. He saw a mendicant, who, despite owning nothing, exuded peace. This sight offered him a stark contrast to his life of luxury and led him to the realisation that spiritual fulfilment transcended material wealth.

Gautama embarked on a quest for understanding, adopting the life of an ascetic. He sought wisdom through rigorous study, meditation, and interaction with various teachers. His quest culminated in a profound experience as he meditated under the Bodhi tree, where he found profound insights into the nature of mind and reality, which became the cornerstone of Buddhist teaching.

He advocated for a path of moderation, away from the extremes of self-indulgence and self-mortification. Throughout his life, Buddha taught the principles of compassion, mindfulness, and the middle way, encouraging people to live ethically and to develop wisdom and understanding.

During his lifetime, Gautama Buddha established a community of monks—the Sangha—who dedicated their lives to practicing and sharing the teachings of Buddhism. They lived together, meditating and studying the Dharma, and their practices have been passed down through generations, spreading across the world, including into Africa.

Christianity

Christianity is based on the teachings of Jesus Christ, believed by followers to be the Son of God. The central text of Christianity is the Bible, which details Jesus's life, works, and teachings.

Jesus was born over two thousand years ago in the region now known as Palestine. His life and death are pivotal events in Christian teachings. He was crucified, a form of execution used by the Romans, which Christians believe was a sacrifice for humanity's sins. According to Christian belief, Jesus rose from the dead, an event known as the resurrection, confirming his divine nature and his promise of eternal life for believers.

How Christianity Came to South Africa

Missionaries brought Christianity to South Africa, setting up schools where they taught people to read and write using the Bible. This played a significant role in spreading Christian beliefs throughout the region.

Hinduism

Hinduism, one of the oldest religions in the world, originates from India and dates back about 4,000 years.

Hindu Beliefs Include:

• The belief in a supreme cosmic spirit, Brahman, who is eternal and infinite.
