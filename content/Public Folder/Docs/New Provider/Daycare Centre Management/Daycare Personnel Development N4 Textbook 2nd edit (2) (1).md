Learning Objectives
Upon completion of this module, learners should be able to:
1. Distinguish between the care provided by parents and that offered at daycare centres.
2. Describe how daycares contribute to local communities in South Africa, especially in relation
to the Bill of Rights and Early Childhood Development (ECD) programmes, as well as meet-
ing the varied needs of different communities.
3. Identify the different types of daycare services, based on location (either home-based or cen-
tre-based) and management (whether privately run, government-funded, or operated by non-
governmental organisations).
4. Understand the varied aims of different daycare centres, including enhancing children's
skills, providing for children of company employees, offering additional care services, and
stepping in as alternative care providers when needed.
5. Enumerate the key features that make daycare provision effective.
6. Recognise the qualities that contribute to a good daycare centre manager or principal, as
well as the attributes of a competent educator within the daycare setting.
7. Gain a broad understanding of how daycare systems operate in various countries around the
globe.

Unit 1.1: Parental care versus daycare
The saying "It takes a village to raise a child" is a valuable piece of wisdom. While children often
have two parents, typically one parent takes the main role of caregiver. However, it's not always
possible for parents to provide constant care.
Typically, parents and daycare centres work together to offer the best possible growth and learning
opportunities for infants, toddlers, and preschoolers. It takes a team to support each child's develop-
ment, not just the parents.
Parental Care
A nuclear family, typically made up of a mother, father, and their children, is one form of family struc-
ture, but it's not the only one. A family can be diverse and may include many different individuals
who play a role in a child's upbringing, such as:
 A single parent, whether biological or adoptive, who provides care.
 Parents who are divorced or separated, resulting in the child spending time with each parent
at different intervals.
 An extended family, which might include grandparents, aunts, or uncles who take on caregiv-
ing responsibilities.
 Foster families, which are approved by the state to look after children who may be in vulnera-
ble situations.
 Same-sex couples who may have biological children or have adopted.
No matter the family structure, it is the duty of the parents or the main caregivers to create a nurtur-
ing environment that allows the child to grow and thrive. In a supportive setting, children should:
 Feel safe and protected.
 Have their physical and spiritual needs met.
 Receive stimulation that encourages them to reach their full potential.
 Learn positive norms and values that will guide them through life.
Daycare
Daycare centres play a crucial role in the nurturing and development of children. Many families rely
on these centres to provide a secure, trustworthy, and engaging environment that supports a child’s
physical, emotional, intellectual, and social growth. The support offered by daycare is designed to
complement, not replace, the care given by the child’s family.
With many parents engaged in the workforce, either for an employer or in their own business, there
is a significant need for accessible daycare options. Families need to find a daycare solution that is
not only affordable but also supportive, offering care that aligns with the child’s needs. The partner-
ship between the family and the daycare centre is vital for the child’s well-being and development.

Unit 1.2: The role of daycare in communities in South Africa
Daycare systems are vital to community life. In South Africa, communities benefit from reputable Ear-
ly Childhood Development (ECD) service providers, including day mothers, crèches, playgroups,
nursery schools, and ECD centres. These services may be government-funded, supported by non-
governmental organisations (NGOs), or privately paid for by parents. NGOs play a particularly im-
portant role in setting up care for children in need or at risk, offering a variety of community-based
solutions.
Daycare's contributions to the community include:
 Boosting the economy by enabling parents to engage in employment.
 Readying children for school with stimulating learning experiences.
 Safeguarding the health and safety of children.
 Offering a network of support for parents, which is particularly beneficial for single-parent fami-
lies, teenage parents, and the less privileged.
 Upholding children's rights by adhering to ECD policies.
 Fortifying the community with knowledge on excellent childcare practices.
Section 28 of the South African Bill of Rights
The Bill of Rights is a cornerstone of democracy in South Africa, with Section 28 focusing on chil-
dren's rights. This section outlines the fundamental entitlements of children, impacting the responsi-
bilities of parents and daycare centres to ensure each child receives appropriate care and education.
According to Section 28, every child has the right to:
 Be registered with a name upon birth.
 Obtain citizenship.
 Receive adequate care.
 Have access to sufficient food and shelter.
 Be protected from maltreatment, neglect, and any form of degradation.
 Not be forced into work that is unsuitable for their age or that could hinder their education or
personal development.
A critical point in the subsection of this legislation emphasises that a child's best interests are of ut-
most importance in any situation concerning them. For more information on children's rights, please
visit the Constitutional Court of South Africa's website.
Early Childhood Development Programmes
In South Africa, the Early Childhood Development (ECD) programmes are integrated within the Gen-
eral Education and Training (GET) division of the Department of Basic Education. Numerous policies
have been established to ensure that all children aged 3 to 7 have the opportunity to attend ECD cen-
tres or pre-primary schools.
9 | Page

Ensuring that all South African children have access to quality ECD programmes, regardless of their
social or economic background, is essential. Often, parents incur substantial costs for private ECD
facilities to guarantee their children are in a safe and supportive environment while they work. This
presents a unique chance for the government, NGOs, and the private sector to enhance the affordabil-
ity and availability of daycare for all communities. While the government is not the sole provider of
ECD centres, there are openings for job creation and improved childcare services across the sector.
ECD programmes in South Africa begin from birth and continue until the child reaches the age of 7,
which is when compulsory formal schooling starts. These programmes are categorised into two
groups based on the child’s age.
From Birth to Three Years Old
Daycare for infants and toddlers up to three years old is often provided by day mothers, playgroups,
crèches, or within community centres. During this crucial developmental phase, it is essential for the
child to have a primary or secondary caregiver who is attentive to their needs, whether it be a parent,
family member, or a daycare provider.
A well-rounded daycare programme should provide a nurturing environment that caters to the child's
overall wellbeing. It's important for the child to feel safe, be fed, experience sensory stimulation, grow
physically, engage in communication, and have ample opportunity for play.
In the initial stages of daycare, whether informal or formal, sensory and cognitive development are
prioritised. Activities that foster language development and vision and hearing improvement are key.
Engaging the child in diverse stimulatory activities like play, singing, and storytelling can enhance their
development.
Example
Siki, an 8-month-old baby, is taken care of by Mama Lebogang while her mother works. Mama
Lebogang ensures Siki's needs are met, providing comfort, feeding, and engagement through
songs and lullabies in isiXhosa, contributing to her language development. When Siki reaches the
appropriate developmental milestones, she will transition to Ukudlala Daycare Centre to begin
learning more structured skills such as writing her name and counting.
Ages 4 to 6: Preparing for School
Programmes for children aged 4 to 6 are designed to prepare them for formal schooling, focusing
particularly on emergent literacy and numeracy skills. Centres that offer such programmes follow a
structured approach to foster the development of these essential skills.
Both government and private institutions support pre-Grade R and Grade R programmes in line with
the National Curriculum and Assessment Policy Statements (CAPS) for the Foundation Phase. Chil-
dren who have access to these programmes often have a head start when they begin Grade 1.

Example
Siki has now grown to join a more structured learning environment. Thuli, her teacher, greets her warmly
each morning. Their day starts with a morning circle where they discuss the previ-ous day's activities.
The day's schedule includes singing, rhyming, painting, and playtime in the jungle gym.
Having developed further, Siki can now write her name and count to 20. Her mother is de-lighted with her
progress and feels confident that Siki will transition smoothly into Grade R, well-prepared for the next
step in her education journey.
Diverse Communities with Different Needs
South Africa is celebrated for its rich diversity in languages, religions, cultures, and ethnicities, earning
the title 'Rainbow Nation' as a tribute to its multiculturalism. Despite this, the country faces substantial
economic disparities which adversely affect access to quality education. The high costs associated with
daycare can be prohibitive for many families, leaving some children without the necessary early educa-
tional support.
In certain under-resourced areas, individuals lacking proper training often step in to provide care, which
may hinder the holistic development of children. Without a strong foundation in basic literacy, numera-
cy, and social skills, these children may not be ready for formal schooling and could fall behind their
peers. However, even in less than ideal circumstances, daycare can still offer significant benefits by
providing nourishment, a stable routine, and mental stimulation.
The multiplicity of languages in South Africa can present challenges, especially when children do not
learn in their home language, which can affect their linguistic and cognitive development.
Daycare centres frequently embody the cultural, religious, and educational values of their communities.
For instance, a daycare that is housed within a church building will naturally incorpo-rate the ethos of
that faith, which can be a drawing factor for Christian parents. Similarly, daycares situated in academic
communities might adopt alternative educational philosophies like the Waldorf or Montessori methods,
reflecting the preferences of the local population.
Unit 1.3: Classification
In South Africa, Early Childhood Development (ECD) systems can be categorised into centre-based or
home-based setups. Additionally, these can vary based on ownership, with some facilities owned pri-
vately and others operated by the government or non-governmental organisations (NGOs).
Centre-based
Centre-based ECD facilities are characterised by their organised routines and structured educational
programmes. They primarily focus on literacy and numeracy to prepare children for school while also
supporting their social and emotional development. These centres may be located in dedicated build-
ings, community halls, or even within larger commercial spaces, and include crèches, nursery schools,
and pre-primary schools.
11 | Page

Crèches
Crèches serve infants and toddlers from birth to three years of age, with some also accommodating
older preschoolers. They offer full-day care, typically from early morning to late afternoon. A well-
structured crèche should have a separate area for babies and toddlers due to their distinct needs for
care and development. Registration of a crèche is guided by stringent standards, including a clear-
ance certificate from the Department of Health and an inspection of the site by the Department of So-
cial Development. The registration certificate specifies the operational hours and the maximum num-
ber of children the crèche is authorised to accommodate, ensuring the environment is suitable for the
children it serves. For more details on crèche facilities, refer to Module 2.
Nursery Schools and Pre-primary Schools
Nursery schools welcome toddlers from the age of two, offering a place for early learning before they
reach formal school age. Pre-primary schools, on the other hand, are designed for children in the year
they turn four, focusing on preparing them for Grade R and the formal education system that follows.
These educational institutions are geared towards half-day programmes, though some offer extended
aftercare services. The educational experience is structured, with formally qualified educators leading
the way. Parents rightly expect a meaningful educational programme that not only engages their chil-
dren but also equips them with the necessary skills for school readiness.
For an institution to operate, it must be registered with the Department of Basic Education, adhering to
the standards set out by the Curriculum and Assessment Policy Statements (CAPS) for the Founda-
tion Phase. However, compliance varies among private service providers, some of whom may not
meet the official requirements for Grade R preparation.
The fundamental skills fostered at this stage include the ability to listen to and comprehend stories,
grasp basic numeracy, write their names, and count to 10. Class sizes should ideally be small to allow
for individual attention, and educators should be well-versed in the principles of emergent literacy and
numeracy, ensuring children are on the right path to educational success.
Home-Based Care
Home-based care offers a less formalised environment and can be provided by day mothers or
through playgroups. Typically, this care takes place within a caregiver's home and serves the local
community.
Day Mothers
Individuals who have a passion for childcare and wish to support working parents can offer to look af-
ter children in their own residence. This arrangement is beneficial for those providing the service as
well as the families they support. Day mothers usually cater to children without needing to formally
register their services, as long as they care for no more than six children at a time.
This type of care is particularly suitable for children under two years old who require more one-on-one
attention and benefit from a homely and caring environment. While many working parents find this
option ideal, it's important to note that as children reach 3 to 6 years of age, they may need more
structured educational programmes that home-based environments don't always provide, to better
prepare them for school.
12 | Page

Playgroups
Playgroups represent a more informal type of home-based service, typically not operating full-time but
rather on set days of the week. These groups are centred on toddlers and older children, with a strong
focus on providing a space for social interaction among peers. It's advisable to maintain a small group
size, ideally fewer than 20 children, to ensure each child receives ample attention.
While formal registration might not be necessary, it's crucial that playgroup facilities are well-
maintained, and operators should be vigilant about safety. Issues such as unfenced gardens or an
open swimming pool must be addressed to prevent accidents. Playgroup owners are encouraged to
seek guidance from the Department of Health regarding the safety and suitability of their premises,
including the building structure and food preparation areas, to safeguard against potential dangers or
hazards.
Ownership of Daycare Systems
Daycare facilities can be distinguished by their ownership and management structures. Home-based
daycare settings are typically owned and operated by individuals from their residences. In contrast,
centre-based daycare can be administered by governmental bodies, non-governmental organisations
(NGOs), or private entities.
Private Centres
 Private centres are daycare establishments owned by an individual or a company.
 The owners of these centres have the autonomy to establish their policies and are not neces-
sarily bound to adhere to all government regulations.
 Unlike government-funded centres, private daycare facilities do not receive financial support
from the state and rely on the fees paid by parents to cover expenses such as staff salaries and
resources.
Unit 1.4: Objectives
While all daycare centres provide care for children, they may have distinct objectives that guide their
services. Parents often select a daycare centre for their child based on these specific goals, which are
aligned with the needs and preferences of their family. For instance, some centres might focus on ed-
ucational readiness, while others emphasise cultural or language development. A centre can have
multiple objectives, offering a broad range of benefits to the children in their care. It's important for
parents to understand these objectives to make an informed choice about where to enrol their child.
Objective Explanation
To provide supplementary care Daycare centres complement parental care, allowing parents to learn
from ECD professionals. The partnership of parent, child, and daycare
creates a stable triangle of support.
To further the child’s abilities Focus on developing the child's potential to ensure they are equipped
for success throughout their schooling, up to Grade 12.

Objective Explanation
To provide support to employees Company-run daycare centres enhance employee satisfaction
and productivity, reduce late-coming and absenteeism, and facili-
tate work-life balance, particularly aiding breastfeeding mothers.
To provide remedial services Centres offer support for children with developmental delays to
help meet milestones, often requiring intensive support for chil-
dren from disadvantaged backgrounds. Specialists may be in-
volved for severe cases.
To provide substitute care Centres offer care for children who cannot be with their families,
(orphanages) often due to societal challenges like HIV/AIDS, providing a safe
and nurturing environment that emulates family care.
Unit 1.5: Effective daycare
In your studies, you will encounter various daycare facilities. It's important to reflect on what makes a
daycare effective. The characteristics listed below are essential for a daycare to positively impact a
child's development:
Characteristics of Effective Daycare:
 Child-Centric Operations: All actions within the daycare should prioritise the child's well-being.
 Holistic Development: Programmes should cater to the child’s social, emotional, physical, cogni-
tive, and spiritual growth.
 Nutritional and Health Support: All daycare facilities must offer proper nutrition and healthcare to
support overall well-being.
 Qualified Staff: Individuals working in daycare should be suitably qualified and embody the neces-
sary traits to nurture young minds, as detailed in Unit 1.6.
 Planned and Structured Environment: The daycare should be well-organised with strategic plan-
ning to ensure a stable and consistent routine.
 Contingency Measures: Plans should be in place to address any unexpected challenges or emer-
gencies.
 Age-Appropriate Resources: Toys, activities, and educational materials should be age-
appropriate, engaging, and safe.
 Environment Safety: The safety of the daycare environment is paramount, with measures to mini-
mise any health risks.

Unit 1.6: Characteristics of competent daycare staff
For a daycare to thrive, it is essential for the staff to be competent. Their skills and attitudes signifi-
cantly influence the quality of care and education the children receive. Competent daycare staff pos-
sess not only the requisite skills and knowledge but also inherent personal traits that enhance the day-
care environment.
The Principal
A proficient principal is pivotal to a daycare centre's success and should exhibit:
 Leadership Qualities: Capable of delegating tasks, organising, controlling, and providing support
to staff as needed.
 Fair and Just: Demonstrates fairness and an impartial attitude towards decision-making and
problem-solving.
 Emotional Intelligence: Skilled in understanding emotions to make sound decisions and manage
conflicts effectively.
 Interpersonal Skills: Builds strong relationships with parents, staff, and children.
 Initiative and Creativity: Proactive in planning and implementing innovative strategies for the
centre's growth.
 Commitment: Shows genuine enthusiasm for improving the daycare and is invested in staff de-
velopment.
 Compliance Awareness: Understands and adheres to labour laws and government regulations
related to daycare operations.
 Parental Support: Offers assistance to parents needing guidance beyond child care.
 Community Respect: Earns the respect of staff, parents, and the broader community through
their professional conduct and dedication.
These qualities are essential for the effective management of a daycare centre, ensuring a positive
and productive environment for all.
The Educarer
As you embark on the journey to become an educator, embodying the right qualities is crucial. A
skilled educator is characterised by the following traits:
 Ethical Responsibility: Holds the best interests of each child at heart and acts with moral integri-
ty.
 Inclusiveness: Embraces love and acceptance of all children, regardless of race, religion, or so-
cioeconomic status.
 Holistic View: Acknowledges the importance of developing each child holistically.
 Partnership with Parents: Engages parents as active participants in their child's educational jour-
ney.
 Emotional Intelligence: Manages their own emotions effectively and is sensitive to the emotions
of children and colleagues.
15 | Page

 Sociability: Is approachable, has a sense of humour, and communicates effectively through
body language.
 Resilience: Stays determined and optimistic, even when faced with difficulties.
 Lifelong Learning: Has a passion for learning and seeks to deepen their understanding of child-
care and early education.
 Team Contribution: Actively participates in staff development and contributes positively in team
settings.
 Independence: Is capable of working without constant oversight.
 Self-Care: Prioritises personal health and appearance, understanding the impact on their profes-
sional role.
 Personal Fulfilment: Engages in hobbies and interests beyond their professional life, contrib-
uting to a well-rounded personality.
These qualities ensure that an educator can provide the highest level of care and education, fostering
a supportive and enriching environment for the children they teach.
Unit 1.7: Daycare in other countries (not for examination pur-
poses)
Understanding international best practices in Early Childhood Development (ECD), preschool care,
and education is invaluable. By looking at approaches in other countries, we can adopt successful
strategies and avoid pitfalls in our own practices.
Reports on ECD trends in Africa by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisa-
tion (UNESCO) have highlighted some practices with worrying implications. It's crucial to examine
these in detail to inform better ECD policy and implementation. This unit is for knowledge enhance-
Example
Africa is home to the youngest population globally, with 130 million children under six, making up
20% of the continent's population. Of the 49 countries where 38% of children under five suffer from
severe or chronic malnutrition, 30 are in Africa. The 2009 World Report on children's situation indicat-
ed that infant mortality before age five in Africa was 158 per thousand live births, compared to a glob-
al average of 71.
Challenges such as HIV/AIDS, conflict, and displacement have left many African children orphaned
or as refugees; in 1999, there were 10.6 million orphans and 35 million refugees. These circumstanc-
es underline the critical importance of prioritising early childhood care and education.
Research has shown that proper nutrition, healthcare, and intellectual stimulation in early years are
pivotal for developing children's aptitude and learning capabilities throughout their lives. The availabil-
ity of books in the home environment also has a significant impact on educational attainment, with
children having access to many books at home likely to stay in school about three years longer than
those without.
This information highlights the profound importance of robust ECD programmes as foundational sup-
port for children's development and future educational success.

Module Summary
Daycare services play a crucial role in modern society, offering significant economic benefits by ena-
bling parents to participate in the workforce while ensuring children are well-prepared for formal
schooling. These centres are vital in providing safe, supportive, and educational environments where
children’s rights are respected and parents can find guidance on effective childcare practices.
In distinguishing between parental care and daycare, it is essential to recognize that parents or guardi-
ans are considered the primary caregivers. In contrast, daycare centres are meant to offer supplemen-
tary or secondary support. The relationship between parents and daycare centres is pivotal, and it
should always focus on the child’s best interests.
Home-based daycare options are commonly found in private homes and may include services provid-
ed by day mothers and playgroups. Centre-based daycares operate in various settings, including com-
munity centres and dedicated educational facilities, ranging from crèches to pre-primary schools.
These may be privately operated or fall under government educational departments, where adherence
to governmental policies is a strict requirement.
Daycare centres can have diverse objectives, but they all aim to enhance the child’s developmental
potential. Corporate daycare centres, for example, cater to the needs of working parents, potentially
increasing job satisfaction and productivity. There are also specialised daycare services that provide
remedial assistance to children with additional developmental needs and substitute care for those

The effectiveness of a daycare is largely dependent on the quality of its structure and programmes,
including nutritious meal offerings, appropriate facilities and equipment, and a secure, safe environ-
ment for the children. The leadership and staff of a daycare centre are equally important, with a skilled
principal at the helm and educators who possess the necessary interpersonal skills, leadership abili-
ties, and a dedication to fostering an inclusive environment that promotes the holistic development of
every child.
Furthermore, there is much to be learned from observing international early childhood development
(ECD) practices, which can provide valuable perspectives and approaches that can be adapted to im-
prove local daycare services.
