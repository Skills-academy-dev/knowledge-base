INTRODUCTION
Welcome to the world of accounting! This textbook is your first step toward understanding how
businesses manage their finances, make important financial decisions, and keep their
operations running smoothly. Whether you're interested in running a business one day or simply
want to understand how money moves in organisations, this textbook will provide the
foundation you need.
Accounting is often referred to as the "language of business." It enables individuals and
organisations to record, summarise, and interpret financial information in a structured way. The
skills and knowledge you gain here will not only help you understand financial statements but
also equip you with tools to make sound financial decisions in both your personal and
professional life.
Why Study Introduction to Accounting?
Accounting is essential to every business and organisation. It helps answer crucial questions
like: Is the business making a profit or a loss? How can the business increase wealth over
time? What financial records are necessary to run a business effectively?
By studying Introduction to Accounting, you will develop a deeper understanding of how
businesses are structured, how they generate wealth, and the principles that guide financial
decision-making. Whether you aim to pursue a career in business, finance, or entrepreneurship,
or simply want to make better financial decisions, this textbook will serve as a valuable guide.
Here’s why learning accounting is important:
1. Understanding Business Operations: Knowing how businesses make decisions based
on financial data can help you appreciate how companies grow, operate, and succeed.
2. Financial Decision Making: Accounting skills allow you to make informed financial
decisions, whether for a business or in your personal life.
3. Career Versatility: Many careers in business, finance, management, and beyond require
a basic understanding of accounting.
What Will You Learn?
Throughout this textbook, you will:
 Learn why businesses exist and how they generate wealth through different forms of
ownership.

 Understand the vital role of accounting documents in recording transactions and keeping

Introduction
In this module, you will explore the essential concepts of why businesses exist and how they
generate wealth. Businesses play a crucial role in the economy, and their primary motive is often to
make a profit. This module will guide you through the various ways businesses can increase or
decrease wealth over time, and how business owners can grow or reduce their capital in both
service-oriented and trading businesses.
We will also examine the different forms of business ownership, from sole traders and partnerships
to various types of companies. You will learn the key differences and similarities between these
forms, as well as the unique activities carried out by different types of organisations, including
service, trading, and manufacturing businesses. By understanding these fundamental concepts, you
will gain insight into how businesses contribute to wealth generation and the economy as a whole.
1. Wealth Generation and Forms of Business
Enterprises
1.1 Wealth Generation
Wealth generation refers to the ability of businesses to create value, mostly through the profit they
earn from their activities. For businesses to survive and grow, they need to be able to generate
wealth, which means making more money than they spend.
1.1.1 The Profit Motive
The main reason most businesses exist is to make a profit. Profit is the difference between the
money a business earns from selling products or services (its income) and the money it spends to
run the business (its expenses). For example, a bakery makes a profit by selling bread for more
than it costs to produce. Without a profit, businesses cannot grow or continue operating.
Example: Imagine a small shop that sells school supplies. If it buys pencils for R5 each and sells
them for R10, it earns a profit of R5 per pencil. This is an example of the profit motive driving the
shop’s business.
1.1.2 Increase and Decreasing of Wealth
Businesses can increase or decrease their wealth over time. If a business makes a profit year after
year, it will increase its wealth. However, if it spends more than it earns, it will lose money and
decrease its wealth. There are many factors that can influence whether a business increases or
decreases its wealth, such as changes in customer demand, competition, and business decisions.
12 | Page

Example: A restaurant may increase its wealth by attracting more customers with a new menu. On
the other hand, if it spends too much money on rent and wages without making enough sales, it may
lose wealth.
1.1.3 Capital Increase by Service and Trading Organisations
Capital refers to the financial resources that a business owner uses to start and run a business.
Both service and trading organisations can increase their capital in different ways. Service
organisations (like a hair salon or a consulting firm) grow their capital by providing services, while
trading organisations (like a grocery store) increase their capital by buying and selling goods.
Example: A trading business such as a clothing store can increase its capital by selling more
clothes than it buys. Similarly, a service business like a cleaning company can increase its capital
by taking on more clients and charging them for services.
1.2. Forms of Business Ownership
There are different types of business ownership, each with its own structure, legal responsibilities,
and way of generating wealth. The main forms of business ownership are sole traders, partnerships,
and companies. Understanding the differences and similarities between these forms of ownership is
important when deciding how to start or run a business.
1.2.1 Sole Trader
A sole trader is an individual who owns and runs a business by themselves. They are responsible
for all aspects of the business and keep all the profits. However, they are also personally
responsible for any losses the business makes. This is the simplest and most common form of
business ownership.
Example: A person who opens a small coffee shop by themselves, makes all the decisions, and
keeps the profit is a sole trader.
1.2.2 Partnerships
A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who share the profits and responsibilities
of running the business. Each partner contributes to the business, either by investing money or
providing skills, and they share in the profits. However, they also share any losses, which means if
the business fails, they are all responsible for paying off its debts.
Example: Two friends decide to open a bakery together. One friend is responsible for baking, while
the other handles the finances. They share the profits and any losses from the business.
13 | Page

1.2.3 Companies
A company is a more complex form of ownership where the business is legally separate from its
owners. Companies can be formed to make a profit (profit companies) or for charitable or other
non-profit purposes (non-profit companies). There are several types of companies, including
personal liability companies, state-owned companies (SOC), public companies, and private
companies.
1.2.3.1 Non Profit Companies
Non-profit companies are organisations that exist not to make a profit but to serve a public or
charitable purpose. These companies reinvest any money they earn back into their activities rather
than distributing it to owners or shareholders.
Example: A non-profit organisation that provides meals to homeless people uses any donations or
income to help more people rather than keeping profits.
1.2.3.2 Profit Companies
Profit companies are created with the main goal of making money for their owners or shareholders.
There are several types of profit companies, including:
 Personal liability companies: The owners are personally responsible for the company's
debts.
 State-owned companies (SOC): These are companies owned by the government.
 Public companies: These companies can sell shares to the public, allowing many people to
invest and own part of the company.
 Private companies: These companies are owned by a few people, and their shares are not
available to the public.
1.2.4 Activities of organisations
Organisations engage in different types of activities depending on the nature of their business.
These activities determine how they generate wealth.
 Service activities: Businesses that provide services, such as a law firm or a cleaning service,
earn money by charging for the services they offer.
 Trading activities: Businesses that buy and sell goods, such as supermarkets or clothing
stores, make money by selling products for more than they paid to purchase them.
 Manufacturing activities: Businesses that produce goods, like a car manufacturer, make
money by selling the items they produce.
14 | Page

 Activities with no profit motive: Some organisations, like charities, operate without the goal
of making a profit. Instead, they focus on helping others or providing a service to the
community.
Example: A service activity could involve a consulting firm that helps companies improve their
marketing strategies. A trading activity could involve a store that buys electronics in bulk and sells
them individually at a higher price.
By understanding the various forms of business ownership and the activities that businesses
engage in, you will have a clearer picture of how businesses operate and contribute to wealth
generation. This knowledge will also help you better understand the responsibilities and rewards
that come with owning or managing a business.
Summary
In this module, you explored why businesses exist, with a focus on their primary goal of generating
profit. You learned how businesses could increase or decrease wealth over time, as well as the
factors that influenced profitability. The module also covered how service and trading businesses
grew or reduced capital.
You were introduced to different forms of business ownership, including sole traders, partnerships,
and companies (both profit and non-profit). Each type of ownership was explained in terms of
structure, liability, and wealth generation. The module concluded by examining the types of activities
businesses engaged in, such as service, trading, and manufacturing, and how these activities
contributed to wealth creation.

Introduction
In this module, you will explore the key steps and decisions involved in starting a trading business.
Opening a business requires careful planning, and you must make important management
decisions such as selecting the type of business activities, acquiring the necessary capital, and
choosing the appropriate form of ownership. You will also learn how to determine the size of the
business, the basis for conducting business, and the assets required to operate successfully.
Additionally, the module covers other important preparations, such as setting the opening date,
advertising, printing necessary business documents, appointing employees, and registering the
business with tax authorities and local government. These steps are critical to ensuring that the
business is legally established and ready for operation.
By the end of this module, you will have a clear understanding of the essential decisions and
preparations needed to open a trading concern successfully.
2.1 Management Decisions on Opening a Trading Business
Before opening a trading business, business owners must make several key management
decisions. These decisions will shape how the business operates, what it needs to get started, and
how it will grow in the future.
2.1.1 Business Activities
The first decision is to determine the type of business activity. A trading business focuses on buying
and selling goods. The owner must decide what products the business will sell and who the target
customers will be. It’s important to select a product that has demand in the market, ensuring there
will be customers who want to buy it.
Example: A trading business could be a shop that buys stationery in bulk and sells it to schools and
offices.
2.1.2 Capital Acquisition
Capital refers to the money a business needs to start and operate. Before opening a trading
business, the owner needs to plan how to acquire this capital. Some businesses start with personal
savings, while others may take out a loan or attract investors. Proper financial planning is important
to ensure the business has enough money to cover its costs until it begins making a profit.
Example: If someone wants to open a clothing store, they might use their savings to buy the first
batch of clothes or borrow money from a bank.
19 | Page

2.1.3 Forms of Ownership
Choosing the right form of business ownership is another key decision. As mentioned in the
previous module, there are different forms of ownership, such as sole traders, partnerships, and
companies. Each form has its advantages and disadvantages. For example, a sole trader keeps all
the profits but is personally responsible for all debts, while a partnership shares the responsibilities
and profits among the partners.
Example: A person opening a small trading business may choose to operate as a sole trader, as it
is simpler and requires less paperwork than forming a company.
2.1.4 Size of Enterprise
The size of the business is determined by how much capital is available and what the business aims
to achieve. A trading business could start as a small local store or as a large retail chain. The size of
the business will affect how many employees are needed, the number of products sold, and the
overall management structure.
Example: A small grocery store in a neighbourhood will have fewer employees and a smaller range
of products compared to a large supermarket.
2.1.5 Basis of Conducting Business
The business owner must also decide on the basis for conducting the business. This includes
deciding whether the business will operate from a physical store or online. Nowadays, many
businesses combine both methods, offering their products in physical locations as well as through
websites.
Example: An entrepreneur might open a clothing store with both a physical shop in the town and an
online platform to reach more customers.
2.1.6 Assets Needed for Operations
A trading business requires certain assets to operate. These assets may include equipment, stock
(products for sale), vehicles for transportation, and business premises. Planning for these assets is
essential because they form the backbone of the business’s operations.
Example: A hardware store might need shelves, a computer for tracking sales, and vehicles to
deliver goods to customers.
