INTRODUCTION

Have you ever thought about what makes people learn the way they do? Why some people grasp

new ideas quickly, while others need more time? Or how our minds develop as we grow from

children into adults? Learning Psychology explores these questions and more. It looks at how we

grow socially, morally, and mentally, how our brains work when we learn, and how things like

motivation, personality, and individual differences shape the way we understand the world.

In this textbook, you’ll dive into fascinating topics such as how memory works, what different learning

theories teach us, and how our personalities and temperaments play a role in the way we learn.

You’ll discover why knowing yourself your strengths, challenges, and what drives you is such a

powerful part of becoming a successful learner. You’ll also explore how the brain processes

information, how we see and make sense of things through perception, and how to make the most of

different learning strategies.

By studying Learning Psychology, you’ll not only gain insights into how other people learn, but also

learn valuable lessons about yourself. This knowledge will give you tools to improve your own

Whether you want to teach, guide, support, or simply understand learning more deeply, this subject

Unit 1: Basics of Learning Psychology

Learning Psychology is the study of how people learn. It looks at how we grow socially, morally, and

mentally, and how we acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values. In an educational setting,

Learning Psychology also considers how teaching methods and environments influence learning.

1.1 What is Learning Psychology?

Definition

Learning Psychology: The study of how people acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes and

values. It explores how we learn, grow, think, and behave in different situations.

Why is it important?

Learning Psychology helps us understand:

● How people develop: socially, morally, mentally and emotionally.

● Why learners differ: no two people learn in exactly the same way.

● What makes teaching effective: so educators can support learners better.

● How to become a better learner yourself: by applying learning strategies that suit you.

Real-life example

In a South African classroom, a teacher might notice that some learners understand

maths quickly while others need more practice. By applying ideas from Learning

Psychology, the teacher can use different methods — like group work, visual aids, or

extra practice — to help each learner succeed.

How Learning Psychology Links to Education

Learning Psychology is closely tied to education. In fact, you might hear people use terms like

“educational psychology” or “school psychology” — these overlap a lot. They all look at

how people learn best in schools, colleges, workplaces, or even informal settings.

Unit 2: Development Across the Lifespan

As people grow from babies into adults and eventually into older age, they go through many

developmental changes. Learning Psychology looks at how we develop socially, morally, and

mentally over time. By understanding these stages, teachers, caregivers, and even learners

themselves can better support growth and learning.

2.1 Social Development

Social development is about how we learn to interact with others and build relationships. For

instance, young children learn to share and play in groups, teenagers explore friendships and

identity, and adults form deeper social bonds and work connections.

Table: Stages of Social Development

Age Typical Social Development

Infancy (0-2) Bonding with parents, showing joy or fear.

Early Childhood (3-6) Playing alongside or with other children, learning to share.

Middle Childhood (7-12) Building friendships, understanding teamwork.

Adolescence (13-18) Seeking independence, caring more about peer opinions.

Adulthood Forming deeper relationships, working with others.

Example

A child in Grade 1 may prefer playing alone but starts learning to take turns in group activities. By

Grade 6, the same child might enjoy working in a team project, showing how social skills grow.

Tip

Try to notice how your own friendships and group activities have changed since you were

younger. This is a sign of your social development.

2.2 Moral Development

Moral development is about understanding what is right and wrong, fair and unfair. It involves

developing a sense of rules, justice, and ethics.

Kohlberg’s Theory Simplified

Psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg proposed that people move through stages of moral thinking.

Table: Stages of Moral Thinking

Conventional Doing right to gain approval or follow rules. Example: “I follow classroom

Post-conventional Understanding deeper principles of justice, even if it means breaking

Did You Know?

Many people stay at the conventional stage most of their lives, following rules mainly because

society expects it.

Why is Moral Development Important in Learning?

● Helps learners work together fairly (e.g. not cheating).

● Encourages respect for classmates from different backgrounds.

● Builds responsible citizens in schools and communities.

Cognitive development is about how we think, solve problems, remember, and learn. It looks at

how mental abilities grow from simple to complex.

Unit 3: Individual Differences and Disabilities

Not every learner is the same. Some people are quick to pick up new ideas, while others need more

time. Some are naturally creative, while others shine in logical tasks. Learning Psychology helps us

understand these individual differences and why it’s important to support every learner’s unique

needs. This unit also looks at how disabilities affect learning, and why knowing about them matters

for teachers, caregivers, and learners themselves.

3.1 Profiles of Intelligence, Creativity, Motivation

Each person has a unique mix of intelligence, creativity, and motivation. Some learners excel at

logical thinking, while others show strength in artistic skills. Motivation also varies, with some

learners eager to explore and others needing encouragement.

Real-life Examples:

● A learner in Cape Town might be very logical and enjoy solving maths problems (strong in

intelligence).

● A student in Limpopo might come up with beautiful poems or designs (high creativity).

● A learner in Durban who tries hard to improve test scores even when it’s tough shows high

motivation.

21

Did You Know?

Research suggests that motivation can matter just as much as intelligence. A learner who keeps

trying will often achieve more than someone who gives up quickly, even if they started off at the

same skill level.

3.2 Disabilities

Some learners face challenges that affect how they process information or interact with the world.

Recognising these disabilities is important so teachers, parents, and peers can give the right

support.

Table: Common Examples

Disability How It Affects Learning

ADHD (Attention Deficit

Hyperactivity Disorder)

Makes it hard to focus, sit still, or complete tasks. A learner might get

distracted easily in class.

Hearing Impairment Can delay language and reading skills, as the learner may not hear

speech clearly.

Cerebral Palsy May affect muscle control and movement, making writing or certain

activities difficult.

Tip

If you are working with someone who has a learning disability, be patient. Give clear instructions

and break tasks into small steps. Encouragement makes a big difference.

22

Why It Matters to Understand Differences

Understanding that people learn differently helps:

● Teachers choose teaching methods that fit all learners.

● Peers support one another with kindness instead of judgement.

● You know your own strengths and areas you may need to work on.

Small differences matter too

Not all differences are disabilities. Some learners may simply learn better with pictures, while others

prefer listening. This is why variety in teaching helps everyone.

Did You Know?

About 15% of the world’s population lives with some form of disability, according to the

World Health Organization. Many succeed in school and careers with the right support.

Example scenario:

Lebo, a Grade 5 learner with hearing impairment, might sit closer to the front so she can lip-read the

teacher. Her classmates learn to face her when talking so she can see their lips. This simple support

makes learning smoother for her.

Unit 4: Learning and Cognition

Have you ever wondered how your mind takes in new information, keeps it, and then uses it later?

This is where cognition — the processes of thinking, understanding, remembering, and learning —

comes in.

In this unit, we will explore how memory works, what psychologists mean by schema theory, and

how knowledge transfer allows you to use what you’ve learned in new situations. Understanding

these ideas helps you see what makes learning successful and why sometimes you forget or

misunderstand things.

4.1 Memory, Schema Theory, Knowledge Transfer

Memory is key to learning. When we learn, we create “schemas” — mental structures that organise

information. Knowledge transfer means applying what you’ve learned in one situation to another,

like using maths skills from class to measure ingredients at home.

● Memory

Memory is your mind’s way of storing and recalling information. It lets you remember facts,

experiences, skills, and even emotions.

Table: Types of Memory

Type of Memory What It Does Example

Short-term Memory Holds small amounts of information

for a few seconds or minutes.

Remembering a phone number just

long enough to dial it.

Long-term Memory Stores information for days, years, or

a lifetime.

Remembering your first day at

school.

Tip

To help move information from short-term to long-term memory, repeat it, use it in practice, or link

it to something you already know.

24

● Schema Theory

A schema (plural: schemas or schemata) is like a mental file folder. It helps you organise and

interpret information. Your mind groups similar pieces of information together to make sense of the

world.

Example of a Schema

When you hear “dog,” your brain brings up your schema for dogs: four legs, barks, wags tail. This is

why even if you see a new breed of dog, you still recognise it as a dog.

Did You Know?

Schemas can also lead to misunderstandings. For example, if someone’s schema for “teacher”

is strict and unfriendly because of past experience, they might wrongly expect all teachers to be

that way.

● Knowledge Transfer

Knowledge transfer is when you apply what you’ve learned in one situation to a different context.

Real-life Examples

● Using fractions you learned in maths class to adjust a recipe in the kitchen.

● Applying teamwork skills from playing soccer to a group project at college.

Unit 5: Perspectives on Learning

Have you ever wondered why some people learn best by doing, while others prefer watching or

listening? Different psychologists have tried to answer this question by developing theories or

perspectives on learning.

This unit looks at four main perspectives:

● The behavioural perspective

● The cognitive perspective

● The social cognitive perspective

● The constructivist perspective

Understanding these gives you a toolkit of ideas for how people learn and how teachers can help

learners succeed.

5.1 Behavioural Perspective

The behavioural perspective focuses on observable behaviours — things we can see people do. It

explains learning as a result of rewards and punishments that shape behaviour over time.

Table: Key Concepts

Term Meaning

Operant Conditioning Learning through rewards or punishments. B.F. Skinner was a leading

figure.

Applied Behaviour Analysis

(ABA)

Using rewards and small steps to teach new skills, often used with

young children or learners with special needs.

Example

A teacher gives a sticker to a learner who completes their homework. The reward increases the

chance the learner will do homework again.

28

Tip

Rewards don’t always have to be things. Praise, a smile, or extra free time can also reinforce

positive behaviour.

5.2 Cognitive Perspective

The cognitive perspective looks at how people process information, like how they remember,

organise, and retrieve knowledge. It focuses on what happens inside the mind.

Useful Cognitive Tools

● Dual coding: Combining words and pictures helps memory.

● Cognitive load: Keeping learning material simple so the mind isn’t overloaded.

● Spaced learning: Studying a little over several days is better than cramming.

● Mnemonics: Tricks like “Every Good Boy Deserves Fruit” for musical notes.

Example

When learning history, looking at a timeline (visual) while reading about events (verbal) uses dual

coding. This strengthens memory.

Did You Know?

Your brain can only hold about 7 pieces of new information at a time in short-term memory.

That’s why breaking work into chunks helps.

5.3 Social Cognitive Perspective

This perspective, linked to psychologist Albert Bandura, highlights how we learn by watching others.

It includes the concept of self-efficacy, which means believing in your ability to succeed.

29

Example of Observational Learning

● If you see a friend successfully solve a maths problem, you may feel more confident to try it

yourself.

Definition

Self-efficacy: Your belief in your own ability to do something successfully.

Tip

To build self-efficacy, start with small tasks you can achieve, then gradually try harder

challenges.

5.4 Constructivist Perspective

Constructivism suggests that learners build knowledge themselves, connecting new information to what

they already know. It is often linked to psychologists Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky.

Examples

● Piaget: Learners actively explore and discover concepts. A child learns about weight by playing

with blocks.

● Vygotsky: Learners do best when supported by others — this is called scaffolding.

Real-life classroom use

● Teachers might use group discussions or projects so learners can share ideas and

build understanding together.

30

Did You Know?

Vygotsky introduced the idea of the zone of proximal development (ZPD) — the gap

between what you can do alone and what you can do with help. Learning is strongest in

this zone.

Table: Comparing the Perspectives

Perspective Main Idea Example

Behavioural Learn by rewards & punishments. Stickers for good work.

Cognitive Learn by processing & organising info. Using mind maps to study.

Social Cognitive Learn by watching others. Copying a friend’s study technique.

Constructivist Build knowledge by linking new to

known.

Group projects exploring new ideas.

Reflection Questions

1\. Which perspective do you think matches how you learn best? Why?

2\. Have you ever copied a way of studying or working after watching someone else do it?

3\. Why do you think it helps to link new information to what you already know?

Unit 6: Motivation Theories

Why do some learners work hard even when a task is difficult, while others give up easily? What

pushes us to learn, try new things, and keep going even after failing? The answer lies in

motivation. In this unit, we explore what motivation is, how it influences learning, and look at two

important ideas: attribution theory and the difference between mastery goals and performance

goals.

Understanding these theories helps learners, teachers, and parents create an environment where

people feel encouraged to try, improve, and succeed.

6.1 Attribution Theory

Attribution theory explains how people make sense of why they succeed or fail. It looks at what

they “attribute” results to. This matters because these beliefs affect whether a person will try again

or give up.

Table: Types of Attributions

Type What it means Example

Internal You believe success or failure is due to your own

effort or ability.

“I failed because I didn’t study

enough.”

External You believe it happened because of outside

factors.

“I failed because the test was

unfair.”

Why does this matter for learning?

● Learners who see effort as the reason for success or failure are more likely to keep trying.

● Learners who blame things they cannot control (like bad luck) may give up.

32

Real-life Example

Sam gets a low mark on a maths test. If he thinks, “I did badly because I didn’t revise enough,”

he might work harder next time. But if he thinks, “I did badly because the teacher doesn’t like

me,” he may not try to improve.

Did You Know?

Research shows that teaching learners to attribute success to effort rather than luck builds

resilience. They become more willing to practise and learn from mistakes.

6.2 Mastery Goals vs Performance Goals

Mastery goals focus on learning for understanding, while performance goals focus on proving

ability to others. Learners with mastery goals tend to keep trying even when work is hard.
