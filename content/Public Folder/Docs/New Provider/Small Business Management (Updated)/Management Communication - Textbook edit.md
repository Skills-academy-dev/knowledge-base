Preface
Management Communication is an essential process of controlling people occupying different roles,
responsibilities, and levels of authority, in professional business settings. The process promotes,
maintains and sustains the efficiency of professional functions in places of work. Effective
Management Communication enables appropriate transmission of interpersonal information and
social messages, within and between organisations.
The content of this book is well articulated to help readers to fully comprehend the process,
components and fundamental principles of Management Communication. The textbook is well
structured to enable students, future and current managers to understand the basic principles of
communication that are applied to different business contexts and situations. The book helps
readers to become familiar with interactive requirements of the communication process and how to
address barriers and create solutions when faced with communication challenges.
Readers of this book will also develop unique skills that can be applied when preparing for job
interviews, both as a potential employer and as a prospective employee. In this respect, the
interviewing process is well covered in the textbook. In addition, readers of this book will be able to
recognize various media modes of communication, evaluate mass communication methods, and
advertising methods. The proper procedure to follow when conducting and record meetings is also
addressed in the textbook.
The textbook also covers the use appropriate language in speech and writing, correspondence for
business matters and advertising, logical verbal and written report writing and the development of
listening skills. Each chapter is accompanied by a summary covering the key learning outcomes. The
format of the content also comprises instructions for practical application that prompts revision.
The application of skills in simulated exercises is included in the coursework, as a strategy for testing of
knowledge in a practical way.
The aim of this book is to equip readers with the knowledge and skills for communicating
effectively in the business world. Learning outcomes of chapters presented in this course, explain
many factors that shape norms, ethics and practices in terms of self-development, interpersonal and
social exchange processes. This allows learners to understand communication principles and
processes that surround workplace interactions.

1. Introduction
This first chapter introduces the important principles that underpin the management of
communication, before moving on to identify factors that impact on communication
processes. The chapter will discuss how the communication process enables sharing of
information and ideas between people with different perceptions in different contexts.
1.1 The meaning of Management communication
The process of management communication is central to the human desire to improve
one’s own achievements and position, alongside managing workplace social relations and
creating a harmonious working relationship between management and employees. The
New Oxford Dictionary (1998) defines the verb communicate as: “to share or exchange
information, news or ideas” Management is defined as: “the responsibility for dealing with, or
controlling people and things”.
As a person speaking or writing seriously, you seek to communicate with others in various
settings that you may have experienced, or expect to find yourself in future. Management
communication is the formal interaction between the organisation’s leadership and its
workers. This process enables the management team to formally convey important
information to its workers using various formal platforms.
12

11
Understanding Management Communication principles is the central concern of this
chapter. In this regard, the chapter is designed to expose Why effective management
communication is important in the working world. The chapter will show that as a
constant flow of spoken and written information, management communication aims to:
 Provide information and ideas to another, or others in the place of work;
 Obtain relevant and accurate information to facilitate work process;
 Negotiate agreements with co-workers, employers, suppliers and clients;
 Establish sound relationships with work colleagues and clients;
 Avoid confusion and improve clarity to increase productivity in the
organisation;
 Provide efficient services to clients;
 Identify and solve work related problems logically;
 Encourage discussion to ensure effective teamwork;
 Prevent conflict at work and overcome information transfer interferences;
 Reduce risks of mistakes, complaints or legal action;
 Ensure that the organisation’s vision, goals and aims are well articulated and
well understood by everyone in the organisation;
 Promote and maintain multicultural public relations and a sustainably good
public image for the organisation.

1.2 Principles and process of communication
Communication is done in diverse settings. In management, communication focus is on
ways that business people employ to convey information and messages from one source to
the other. In this information exchange, senders and recipients of messages could be indi-
vidual partners or employers, clients or customers and employees, who could be within a
large group of people, or small groups or teams.
The exchange of information invokes different perspectives in order to generate a common
understanding between parties. The communication process conveys information in mes-
sages, to a receiver who responds.

In the communication process, who encodes what message, how it is conveyed, to whom,
with what method? Also ponder on which elements make for two-way communication
processes. A key point is that the outcome of the communication process, is subject to
many factors. Key variables in the communication process are:
 Context of communication is surrounded by different circumstances;
 Mutual understanding of messages is subject to interference;
 Interferences affect interpretations.
Two-way communication is subject to many variables. In principle these variables are
broadly related to the context, or setting, and circumstances surrounding the
communication process. In a classical model that remains in use, elements of the
communication process are represented in a circulating sequence.

The model above shows that within a particular Context, a Sender initiates the
communication process. A Message is encoded, for a particular channel, to convey the
information to a destination, so the Code is the message itself.
This code is designed for an appropriate Channel for sending the encoded message to the
Receiver, who then reacts. Feedback is then expressed to the sender, whether negative or
positive.
Throughout the communication process, Interferences (noise) may affect the
interpretation of the sender’s message as it is received, as well as affecting feedback from
the receiver as it reaches the sender.
Each identified element illustrated by Jakobson in this model is further explained as:
 Context is the setting, or circumstances that surround the communication
process.
 Sender or addresser is the source of the information or message.
 Message is the information and ideas to be conveyed to another, or others.
During the communication process, the sender’s messages and the receiver’s
responses may be verbal or non-verbal, direct or indirect, and conscious or
unconscious.
 Code is the message itself, as ideas and information are encoded in some form.
 Channel is the medium for transmitting the encoded message to a destination.
 Receiver is the recipient(s) or addressee(s) of the intended message.
 Interference, described as “noise”, is the barrier that halt the communication
process causing communication breakdown. This “noise” may be caused by
factors within (internal) or around (external) the sender and the receiver.
 Feedback is an expressed reaction to the message, whether negative or positive,
feedback indicates the receiver’s understanding and successful communication.
Feedback indicates the receiver’s understanding and shows how successful the
communication is. Feedback informs the sender of whether adaptations to the message,
code or channel, may be necessary, in order to improve the extent to which the intended
message has been understood. Thus, the ability to give feedback is a reflection of
overcoming communication barriers or an intention to do so through seeking further
clarity.

1.3 Categories of Communication
Different types of communication that are central to managing people in the working
world of professionals and business reveal three significant and distinct categories, as
given below.
1.3.1 Intrapersonal Communication
Intrapersonal communication describes the communication that occurs within oneself. This
type of communication is derived from the Latin root term ‘intra’, meaning within, as a
prefix to the adjective ‘personal’.
17

11
Forms of communicating within oneself include thinking things over, writing our thoughts
and ideas, and making comments to ourselves. This process often takes place as
self-introspection.
At an individual level, what you envisage and plan, helps to organize your thoughts. We
may praise ourselves, be more or less encouraging, or inflict harm on our self-image. Self-
reflection and other intrapersonal activities, such as day-dreaming and meditating are all
intrapersonal communication processes that shape an individual’s self-image.
1.3.2 Interpersonal Communication
Interpersonal communication refers to a two-way information exchange that occurs
between a person and with other persons. It is derived from the Latin root term ‘inter’,
meaning between or among, as a prefix to the adjective ‘personal’. This two-way
transmission, of messages and feedback, may be among or between individuals, within
groups or between groups. Different forms of interpersonal communication have a bearing
on features in the behaviors of both senders and receivers, as illustrated with examples in

1.3.3 Mass Communication
Mass communication transmits information to a large-scale public audience at the same
time. The sender chooses the most suitable channels available in sending one message
simultaneously to many receivers in different places. The recipients will interpret the
message individually. Mass communications are not guaranteed to reach target audiences,
and may or may not get feedback.
Mediums used for such messages are chosen from a wide spectrum to fit the purpose of
achieving objectives of the sender. The intention is that receivers are persuaded to adopt a
point of view that is desirable to the sender. Media options in mass communication include
a spectrum of audio-visual aids, chosen as most suitable for:
 Achieving the objective of the sender;
 Meeting needs and expectations of the target audience.
Recently, computers and cellular technology, with evolving internet and programme
applications are overtaking previous reliance on newspapers and magazines, radio and
television, the public library and film in mass communication. Websites and social
networks are widely available to accommodate ever larger groups and recipients.
20

21
In mass communication, there are differences in the type of channels, scale of
communication and in the scope for feedback from target receivers.
Internet Communication Technology has introduced fresh challenges, as norms and
practices evolve in mass communication. Ensuring that target receivers, whether
individuals, smaller groups, or larger audiences, gain understanding of the subject and the
information conveyed in a sender’s message. Currently, the widespread use of media
channels frequently leads to combinations that supplement written contacts through Sms
and social media, with telephonic conversations, which may precede the direct contact of
meeting.
Dealing with different communication situations, requires different approaches that
communicators adapt to deal with combinations of factors that influence communication.
Understanding that negative attitudes of senders or receivers, may be due to factors that
are inherently human, including social factors, will assist your approaches. Whether
formally speaking to large or small groups, or informally talking with just one person,
many well-tried ways to communicate effectively makes use of fundamental skills. These
are skills that are used to prepare, to be alert during exchanges, and to identify factors that
influenced communication processes.
In mass communication, delayed Receiver feedback remains a challenge. Due to the
technical complexity of mass media channels, the transmission of one-way information is
subject to magnified interferences in efforts to “connect” with others. Preparation of
information contained in a faceless message, is thus subject to distortions.
