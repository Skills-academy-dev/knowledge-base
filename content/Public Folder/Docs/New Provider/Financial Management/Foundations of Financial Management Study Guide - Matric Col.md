INTRODUCTION

Financial management is an important part of running any business. It helps business

owners plan, organise, and control how money is used. This module introduces you to the

basic ideas of financial management. You will learn what it is, why it matters, and how it

helps businesses succeed. The module also explains the role of the financial manager and

introduces the three key financial goals—profitability, liquidity, and solvency. Lastly, you will

learn useful financial terms that are used throughout the textbook.

Unit 1.1: What is Financial Management?

This unit explains the meaning of financial management. It explores how businesses plan,

organise, control, and monitor their money to reach their goals. You will learn about the

main activities in financial management, such as budgeting, making financial decisions, and

tracking income and spending. This unit also includes examples of how businesses apply

financial management in real life.

Read pages 11— 14 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 1.2: Why Businesses Need Financial Management

This unit explores the reasons why all businesses—large and small—need proper financial

management. It explains how financial management helps a business control its spending,

avoid cash shortages, grow, and make smart financial decisions. You will see how poor

money management can cause a business to fail, and how proper planning can help a

business succeed.

Read pages 15—17 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 1.3: The Role of a Financial Manager

This unit explains who a financial manager is and what they do. It describes their main

responsibilities, such as creating budgets, managing cash flow, preparing financial reports,

and advising business owners. You will also learn what skills a good financial manager

needs and how they help a business make the best use of its money.

Read pages 18—19 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

business.

This unit introduces key financial terms that will help you understand the rest of the

textbook. You will learn simple definitions for terms such as income, expenses, profit, loss,

assets, liabilities, and equity. Real-life examples are included to help make these terms

easier to understand.

Read pages 23—25 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

INTRODUCTION

Accounting is the language of business. It helps people record and understand where

money comes from and where it goes. In this module, you will learn the meaning and

purpose of accounting. You will also discover who uses accounting information and what

they use it for. The module covers the five elements of financial statements—assets,

liabilities, equity, income, and expenses. You will also be introduced to the basic

accounting equation, and learn how financial information is recorded during an accounting

period using transactions.

Unit 2.1: Definition of Accounting

This unit explains what accounting is and why it is important for any business. You will

learn that accounting is the process of recording, organising, and reporting money matters

so that business owners and managers can make informed decisions. This unit also

explores the functions of accounting and how it helps to keep a business organised and

successful.

Read pages 32—35 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 2.2: Users of Financial Information

This unit explores the different people and groups who need accounting information. These

users include internal users (such as owners and managers) and external users (such as

banks, investors, and SARS). You will learn what information each group needs and why

accurate financial records are important for making good decisions.

Read pages 35—37 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 2.3: Elements of Financial Statements: Assets, Liabilities, Equity,

Income, and Expenses

This unit introduces the five key parts of any financial statement. You will learn the meaning

of: each of the following:

•

•

•

•

•

Assets – what the business owns.

Liabilities – what the business owes.

Equity – what the owner owns after debts are paid.

Income – money earned by the business.

Expenses – costs paid to run the business.

these elements work together to show the financial health of a business.

Read pages 37—40 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 2.4: The Basic Accounting Equation: Assets = Equity + Liabilities

This unit explains the accounting equation, which is the foundation of all accounting. You

will learn how everything a business owns (assets) is either owed to others (liabilities) or

belongs to the owner (equity). The unit uses real-life examples to show how the equation

stays in balance and helps to keep accurate financial records.

Read pages 41—43 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 2.5: Accounting Periods and Transactions

This unit explains how financial information is grouped into time periods called accounting

periods. You will learn that during each period, businesses record every activity involving

money—these are called transactions. The unit shows examples of different transactions

and explains why it is important to keep accurate records during each period.

Read pages 44—46 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

INTRODUCTION

The accounting cycle is the step-by-step process that businesses use to keep accurate and

organised financial records. It begins when a transaction takes place and ends with the

preparation of financial statements. In this module, you will learn how each step in the cycle

helps a business record, check, and report its financial activities. You will also explore

source documents, double-entry bookkeeping, the general journal, the general ledger, the

trial balance, and common accounting errors. These tools and processes are essential for

producing reliable financial information.

Unit 3.1: The Steps in the Accounting Cycle

This unit explains the eight main steps in the accounting cycle. It begins with identifying

transactions and ends with preparing financial statements and closing accounts. You will

learn why each step matters and how following the cycle helps a business stay organised

and accurate in recording financial information.

Read pages 54—57 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 3.2: Source Documents and Transaction Analysis

This unit introduces source documents—like receipts, invoices, and bank statements—

which serve as proof of financial transactions. You will learn how to use these documents

to analyse transactions and decide which accounts are affected. Understanding how to

interpret source documents is the first step in recording transactions correctly.

Read pages 58—60 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 3.3: Double-entry Bookkeeping

This unit explains the principle of double-entry bookkeeping, which means every

transaction is recorded in two accounts: one as a debit and the other as a credit. You will

learn how this system keeps the accounting equation balanced and ensures accuracy in

the financial records.

Read pages 61—63 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Foundations of Financial Management | Study Guide | Matric College Publishing

17

Unit 3.4: General Journal and General Ledger

This unit explores the general journal and general ledger—two key tools used to record and

organise transactions. You will learn how transactions are first written in the journal and

then posted to the ledger. These steps help prepare for later tasks such as drawing up a

trial balance and financial statements.

Read pages 63—65 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 3.5: Trial Balance: Purpose and Preparation

This unit teaches you how to prepare a trial balance. A trial balance is a summary that

checks whether all debits and credits from the ledger are equal. If the trial balance is

correct, it means that the basic recordings have been done properly. You will learn how to

list account balances and check that everything adds up.

Read pages 66—68 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 3.6: Common Errors and How to Correct Them

This unit explains that even with care, errors can happen in accounting. You will learn

about different types of errors—those that affect the trial balance and those that do not. The

unit also teaches you how to find and correct mistakes using journal entries. These skills

are important for keeping accurate records.

Read pages 68— 71of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Summary

This module teaches the steps to record and check financial records.

•

•

•

•

•

The cycle includes 8 steps from transaction to final reports.

Source documents prove that transactions happened.

Double-entry means recording each item twice—debit and credit.

Use journals and ledgers to sort records.

Trial balances check accuracy and help find errors.

INTRODUCTION

Financial statements are important reports that show how well a business is performing.

They provide a clear picture of what the business owns, what it owes, how much money it

earns, and how much profit or loss it makes. In this module, you will learn about the four

main financial statements—what they are, what information they show, and how to read

and understand them. These statements help business owners and other users make

smart financial decisions and plan for the future.

Unit 4.1: Overview of Financial Statements

This unit introduces the four main types of financial statements and explains the purpose of

each. You will learn how these statements give different kinds of information about a

business’s financial health. Each statement plays a special role in helping owners,

managers, and investors understand how the business is doing.

Read pages 79—82 of your textbook for more information on the following:

•

4.1.1: The Income Statement (Statement of Comprehensive Income)

This part explains how the income statement shows whether a business made a profit

or a loss over a certain period. It lists all the income earned and expenses paid. You

will learn how to use the income statement to find out if the business is successful.

•

4.1.2: The Balance Sheet  (Statement of Financial Position)

This section introduces the balance sheet, also called the statement of financial

position. It shows what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and how

much belongs to the owner (equity) at a specific point in time.

•

4.1.3: The Cash Flow Statement

Here, you will learn how the cash flow statement shows the movement of money into

and out of the business. It covers daily business activities, investments, and loans,

and shows whether the business has enough cash to operate.

•

4.1.4: Statement of Changes in Equity

This part explains how this statement shows changes in the owner’s capital, including

profit or loss and drawings. It helps owners track the value of their investment in the

business.

Unit 4.2: Structure and Components of Each Statement

This unit breaks down the layout and important parts of each financial statement. You will

learn how each one is arranged and what information is included. Understanding the

Read pages 82— 85 of your textbook for more information on the following:

•

4.2.1: Income Statement Structure

This section explains how to set up an income statement by listing income, expenses,

4.2.2: Balance Sheet Structure

This part explains how to set up a statement showing how the owner’s equity has

changed over time. It includes capital contributions, profits, and drawings.

Unit 4.3: Interpreting Financial Results

This unit teaches you how to read and understand what financial statements are saying.

You will learn to ask key questions—like whether the business is making a profit, paying its

bills, and growing in value. It also explains how all the financial statements are connected

and introduces simple ratios to help measure business performance.

Read pages 86— 88 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

INTRODUCTION

Every business wants to know if it is making a profit. The income statement is the financial

report that answers this question. It compares how much money a business earns with how

much it spends. In this module, you will learn how to understand and prepare an income

statement. You will also learn how to calculate gross profit, net profit, and important ratios

that help measure how well a business is performing. These tools help business owners

make better financial decisions.

Unit 5.1: What is an Income Statement?

This unit introduces the income statement, also known as the profit and loss statement.

You will learn that this report shows income, expenses, and the profit or loss over a specific

period. The unit also explains who uses the income statement and how it helps with

financial planning and reporting.

Read pages 96—97 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 5.2: Calculating Gross Profit and Net Profit

This unit explains how to calculate two important profits in a business: gross profit and net

profit. You will learn simple formulas for both, and work through examples to see how much

a business really earns after paying for goods and other costs. These calculations help

show whether a business is earning enough to cover its expenses and make a profit.

Read pages 98—99 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 5.3: Understanding Income and Expenses

This unit explores the two key parts of an income statement: income and expenses. You

will learn what counts as income (like sales or interest) and what counts as expenses (like

rent, wages, and electricity). Understanding these will help you keep track of where a

business’s money comes from and how it is spent.

Read pages 100—102 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Foundations of Financial Management | Study Guide | Matric College Publishing

Unit 5.4: Profitability Ratios: Gross Profit Margin, Net Profit Margin

This unit introduces profitability ratios that show how much profit a business makes from its

sales. You will learn how to calculate the gross profit margin and the net profit margin using

simple formulas. These ratios help measure how well a business is managing its income

and expenses and whether it is financially healthy.

Read pages 102— 104 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Summary

This module focuses on how to read profits and losses.

•

•

•

•

The income statement shows income, expenses, and profits.

Gross profit = Sales – Cost of Sales.

Net profit = Gross Profit – Expenses.

Profitability ratios show how well a business earns money.

INTRODUCTION

The balance sheet is one of the most important financial statements in business. It shows

what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and how much of the business

belongs to the owner (equity) at a specific moment in time. In this module, you will learn

what a balance sheet is, how to read it, and how to understand the information it gives. You

will also explore how to identify current and non-current items, calculate owner’s equity,

and judge whether a business is financially strong or struggling.

Unit 6.1: What is a Balance Sheet?

This unit explains the purpose and layout of a balance sheet. You will learn that a balance

sheet gives a snapshot of the business’s financial position at a certain date. It shows the

relationship between assets, liabilities, and owner’s equity, using the basic accounting

equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. The unit also includes examples to show how the

balance sheet stays balanced.

Read pages 112—114 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 6.2: Current and Non-current Assets and Liabilities

In this unit, you will learn the difference between current and non-current assets and

liabilities. Current items are expected to be used or paid within 12 months, while

non-current items are used or paid over a longer period. You will explore common

examples such as cash, stock, vehicles, loans, and creditors. Knowing the difference helps

with budgeting and planning for the short and long term.

Read pages 114—117 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 6.3: Owner’s Equity and Capital

This unit explores the owner’s share in the business, called equity. You will learn how

equity is calculated by subtracting liabilities from assets, and how it includes capital

contributions, profit, and drawings. The unit also shows how profits and withdrawals affect

equity and why keeping track of these changes is important for understanding business

value.

Read pages 117—119 of your textbook for more information on this topic.

Unit 6.4: How to Read and Interpret a Balance Sheet

This unit teaches you how to read a balance sheet and understand what it says about the

financial health of a business. You will learn how to check if assets are greater than

liabilities, if equity is increasing, and whether the business can pay its debts. The unit

includes tips, real-life examples, and key questions to ask when interpreting a balance

sheet.
