Introduction

This module introduces you to the basic principles and structure of human anatomy. It
explains how the body is organised and how its parts work together to maintain health and
balance. You will explore key concepts such as cells, tissues, organs, and systems, and learn
the correct anatomical language used to describe body regions and directions. Understanding
these foundations is essential for studying skin science and for performing safe, effective
skincare and body treatments.

Unit 1: Introduction to Anatomy

This unit defines anatomy and outlines its importance in understanding the structure of the
human body, especially for those working in skin and body science.

Read pages 12–15 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

1.1 Definition, Scope, and Importance of Anatomy
Anatomy is the scientific study of body structure. It provides the foundation for understanding
how the body functions and supports professional skincare practice.

1.2 Divisions of Anatomy (Gross, Microscopic, and Surface Anatomy)
Gross anatomy examines structures visible to the naked eye; microscopic anatomy studies
cells and tissues; and surface anatomy relates internal structures to external landmarks.

1.3 Anatomical Terms of Reference
Anatomical terms provide a standard way to describe body parts and positions—such as
anterior (front), posterior (back), medial (toward the midline), and lateral (away from the
midline).

1.4 Practical Identification of Body Regions
The body is divided into main regions head, neck, trunk, and limbs each important in
understanding treatment areas and body mapping.

Unit 2: The Structural Organisation of the Body

This unit explains how the human body is built from simple to complex structures, showing
how cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs form systems.

Read pages 16–18 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

2.1 Levels of Structural Organisation (Cells → Tissues → Organs → Systems)
The body is organised hierarchically: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form
systems, and systems make up the organism.

2.2 Overview of the Major Organ Systems (Structure Only)
There are eleven organ systems in the human body, each with distinct structures that perform
vital roles—such as the circulatory, nervous, and integumentary systems.

2.3 How Anatomical Form Relates to Visible Features
The shape and structure of the body determine external features, such as muscle tone and
skin contours, that are visible during treatment.

11

2.4 Linking Anatomy to Professional Observation
Anatomical knowledge helps professionals interpret external appearance, posture, and skin
condition to provide safe and accurate care.

Unit 3: Anatomical Planes and Body Orientation

This unit introduces the standard body positions, planes, and cavities used to describe and
locate internal organs accurately.

Read pages 19–22 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

3.1 Anatomical Position and Body Planes
The anatomical position is the standard reference stance; body planes—sagittal, frontal, and
transverse divide the body for clear description.

3.2 Body Cavities and Structural Landmarks
Major body cavities, such as cranial, thoracic, and abdominal, house and protect vital organs
within the body.

3.3 Surface Anatomy in Skin Examination
Recognising visible landmarks like veins, muscles, and bony structures helps professionals
assess skin health and plan treatments accurately.

Unit 4: Cells and Microscopic Structure

This unit explores the basic unit of life—the cell and introduces microscopic anatomy, which
helps us understand tissues and their function.

Read pages 23–25 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

4.1 Cell Shape and Structure Overview
Cells vary in shape and size but share basic parts such as the nucleus, cytoplasm, and cell
membrane, which regulate activity and protection.

4.2 Microscopic Anatomy (Histology) Introduction
Histology studies tissues under the microscope, revealing the intricate arrangement of cells
that form body structures.

4.3 Common Staining and Slide Observation Techniques
Special dyes and microscopes are used to make tissues visible, allowing scientists to identify
cell types and abnormalities.

4.4 Practical Link: Viewing Skin Cells Under a Microscope
Observing epithelial cells from the skin helps learners connect theoretical anatomy to
real-world applications.

Unit 5: The Tissues of the Human Body

This unit explains the four main types of tissues and their functions in supporting and
protecting the body.

Read pages 26–29 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

12

5.1 Definition and Classification of Tissues
Tissues are groups of similar cells performing a common function; they are classified as
epithelial, connective, muscle, or nervous tissue.

5.2 Epithelial, Connective, Muscle, and Nervous Tissue Structures
Each tissue type has unique structures: epithelial covers surfaces, connective supports,
muscle enables movement, and nervous tissue transmits signals.

5.3 Histological Features of Skin Tissues
Epithelial and connective tissues form the main layers of the skin, influencing elasticity,
thickness, and repair.

5.4 Identification Exercises: Tissue Slides and Diagrams
Examining tissue samples under magnification helps identify structural differences and
recognise their appearance in skin.

Unit 6: Introduction to the Integumentary System (Structure Only)

This unit provides an overview of the skin, hair, and nails, forming the integumentary system
that protects and supports the body.

Read pages 30–32 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

6.1 Structural Overview of the Skin, Hair, and Nails
The integumentary system includes the skin and its appendages, which act as a barrier and
maintain internal balance.

6.2 Basic Identification of Skin Layers
The skin consists of three layers—epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis—each with distinct
structures and functions.

6.3 Structural Link Between Skin and Body Framework
The skin connects with underlying muscles and bones, influencing appearance, tone, and
movement.

This module introduced the foundations of anatomy and its importance to understanding
human structure and function.

● Explained levels of body organisation from cells to systems.
● Described body planes, orientation, and cavities.
● Identified cells and tissues and their roles in the human body.

Introduction

This module examines the skin as a complex organ made up of multiple layers and structures
that protect, support, and regulate the body. You will explore the organisation of the
epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis, and how their composition contributes to the skin’s
strength, flexibility, and protective function. The module also covers appendages such as hair,
nails, and glands, and introduces microscopic analysis techniques for observing skin tissue
structure.

Unit 1: Overview of the Skin as an Organ

This unit provides a general overview of the skin as an organ and highlights its visible and
structural characteristics.

Read pages 39–41 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

1.1 Classification of skin (thick vs. thin)
Skin can be classified as thick or thin based on epidermal thickness and the presence or
absence of certain layers. Thick skin occurs on the palms and soles, while thin skin covers
most of the body.

1.2 Surface anatomy and regional differences
The appearance of the skin varies by region due to differences in thickness, pigmentation,
and the presence of appendages such as hair and glands.

1.3 The skin as a protective covering (structural perspective only)
Structurally, the skin forms a durable outer barrier that shields the body from physical
damage, microorganisms, and water loss.

Unit 2: The Epidermis

This unit explores the structure, cell types, and arrangement of the epidermal layer of the
skin.

Read pages 42–44 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

2.1 Layers of the epidermis (stratum basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum,
corneum)
The epidermis is composed of five layers that form a continuous surface from deep to
superficial, each with distinct cellular characteristics.

2.2 Types of cells: keratinocytes, melanocytes, Langerhans, Merkel cells
These specialised cells perform vital roles—keratinocytes build protection, melanocytes
provide pigment, Langerhans defend against pathogens, and Merkel cells sense touch.

2.3 Arrangement and visual features of epidermal layers
Cells are arranged in an orderly progression from the regenerative basal layer to the flattened
keratinised surface, visible under a microscope as distinct strata.

2.4 Microscopic observation of epidermis slides
Laboratory observation allows identification of each epidermal layer and the different cell
types that maintain its structure and function.

15

Unit 3: The Dermis

This unit examines the dermis as the supportive and connective layer beneath the epidermis.

Read pages 45–48 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

3.1 Structural divisions: papillary and reticular layers
The dermis is divided into two main regions: the papillary layer, containing capillaries and
sensory endings, and the reticular layer, containing dense connective tissue.

3.2 Collagen, elastin, and connective tissue arrangement
Collagen provides strength while elastin fibres give flexibility; their arrangement determines
skin texture and resilience.

3.3 Nerves, blood vessels, and their structural placement
The dermis houses a rich network of blood vessels and nerves that support nourishment,
thermoregulation, and sensory function.

3.4 Clinical relevance: structure of wrinkles and scars
Ageing and injury alter dermal collagen and elastin fibres, leading to visible changes such as
wrinkles and scar formation.

Unit 4: The Subcutaneous Layer (Hypodermis)

This unit describes the hypodermis, the deepest layer of the skin, and its role in protection
and insulation.

Read pages 49–51 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

4.1 Composition: connective tissue and fat
The hypodermis is composed mainly of loose connective tissue and adipose cells that
cushion and insulate the body.

4.2 Relationship between hypodermis and deeper tissues
It connects the skin to underlying muscles and organs, allowing flexibility and movement
without tearing.

4.3 Structural role in body shape and contour
Fat distribution in the hypodermis contributes to body contour and visible shape variations
between individuals.

4.4 Application: subcutaneous injections and skin thickness
This layer is used for subcutaneous injections due to its vascular structure and consistent
absorption capacity.

Unit 5: Skin Appendages

This unit focuses on structures associated with the skin, such as hair, nails, and glands.

Read pages 52-55 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

16

5.1 Hair: follicle, root, shaft, bulb structure
Each hair grows from a follicle that extends into the dermis, with a root and bulb responsible
for nourishment and growth.

5.2 Nails: matrix, bed, and plate anatomy
Nails consist of keratinised plates that protect fingertips and toes, supported by the matrix
and nail bed for continuous growth.

5.3 Sweat and sebaceous glands: structural anatomy
Sweat glands assist in temperature control, while sebaceous glands secrete oil to lubricate
the skin and hair.

5.4 Observation activity: identifying appendages on diagrams
Students should locate and label hair follicles, glands, and nail structures on anatomical
diagrams to reinforce structural understanding.

Unit 6: Microscopic and Structural Analysis of Skin

This unit introduces histological methods used to observe and evaluate skin tissue under a
microscope.

Read pages 56–58 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

6.1 Histological preparation and observation methods
Proper sample preparation, staining, and microscope use allow for detailed observation of
skin tissue structure.

6.2 Cellular layering patterns
Microscopic examination reveals the orderly arrangement of epidermal and dermal cells that
maintain skin integrity.

6.3 Identifying normal versus structurally altered skin samples
Comparing healthy and damaged samples helps recognise pathological changes such as
thinning, thickening, or scarring.

This module provided a detailed study of the structure and composition of the human skin.
● Identified thick and thin skin classifications.
● Explained the organisation and cellular arrangement of the epidermis, dermis, and
hypodermis.
● Describe the structural features of skin appendages.
● Introduced microscopic observation techniques to identify normal and altered skin.

Introduction

This module focuses on the visible and surface features of the human body. It explores how
anatomical landmarks, muscle attachments, and skin variations appear across different
regions such as the face, neck, limbs, trunk, and feet. Understanding these features helps
practitioners perform accurate assessments and adapt treatments to specific body zones.
Regional anatomy provides the foundation for safe and effective skin, massage, and
aesthetic care.

Unit 1: Head and Face Anatomy

This unit identifies major structural landmarks of the face and explains their importance in
professional practice.

Read pages 65–71 of your textbook for more information on the topics below:

1.1 Structural landmarks: forehead, eyes, cheeks, chin
The head and face contain prominent bony and muscular landmarks that guide analysis and
treatment placement, such as the frontal bone, zygomatic arches, and mandible.

1.2 Facial muscle attachments (structural overview)
Facial muscles attach directly to the skin, allowing for expression and movement—an
essential factor in massage and facial techniques.

1.3 Anatomical zones of the face for skin care and treatment

3.1 Skin structure on arms and hands
The skin of the arms is thinner than on the palms, with fewer glands and greater exposure to
environmental factors.

3.2 Nail bed and cuticle structure
The nail bed supports the nail plate, while the cuticle seals the base against infection—key to
professional hand and nail care.

3.3 Anatomical differences in palm vs. dorsal hand
Palmar skin is thicker, hairless, and features ridges for grip, while the dorsal side is thinner
and more flexible.
