1.1 Project Fundamentals

People from all walks of life have been managing projects for many years without even really noticing it – think of the pyramids in Egypt, for example. These ancient structures are early examples of project management. Over time, the concept of managing projects has evolved, and today it plays a crucial role in various fields such as construction, information technology, healthcare, and many more.

To begin with, you need to understand what a project is and what it is not. A project is a unique effort that creates something new, such as a product or service, which does not exist yet. This means that a project is distinct because it generates a new service or product that hasn't been produced before.

A project is not a daily business process that has a definite start and finish date that has to be met. This is very important because a large part of the project effort is committed to guaranteeing that the project is finished on time. Daily operations are ongoing and repetitive, like managing a store or maintaining a website. In contrast, projects are temporary and have specific goals. They start with an idea and end when the goal is achieved.

To ensure that the project is finalised on time, schedules are planned to show when tasks should be started and completed. Projects can last for days, months, or even years, depending on the type of project. Proper planning and scheduling are key to completing projects efficiently and effectively.

Example: Consider the construction of a school. This project starts with an idea and a goal to build a new school. It involves specific stages such as planning the design, securing permits, constructing the building, and finally, completing and opening the school. The project is temporary and ends once the school is operational.

1.1.1 The stages of a project

A project goes through a number of stages to reach its end goal. Understanding these stages helps in managing the project effectively. The five stages of a project are:

1\. Initiation

2\. Planning

3\. Execution

4\. Monitor and Control

5\. Review

Stages of a project

Initiation

The initiation stage of the project is when the main focus and target of the project are determined. The project might begin with a simple suggestion or request from a client or from management. During this stage, the project's purpose is defined, and its feasibility is assessed. This involves understanding the project's objectives, its scope, and identifying the stakeholders involved.

Once the focus and expected outcomes have been established, the project manager is responsible for calculating a budget, creating time schedules, and assembling a project team to get the project up and running. This stage sets the foundation for the project's success, so it's crucial to get it right.

Example: A company decides to develop a new software application. In the initiation stage, the purpose of the software is defined, its feasibility is assessed, and key stakeholders such as potential users and developers are identified.

Planning

Once the initiation of the project has been completed and you have all the information you need about the project, the overall planning can start. The planning stage involves developing a detailed project plan that outlines the project's scope, objectives, and the steps needed to achieve them.

The project plan has to be clear so that the whole team knows exactly what is expected of them, what their individual tasks will be, and when these tasks need to be completed. This stage includes creating schedules, setting deadlines, and allocating resources. Effective planning helps to ensure that the project progresses smoothly and efficiently.

Example: For the software development project, the planning stage involves creating a timeline for the development process, setting milestones, and assigning tasks to different team members such as designers, programmers, and testers.

Execution

In the execution stage, the team will begin the tasks assigned to them. This is where the project plan is put into action. The project manager oversees the execution process, ensuring that team members have everything they need to start their part of the project.

The execution stage usually includes the project manager having face-to-face meetings with the team members to make sure that everyone is on the same page and has everything they need to start executing their part of the project. Getting the group started on the right track is vital to the project's success, so discussing the project plan and schedule clearly with the team is essential for achieving a successful outcome.

11 | Page

Example: During the execution stage of the software development project, the designers

create the user interface, programmers write the code, and testers check the software for bugs.

Monitor and Control

Once the project has started, the project manager will start monitoring and controlling the team. This involves tracking the project's progress, ensuring that tasks are being completed on time, and that the project stays within budget. The purpose of this stage is to identify any issues early on and take corrective action to keep the project on track.

There are different ways to monitor and control the project. Having weekly meetings or site check-ins is a good way to keep track of everyone's progress. By discussing the progress of the project and checking on everyone, the project manager can adjust the initial plans when needed to ensure that the project continues to run smoothly.

Example: In the software development project, the project manager monitors the progress of the coding, ensures that deadlines are being met, and checks that the project remains within the allocated budget.

Review

The review stage is the final stage of the project. When the project has been completed, a project team meeting should be scheduled to assess and discuss the successes and failures of the project. This stage involves evaluating the project to determine if it met its objectives and identifying any lessons learned.

Everything discussed in this meeting should be documented so that you can go back and re-do or fix something if necessary. This is the perfect time for the team as a whole to discuss their experiences, as well as where they succeeded or failed during the process. The insights gained from this review can be used to improve future projects.

Example: Once the software application is completed, the team holds a review meeting to discuss what went well, what challenges they faced, and how the process can be improved for future projects.

1.1.2 Characteristics of a successful project

When it comes to determining a project's success, there are four factors that need to be looked at:

• Objectives

• Cost

• Satisfaction

• Time

Factors that determine a project’s success

Objectives

The first question to ask is, "Did we meet the project objectives?" When the project is complete, the group needs to examine the original goal set and identify whether they have been met. Meeting the project objectives means delivering the desired outcome as specified at the start of the project.

Example: If the objective was to develop a software application with specific features, the project is successful if these features are implemented as planned.

Cost

Next, you need to determine whether you managed to stay within the planned budget for the project. The team members are required to complete their tasks without compromising on quality within budget. Ideally, at the end of the project, the total cost of the expenses should be lower than the initial budget. Efficient cost management is crucial for the financial success of the project.

Example: If the software development project was budgeted at R500,000, and it was completed for R480,000, the project has successfully managed its costs.

Satisfaction

The manager and the rest of the team should feel pleased with their outcome and what they achieved. If the project was done for a client, they should also be satisfied with the end results before it can be seen as a project well done. Client satisfaction is a key indicator of project success, as it reflects the quality and effectiveness of the project outcome.

Example: If the client is happy with the software application and it meets their needs, the project is considered successful in terms of satisfaction.

13 | Page

Time

Was the project completed on time? This is the final factor for a successful project. The team members should work and stay within the time frame given at the beginning of the project. If at the end they completed the project within the time frame and everything else is also a success and in working order, only then can it be labelled as a successful project. Timely completion is essential to meet the project's goals and maintain the client's trust.

Example: If the software development project was scheduled to be completed in six months and it was delivered on time, the project has achieved its time objective.

In conclusion, understanding the fundamentals of project management and the stages involved is essential for managing projects successfully. Proper planning, execution, and monitoring are key to ensuring that projects meet their objectives, stay within budget, and are completed on time.

When businesses have temporary needs that are outside the capacity of the individual employees' jobs, they often need a project team to address these issues. As a manager, it is your duty to acknowledge the necessity for a project team to get the job done, identify the sort of team needed for the project, and assign employees that you think would be best for the team.

1.2.1 Types of project teams

The size of your business and the importance of the project will determine the size of the team. While inside issues and customer problems might require only a few individuals to work on a project team, a regulation-based project team could call for staff members from several various departments in addition to legal guidance.

There are five different types of project teams from which you can make use of – depending on the project: client-based, company-based, technology-based, market-based and regulation-based.

1\. Client-based: Client-based project teams are put together to assist a client to fulfil a particular

need.

• Example: A consulting firm assembles a team to help a client improve their business processes.

2\. Company-based: Company-based project teams concentrate on the internal operations of a

company. For example, company policies and events.

• Example: A company forms a team to plan and execute an annual employee training program.

3\. Technology-based: Technology-based project teams deal with technical advancements that could influence the organisation. For example, a technology-based project team may be assigned to examine an organisation's computer system and network protection.

• Example: A team is created to upgrade the company's IT infrastructure to improve cybersecurity.

4\. Market-based: Market-based project teams concentrate on replying to market changes.

These changes may be caused by economic aspects such as variations in the rate of interest, the unemployment rate or taxes.

• Example: A marketing team is formed to develop strategies to increase sales during an economic downturn.

5\. Regulation-based: Regulation-based project groups review government requirements for

their sector and make sure that the organisation complies with the requirements. For example, a tyre manufacturer may produce a regulation-based team if the government criteria for the disposal of tyres has changed.

• Example: A manufacturing company forms a team to ensure compliance with new environmental regulations.

1.2.2 Project team dynamics

Just like projects, project teams also go through five stages. As the project develops and changes, the team members have to keep up and adjust to the dynamics of each stage.

The five stages are:

1\. Initiation

2\. Planning

3\. Execution

4\. Monitor and Control

5\. Review

Initiation

In the initiation stage, the team members are a bit shaky and they might feel a bit insecure about themselves. The project manager usually guides the team and ensures that everyone knows what they should do. The team members will start socialising with one another and team up with those they best communicate with.

Example: In a new project team, members might feel uncertain about their roles. The project manager helps them understand their tasks and encourages team-building activities to foster communication.

Planning

In the planning stage, there will be people who will be more open and talkative than others. These members will see themselves as the team leaders of the group. Everyone will start settling into their roles and will start taking responsibility for a certain task. The members who have formed bonds with other members will start brainstorming and sharing new ideas that might work better and save more money.

Example: During planning meetings, team members suggest different approaches to completing tasks and share ideas on how to improve efficiency.

Execution

In the execution stage, the team members will start distancing themselves from the project and their drive for finishing the project will start slowing down. This is where the project manager has to step in and keep the team members motivated and ambitious so the project can be finished on time.

16 | Page

Example: As the project progresses, some team members might lose focus. The project manager organises motivational meetings and sets short-term goals to keep the team engaged.

Monitor and Control

As the project is slowly but surely coming to an end, it is important to monitor and control the progress of the team. This is the make or break stage. If anything falls out of plan in this stage, it might cost you a successful project.

Example: The project manager regularly checks the progress, adjusts plans as necessary, and addresses any issues that arise to ensure the project stays on track.

Review

Finally, it is time for review. The project has come to an end and the team members will start going back to working in their old work schedules. They will go back to their everyday tasks and some will be reassigned to start new projects. Depending on the success of the project, team members may be very positive or be negative and agitated. In the latter case, the project manager will need to intervene

Example: After completing a project, the team holds a review meeting to discuss what went well and what could be improved. Positive feedback boosts morale, while constructive criticism helps avoid future mistakes.

1.2.3 Project team focus

Project teams have to stick together, set goals and stay focused while working on a project. There are six things that you can do to help your group stay focused and structured:

1\. Set goals

2\. Appoint tasks to team members

3\. Design a timetable with checkpoints (deadlines)

4\. Get team members committed and motivated

5\. Keep everyone up to date with progress

6\. Approach any setbacks in a smart way

Set Goals

The first thing a project team should do is to set its goals. Think about both the anticipated outcome and the desired outcome. The predicted result is the minimal outcome needed by the team and is normally decided on by the head of management or the department leaders before it gets assigned to the project team. The wanted result is anything better than the anticipated result.

17 | Page

Using the anticipated and wanted results, the group must start working on its goals. The goals must be understandable, measurable, challenging, and achievable. Goals that are not understandable will not be understood by all team members, which could lead to failure. Also, goals have to have clear specifications to allow their status to be measured. Goals ought to be challenging; yet if they are unachievable, team members may feel disappointed or indifferent.

Example: A project team sets a goal to increase customer satisfaction by 20% within six months by improving service response times and product quality.

Appoint Tasks to Team Members

The next step is to plan and hand out tasks. The tasks might be to take notes in the meeting or it might be working along with other teams in other departments. The tasks should be handed out to the team members as soon as a plan of action has been established.

The entire team has to fully understand what is expected of them. The team should also have a list available so they can see who is doing what. This will make it easier for them to communicate with the right person if there is a problem.

Example: In a software development project, tasks such as coding, testing, and documentation are assigned to specific team members based on their expertise.

Design a Timetable with Checkpoints (Deadlines)

The third step is to give team members a timeframe with deadlines to indicate when tasks should be completed. The timeframe should display when tasks should start and end and also when team members need to report on projects. A schedule such as this helps team members better manage their time and focus on different areas of the project. The team leader and members can also liaise better with each other by coordinating their deadlines. When a deadline is added, it should also include points of discussion in order to review the work that was done.

Example: A project timeline is created with key milestones such as design approval, prototype development, and final testing, along with specific deadlines for each task.

Get Team Members Committed and Motivated

Ensure that all team members understand why certain tasks need to be done. They should also know what outcomes are expected from their tasks as well as the total completion of the project. This will help motivate them to finish their work on time and stay focused and motivated.

Members should have a platform where they can ask questions and provide suggestions and receive credit for their input. The sharing of responsibilities increases their motivation and commitment to the success of the project.

Reinforce their motivation by giving them feedback on their work and thanking them.

18 | Page

Example: Regular team meetings are held where members can share their progress, discuss challenges, and receive recognition for their contributions, fostering a sense of commitment and motivation

Keep Everyone Up to Date with Progress

In order for the team to stay organised, all the members need to be informed of goals, changes, budgeting and changes to the schedule. Proper communication will strengthen relationships, motivation, and success.

Example: Weekly progress reports are shared with the team, highlighting completed tasks, upcoming deadlines, and any changes to the project scope or timeline.

Approach Any Setbacks in a Smart Way

In order to keep the team organised and motivated, problems need to be dealt with correctly. Having a creative approach towards problem-solving will enable you and your team to put things in perspective. Allow for input from members and restructure tasks to accommodate or resolve the problem.

Example: If a key team member falls ill, the project manager quickly reallocates tasks and adjusts the timeline, ensuring that the project stays on track without major disruptions.

1.2.4 Project team pitfalls

Problems and setbacks can often cause members of a team to lose motivation or become uncooperative. It is important to avoid these setbacks as they can affect the success of the project. Deal with the following problems in an effective manner:

• Distrust of the project

• Standards can't be agreed on

• Members will not follow schedules

• Feedback is met with resistance from members

Distrust of the Project

The first problem you may encounter with your team is that they do not have faith in the project or trust that it will be beneficial. Members may feel this way if they are uncomfortable with the project or lack experience in the work they are doing. Managers should first try to have volunteers for the project and only add additional individuals who will be beneficial afterwards.

19 | Page

Members who feel negative should be able to communicate their reasons either to their team or to the supervisor. The team leader should be able to help them understand why the project is necessary and motivate the individual. They may feel too challenged but leaders need to let them know why they were chosen.

This problem may also arise when members of a team do not trust each other. They may not know each other well and are therefore unable to communicate effectively or feel that they can rely on one another. The first team meeting should enable members to understand why they are all part of the team and get to know each other better in order to build relationships.

Example: A new team member may feel uncertain about the project's benefits. The project manager holds a meeting to explain the project's goals and address any concerns, building trust within the team.

Standards Can't Be Agreed On

Some team members may feel that others are not delivering the same standard of work. Avoid this by exacting standards to which tasks need to be completed and have the same evaluation of work for all members. This will relieve and prevent such tensions from happening.

Example: The project manager sets clear quality standards for all deliverables and ensures that every team member understands and adheres to these standards, promoting consistency in work quality.

Members Will Not Follow Schedule

Members may sometimes refuse to follow schedules because they feel that the time allocated for their tasks is unreasonable. This may be because they feel that the workload is simply too much or that they feel constantly interrupted by changes and meetings. Include meeting times within the project schedule so that members can be prepared for the interruption. The team leader and members should notify each other when and why deadlines or meetings cannot be met so that they do not delay everybody's work.

Example: A team member struggling with time management receives support from the project manager, who helps them create a more manageable schedule and ensures they have the resources needed to meet deadlines.

20 | Page

Feedback is Met with Resistance from Members

Members may sometimes not want to provide or receive feedback because they feel too busy to make time. Schedule specific feedback times between tasks to keep control of project outcomes. Also ask for feedback from individuals outside of the project who might be affected to keep control of possible setbacks that might arise externally. Remind members that feedback sessions are im- portant for the success of the project because possible changes and problems can be discussed before some work needs to be redone.

Example: Regular feedback sessions are scheduled, and team members are encouraged to provide constructive feedback to help improve project outcomes. External stakeholders are also consulted to ensure all perspectives are considered.
