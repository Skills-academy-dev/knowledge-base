Introduction

In project management, contracts are essential tools that define the terms, conditions, and

expectations for all parties involved. Module 1 provides a comprehensive overview of the key

aspects of contracts, ensuring you understand the fundamental elements necessary for successful

project execution.

We begin with procurement planning and solicitation planning, which lay the groundwork for

acquiring the goods and services needed for your project. Understanding the conditions that

legalise a contract is crucial for ensuring enforceable agreements.

The module then explores clauses in contracts, covering standard provisions and the hierarchy of

documents. We categorise contracts into term and completion types, and explain important contract

terms, including risk management.

Additionally, you will learn about the different types of contracts in project management, such as

purchase orders, and the classification of contracts, focusing on the privity of contract.

By mastering these topics, you'll be equipped to draft, analyse, and manage contracts effectively,

laying a strong foundation for your project management skills.

1.1 Procurement Planning

Procurement planning is a key part of the project planning process. It involves making important

decisions about what items and services need to be bought, when they should be purchased, and

from which suppliers. This step is crucial because it helps to ensure that the project has all the

necessary resources at the right time and from reliable sources.

Firstly, procurement planning begins with identifying the needs of the project. This involves

understanding what materials, equipment, and services are required to complete the project

successfully. For example, if you are building a school, you will need to plan for materials like bricks,

cement, and timber, as well as services from electricians and plumbers.

Secondly, it is essential to determine the timing of these purchases. This means creating a schedule

that outlines when each item or service is needed. Proper timing ensures that the project

progresses smoothly without any delays caused by missing materials or services. For instance, you

wouldn’t want to order windows after the walls have already been built; instead, you would schedule

the window delivery to coincide with the appropriate stage of construction.

12 | Page

Thirdly, selecting the right suppliers is a critical part of procurement planning. It is important to

choose suppliers who can provide quality materials and services on time and within the budget. This

often involves researching different suppliers, comparing prices, and checking reviews or references

to ensure reliability.

Furthermore, procurement planning includes setting clear expectations and requirements for

suppliers. This means detailing the quality standards, delivery schedules, and payment terms in

contracts. Clear communication with suppliers helps to prevent misunderstandings and ensures that

both parties are aware of their responsibilities.

Finally, it is important to monitor and manage the procurement process throughout the project. This

involves keeping track of orders, deliveries, and payments, as well as resolving any issues that may

arise. Effective monitoring ensures that the procurement process remains on track and that any

problems are addressed promptly.

In summary, procurement planning is a vital process that involves identifying what is needed,

determining when it is needed, selecting reliable suppliers, and setting clear expectations. By

carefully planning procurement activities, projects can be completed more efficiently, on time, and

within budget.

1.2 Solicitation Planning

Solicitation planning is an important part of the project planning process. It involves creating a

request for information and developing plans to select and contract products and services.

goods and services needed for the project.

Selection

Selection is also part of the executing process. It involves identifying suitable vendors and

contractors who can provide the necessary goods and services for the project.

Contract administration

Contract administration is part of the executing process. It involves managing contracts made with

customers, vendors, partners, or employees. The team responsible for contract administration

negotiates, supports, and manages these contracts to ensure they are effective and cost-efficient.

Closeout

Closeout is part of the closing process. During this phase, all requirements for completed contracts

Contracts contain legally binding agreements between parties involved. Resources such as

personnel, goods, and services are specified in the contract. When the buyer and seller sign the

contract, they agree to certain terms and conditions.

Contracts highlight the duties of each party, allow for changes if needed, and outline consequences

for not meeting obligations. Financial arrangements and other essential details are also included.

Once the contract is signed, the project team is informed about how the contract affects their work.

In some cases, the terms and conditions of the contract guide the project activities to ensure the

buyer's requirements are met.

1.3 Conditions Which Legalise a Contract

For a contract to be legally enforceable, certain criteria must be met. These conditions ensure that

the contract is legally binding and protects the interests of all parties involved. Here are the key

conditions that legalise a contract:

1.

Voluntary Agreement: The agreement to the terms and conditions between the buyer and

the seller must be voluntary. This means that both parties willingly accept the contract without

any coercion or undue pressure. A voluntary agreement ensures that both sides have

consented freely, making the contract fair and balanced. For instance, if a student decides to

buy study materials from a bookstore, both the student and the bookstore must agree to the

terms of sale without any force or threat.

2.  Mutual Benefit: Both parties must receive some form of benefit from the contract. This mutual

benefit, often referred to as "consideration," is essential as it ensures that the contract is fair

and advantageous to everyone involved. For example, in a service contract, one party may

provide a service, such as tutoring, while the other party pays for the service. This exchange

of value is what makes the contract valid.

3.

Legal Right to Sign: All parties involved in signing the contract must have the legal right to

do so. This means that they must be of legal age, sound mind, and authorised to enter into the

agreement. For instance, a teenager might not have the legal right to sign a business contract

without parental consent. Similarly, a company representative must have the authority to sign

contracts on behalf of the company.

14 | Page

4.

Lawful Purpose: The contract must not violate any laws. It should aim to safeguard the

parties involved and fulfil a legitimate purpose. Any contract that involves illegal activities is

not enforceable. For example, a contract for the sale of prohibited substances would be void

because it involves illegal activities. Contracts must be made for legal activities, such as

buying books, renting a property, or hiring a tutor.

By ensuring these conditions are met, a contract becomes a reliable and enforceable agreement.

This protects all parties involved and helps to avoid disputes and misunderstandings. In any project

or business, having clear, fair, and lawful contracts is essential for successful and smooth

operations.

1.4 Clauses in Contracts

For contracts to be legally enforceable, they must contain contractual clauses. These clauses detail

each party's responsibilities, the limits of their risks, and incentives to motivate everyone to fulfil

their duties. Clauses are the specific agreements within contracts that ensure all parties understand

their obligations and expectations.

Contract clauses become crucial once negotiations are complete and both parties have reached an

agreement. During negotiations, project managers must be aware of potential risks and address

them through contractual clauses. This helps protect the project and ensures that all aspects are

covered legally and practically. There are two main types of clauses in project management

contracts: standard clauses and order of precedence clauses.

1.4.1 The Standard Clauses

Standard clauses are common terms and conditions included in contracts to streamline the

negotiation process. These clauses provide a foundation for legal advisors, ensuring that all new or

existing clauses are justified and consistent. Standard clauses act as basic elements from which

other types of clauses are derived.

Examples of how order of precedence clauses are used:







Hierarchy of Documents: In complex contracts, multiple documents may be

referenced. The order of precedence clause will specify which document takes priority if

there is a conflict between them.

Prioritising Quality Over Speed: A contract might state that meeting quality standards

is more important than adhering to strict deadlines. If there is a conflict between finishing on

time and ensuring quality, quality will take precedence.

Supplier Obligations: The clause might prioritise the main contract over any supplier

agreements. If a supplier's terms conflict with the main contract, the main contract's terms

will prevail.

By setting clear priorities, the order of precedence clauses help avoid confusion and ensure that the

most critical terms are followed. This is particularly important in complex projects where multiple

parties and documents are involved.

Importance of clauses in contracts

Clauses in contracts serve multiple vital functions:









Risk Limitation: Clauses limit each party's risks by clearly defining responsibilities and

expectations.

Motivation: Clauses offer incentives, motivating parties to fulfil their obligations.

Clarity: Clauses ensure everyone understands their duties and expectations, reducing

misunderstandings.

Protection: Clauses protect parties by addressing potential risks and providing solutions for

resolving issues.

By carefully drafting and understanding contractual clauses, project managers can ensure their

projects are legally sound and successfully completed.

1.5 The Categories of Contracts

Contracts can be classified as either term contracts or completion contracts. Understanding these

categories helps in determining how long the contract lasts and under what conditions it can be

ended.

1.5.1 Term Contracts

Term contracts bind organisations or individuals to perform specific activities for a set period of time.

With term contracts, the contract must be fulfilled when the agreed time is reached, regardless of

whether the work is satisfactorily completed or not.

Key points about term contracts:







Fixed Duration: Term contracts have a fixed duration, such as six months or one year. The

parties involved must continue to fulfil their obligations until the end of this period.

Completion Requirement: Even if the work is not fully completed, the contract ends when

the term expires. This means that the focus is on the time frame rather than the completion of

the work.

Conversion to Permanent Contract: A term contract can sometimes be converted into a

permanent contract if both parties agree. This usually happens if the work needs to continue

beyond the initial term.

16 | Page





Termination: The parent company has the right to terminate the contract early if there is a

valid reason. For instance, if the work quality is poor or if the project is no longer needed, the

contract can be ended with notice.

Payment: Payments in term contracts are typically settled according to the agreed schedule,

such as monthly or quarterly, regardless of the work progress.

Example: A school might hire a company to provide janitorial services for a year. Even if

the cleaning is not perfect every day, the contract continues until the year ends unless there is a

major issue that justifies early termination.

1.5.2 Completion Contracts

Completion contracts are fulfilled when the service provider or the resource provider completes a

specific activity within the stipulated time.

Key points about completion contracts:











Focus on Completion: The contract ends when the specified work is completed to the

satisfaction of all parties involved. This means the emphasis is on completing the tasks rather

than on a fixed time period.

Defined Tasks: The contract clearly outlines the tasks that need to be completed. This could

include delivering goods, providing a service, or completing a project phase.

Quality and Satisfaction: All parties must be satisfied with the quality of the work before the

contract is considered complete. If the work does not meet the agreed standards, the service

provider may need to make corrections.

Payment: Payments are made once the work is completed and approved. This ensures that

the provider is motivated to complete the work to a high standard.

Flexibility: Completion contracts can be more flexible in terms of time, as the focus is on

achieving the desired results rather than adhering to a strict schedule.

Example: A school might contract a builder to construct a new classroom. The contract

specifies that the classroom must be built according to certain specifications. The builder is paid

once the classroom is completed and meets the agreed standards.

Understanding the differences between term contracts and completion contracts is essential in

project management. Term contracts focus on time, requiring parties to fulfil their obligations until

the contract's end date, regardless of completion. Completion contracts, on the other hand, focus on

completing specific tasks to the satisfaction of all parties involved, often offering more flexibility in

terms of time but demanding higher standards of work quality.

By carefully choosing the appropriate type of contract, project managers can better ensure that their

projects are completed efficiently and to the required standards.
