1.1 What Is Finance?
Finance is more than just money—it is the system we use to plan, manage, and make decisions
about how to use money wisely. In both personal and business life, finance helps us reach our
goals, prepare for the future, and deal with risks. In this unit, you will learn what finance means,
why it is important, and how it differs between individuals and businesses.
1.1.1 Definition of Finance
Finance is the study and management of money and other valuable resources. It includes how
money is earned, saved, borrowed, spent, and invested.
There are three main areas of finance:
Area of Finance Description
Personal Finance Managing your own money, including budgeting, saving, and spending.
Business Finance Managing money in a business to help it grow and stay profitable.
Managing money for government and public services like schools or
Public Finance
hospitals.
Example:
When you make a monthly budget to manage your allowance or part-time earnings, you are
using personal finance. When a business decides whether to buy a new delivery vehicle, it
uses business finance.
Tip:
Finance is about making smart decisions. Even if you have a small amount of money, good
financial planning helps it go further.
1.1.2 The Objectives of Finance
The main aim of finance is to help individuals or businesses make decisions that lead to
financial stability and success.

Objectives in Personal Finance
 Save for future needs (e.g. education, retirement).
 Avoid debt and spend within your means.
 Prepare for emergencies.
 Build wealth over time.
Objectives in Business Finance
 Ensure there is enough money to pay bills and staff.
 Maximise profit.
 Reduce risks.
 Plan for growth and future opportunities.

1.2 The Role of Finance in a Business
Finance is essential for every part of a business. It allows the business to function each day,
make short- and long-term decisions, and prepare for future growth. In this unit, you will learn
how finance supports daily operations, decision-making, and the planning and use of
resources.
1.2.1 Supporting Daily Operations
Finance is used every day in a business. It helps pay for:
 Rent and electricity
 Wages and salaries
 Stock and supplies
 Transport and delivery
 Maintenance and small repairs
Without enough money, a business cannot run smoothly. Good financial management ensures
there is enough cash available to keep the business operating.
Example:
A local fish and chips shop in East London uses finance daily to buy potatoes, oil, and fish, pay
their employees, and cover the cost of electricity. If they run out of cash, they may not be able
to trade the next day.

Good financial decisions help the business grow and reduce waste. Poor decisions can lead to

1.3 Business Structures and Finance
A business structure is the legal form a business takes. It affects how decisions are made, how
taxes are paid, and—most importantly—how finance is managed. In this unit, you will learn about the
most common types of business structures and how each one affects financial responsibility.
1.3.1 Sole Proprietorship
A sole proprietorship is a business owned and run by one person. It is the simplest and most
common type of business.
Features:
 Easy to start and run
 The owner keeps all profits
 The owner is responsible for all debts (unlimited liability)
Financial Implications:
 Finance is usually from the owner's savings or loans
 The owner is personally responsible for paying back any debt
 Business and personal finances are not separate
Example:
Lunga opens a corner shop in Khayelitsha using his savings. He buys stock, pays for rent, and
manages income and expenses alone. If the business cannot pay its debts, Lunga must cover them
from his own money.

Tip:
Sole proprietorships are good for small, low-risk businesses, especially when just starting out.
1.3.2 Partnerships
A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who agree to share profits, losses, and
responsibilities.
Features:
 Shared ownership and decision-making
 Partners contribute money, skills, or time
 A written partnership agreement is recommended
Financial Implications:
 Finance can come from all partners
 Each partner is responsible for debts (unless limited liability is agreed)
 Disagreements about money can lead to conflict
Example:
Thabo and Karabo start a tutoring business in Polokwane. Thabo provides funding and Karabo
manages day-to-day operations. They agree to share profits 60/40 based on their contributions.

1.3.3 Companies and Corporations
A company is a separate legal entity from its owners (called shareholders). This means the
business can own property, enter into contracts, and be held responsible for its actions.
Features:
 Owned by shareholders, run by directors
 More complex and costly to set up
 Must be registered with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC)
Financial Implications:
 Shareholders are only liable for the money they invested (limited liability)
 Easier to raise finance through selling shares or getting loans
 Must follow strict financial rules and reporting

1.4 Why Finance Matters
Finance is one of the most important parts of running a successful business. It helps business

1.4.1 Impact of Finance on Business Success
Without finance, a business cannot survive. It is used to buy stock, pay salaries, manage
operations, and invest in new ideas. Good financial management helps a business succeed, while
poor financial planning can lead to failure, even if the business has good products or services.
How Finance Affects Success:
 Keeps cash flowing so bills and workers can be paid on time.
 Helps avoid overspending and debt.
 Ensures stock is always available.
 Provides money to take advantage of new opportunities.

1.5 Corporate Governance and Ethics
As a business grows, it must be managed responsibly and fairly. This is where corporate
governance and ethics come in. Good governance ensures that a company is run in a way that
protects all its stakeholders—owners, employees, customers, and the public. Ethics helps guide
decisions that are not only legal but also honest and fair.
1.5.1 What Is Corporate Governance?
Corporate governance refers to the rules, systems, and processes that control and direct a
business. It ensures that managers act responsibly, make fair decisions, and are held accountable.
24 | Page

Key Principles of Good Governance:
 Transparency – Be open and honest in operations and reporting.
 Accountability – Take responsibility for decisions and actions.
 Fairness – Treat all stakeholders fairly.
 Responsibility – Follow laws and do what is right for the business and community.
Did You Know?
South Africa follows the King IV Report on corporate governance, which promotes ethical
leadership, sustainability, and accountability in both public and private organisations.
The King IV Report is a guide that helps South African businesses act honestly, treat people
fairly, and run responsibly for the benefit of the company and the community.
1.5.2 The Role of Directors and Shareholders
In a company, there are two main groups of people responsible for governance:
Role Description
Own shares in the company and invest money. They expect returns on their
Shareholders
investment.
Manage the company and make important decisions. They are elected by the
Directors
shareholders.
What Do Directors Do?
 Approve budgets and financial reports
 Set the company’s goals and strategies
 Make decisions on loans, investments, and major contracts
 Ensure the company follows the law
Example:
Naledi, a director of a construction company in Gqeberha, must approve a proposal to take out a
loan for new equipment. She ensures the decision is based on solid financial information and that
the company can afford to repay it.
25 | Page

Tip:
Shareholders provide the capital, but directors manage it. Both must act in the best interest of the
company.
1.5.3 Financial Transparency and Ethical Conduct
Being financially transparent means giving honest and clear financial information. Ethical conduct
means doing the right thing—even when no one is watching.
Table: Examples of Ethical and Unethical Behaviour
Ethical Behaviour Unethical Behaviour
Reporting accurate profits Lying about income to look more successful
Paying suppliers on time Delaying payments on purpose
Charging fair prices Overcharging or misleading customers
Following safety and environmental laws Ignoring rules to save money
Example:
Sipho owns a small panel-beating shop in Tembisa. Instead of under-reporting his income to SARS,
he submits accurate tax returns and keeps proper records. This builds trust with suppliers and
allows him to apply for business funding.
Did You Know?
Lying on financial statements is called fraud. It is a criminal offence and can lead to fines,
imprisonment, or being banned from running a company.
1.5.4 Compliance with Financial Regulations
All businesses must follow the financial laws and regulations of South Africa. These rules are in
place to protect investors, customers, and the public.
26 | Page

Examples of Financial Regulations in SA:
 SARS (South African Revenue Service) – Collects taxes from individuals and businesses.
 CIPC (Companies and Intellectual Property Commission) – Manages company
registrations and annual reports.
 FIC (Financial Intelligence Centre) – Monitors financial crimes like money laundering.
Example:
Bongiwe owns a catering company in Kimberley. She registers with the CIPC, keeps financial
records, and files tax returns annually. This helps her qualify for tenders and large contracts.
Tip:
Following financial laws protects your business and builds credibility with banks, investors, and
clients.
Key Takeaways
 Corporate governance ensures businesses are run fairly, legally, and responsibly.
 Directors manage the company while shareholders invest in it and expect good returns.
 Ethics and transparency build trust and long-term success.
 Following financial rules is a legal requirement and protects everyone involved.

Module 1: Case Study 1: Noma’s Home Baking Business
Noma is a 24-year-old entrepreneur living in Butterworth, Eastern Cape. She recently started a
home baking business, "Noma’s Cakes," using her savings of R10,000. She sells custom birthday
cakes, cupcakes, and biscuits. She runs the business on her own and works from her kitchen.
Noma uses her savings to buy baking ingredients, a new oven, packaging materials, and to pay for
electricity and transport. She wants to grow her business and is thinking of supplying local schools

Answers:
1. Business finance helps a small business manage its money to pay for daily operations, plan
for growth, and avoid financial problems.
2. Personal finance is about managing your own income and expenses, while business finance
deals with managing a company’s money.
Business finance includes planning for profit, paying suppliers and staff, and funding growth.
3. Financial planning helps a business owner make informed decisions, avoid overspending,
manage risks, and prepare for future needs.
