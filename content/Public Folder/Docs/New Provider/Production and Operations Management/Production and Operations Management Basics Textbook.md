UNIT 1: Nature and Scope of Operations Management
Definition and Scope
Operations Management (OM) is the field of study that focuses on managing the activities involved in
producing goods and delivering services. It includes the planning, organizing, and controlling of
resources such as people, equipment, technology, and materials to ensure that products and services
are created efficiently and meet customer needs.
Operations management is not limited to factories. It also applies to service industries such as
healthcare, retail, education, banking, and transport. For example, a hospital must manage its doctors,
beds, and medical equipment to provide care, just as a factory manages machines and workers to
build cars.
The scope of operations management includes:
 Product and service design
 Process design and improvement
 Quality management
 Capacity planning
 Inventory and supply chain management
 Scheduling and work organization
 Resource management and productivity
Operations management ensures that everything works smoothly so that businesses can deliver the
right products and services, in the right quantity, at the right time, and the right cost.

Historical Evolution of OM
Operations Management has developed over hundreds of years, influenced by industrial progress,
changing customer needs, and technology. Here are some important stages in its history:

9 | Page

1. Craft Production (Before the 1800s)
Goods were made by hand by skilled craftsmen. Products were custom-made, but production was
slow and costly.
2. Industrial Revolution (Late 1700s–1800s)
The invention of machines and the growth of factories allowed for mass production. This led to faster
output, cheaper products, and the growth of industries.
3. Scientific Management (Early 1900s)
Frederick Taylor introduced methods like time-and-motion studies to increase worker efficiency. Tasks
were standardised, and managers began measuring performance.
4. Human Relations Era (1930s–1950s)
Focus shifted from just machines and processes to the people doing the work. Motivation, teamwork,
and leadership were seen as important to productivity.
5. Operations Research (1940s–1960s)
During World War II, mathematical models were used to solve military problems. After the war, these
methods were applied to business operations.
6. Lean Manufacturing and JIT (1970s–1980s)
Japanese companies introduced systems to reduce waste and improve quality. Toyota became known
for its Just-in-Time (JIT) production system.
7. Globalisation and Technology (1990s–2000s)
Companies began outsourcing, using computers, and managing global supply chains. Operations
became faster, more complex, and more connected.
8. Digital and Sustainable Operations (2000s–present)
Operations today involve AI, automation, and data. There is also growing concern for the environment,
social responsibility, and ethical sourcing.
Understanding this evolution helps students see how operations adapt to meet the needs of each
generation and market.

Core Functions in OM
Operations managers are responsible for turning business plans into real products and services. Their
daily tasks involve coordinating many activities. The key functions are:
1. Process Design: Deciding how work is done—from start to finish—to make a product or service.
2. Capacity Planning: Determining how much the operation can produce and how to meet future
demand.
3. Inventory Management: Making sure the right amount of raw materials and finished goods is
available.
4. Scheduling: Planning when and where tasks, activities, and services take place.
5. Quality Management: Ensuring that goods and services meet customer expectations and
standards.
6. Supply Chain Management: Managing the flow of materials, information, and money between
suppliers, the business, and customers.
7. Resource Management: Using labour, equipment, and materials wisely to maximise efficiency.
8. Maintenance and Reliability: Keeping machines and systems in good working order to avoid
delays.
These functions work together to deliver value, minimize costs, and meet customer demand.

UNIT 2: The Transformation Process Model
1. Inputs, Transformation, Outputs
In every organisation, the operations function is responsible for transforming inputs into outputs. This
process happens in every industry whether it's a bakery, a school, a tech company, or a hospital. The
transformation process is a fundamental concept in Operations Management and explains how value is
created through a structured flow of resources. By understanding this model, students can begin to
analyse how organisations work, what resources they need, how they use them, and how to improve
the way products and services are delivered. This section breaks the transformation process into three
main elements: inputs, transformation, and outputs.
Inputs
These are all the resources that go into producing a product or delivering a service. Inputs can
include:
 Materials – raw ingredients, components, parts
 Labour/People – employees, service staff, technicians
 Equipment and Technology – tools, machinery, software
 Information – customer requirements, design plans, schedules
 Capital – financial investment, facilities, infrastructure
Transformation
This refers to the processes that convert inputs into outputs. The transformation can involve:
 Physical changes (e.g. baking, assembling)

 Service actions (e.g., educating, consulting)
 Information handling (e.g., analysing, reporting)
 Customer interaction (e.g., diagnosis, entertainment)
Outputs
Outputs are the final goods or services delivered to customers. These may include:
 Tangible goods – furniture, phones, packaged food
 Intangible services – transport, teaching, repairs
A successful operation adds value by producing outputs that are more useful or desirable than the
inputs.

2. Types of Transformations
Different industries use different types of transformations depending on what they are producing and
how they create value. A restaurant changes raw food into meals. A taxi company changes your
location. A therapist may transform your psychological well-being. These examples show that
transformation is not just about making physical products—it includes changing information, people,
and ownership. Understanding the various types of transformation helps us design better processes
and match the transformation type to the needs of customers more effectively.
Common types of transformation include:
 Physical transformation – Changing the shape or form of materials
 Locational transformation – Moving items or people from one place to another

5. The Process Hierarchy
In large or complex organizations, operations are broken down into smaller pieces to make them easier
to manage. This is called the process hierarchy. It allows managers to view the organization as a
system made up of smaller, related systems. Each level of the hierarchy focuses on different tasks,
and together, they contribute to the final product or service. Understanding the process hierarchy helps
managers identify weak areas, remove bottlenecks, and improve overall performance.
Levels of the process hierarchy include:
 The Operation (Top Level) – The entire system, such as a hospital or airline
 Core Processes (Middle Level) – Major steps that directly deliver customer value
 Sub-processes (Lower Level) – Support activities that ensure the core processes run
smoothly

UNIT 3: Functions and Decision Areas
1. Strategic vs. Tactical vs. Operational Decisions
Operations managers must make decisions at various levels of an organization, depending on the
timeframe, scope, and impact of the decision. These are generally classified into three categories:
strategic, tactical, and operational decisions. Strategic decisions focus on long-term goals and have a
broad impact on the organization. Tactical decisions support the strategic plan and usually involve
medium-term planning. Operational decisions are made daily or weekly and focus on routine, short-
term tasks. Understanding the differences between these decision levels helps managers allocate
time, resources, and attention effectively, ensuring that both day-to-day activities and long-term goals
are aligned and well managed.
 Strategic Decisions
o Long-term in nature (3–5 years or more)
o Made by senior management
o Examples: entering a new market, building a new factory, investing in new technology
 Tactical Decisions
o Medium-term (1–3 years)
o Made by middle managers
o Examples: hiring staff, selecting suppliers, setting schedules

 Operational Decisions
o Short-term (daily to monthly)
o Made by frontline supervisors or staff
o Examples: allocating tasks, managing customer orders, adjusting delivery routes

2. Structural and Infrastructural Decisions
In Operations Management, decisions can also be classified as structural or infrastructural. Structural
decisions involve high-cost, long-term investments such as building new facilities or acquiring major
equipment. These decisions shape the physical and technical capabilities of the operation.
Infrastructural decisions, by contrast, deal with policies, systems, and people that support operations,
such as employee training or quality control processes. Both types are essential for effective
performance, but they differ in cost, complexity, and impact. Understanding these categories allows
operations managers to distinguish between permanent decisions and those that can be adjusted more
easily to improve efficiency.
2.1 Structural Decisions
Structural decisions are large-scale and involve significant investment. They are long-term in nature
and often difficult to change once implemented.

Less capital-intensive

Can be changed more easily
They are more adaptable and can be revised frequently in response to operational needs.

4. Long-Term vs. Short-Term Decision Impacts
Every decision in Operations Management has consequences—some are immediate, while others
unfold over time. Understanding the difference between long-term and short-term impacts helps
managers make more informed choices. Long-term decisions often involve structural changes and
large investments that shape the organization’s future. They are harder to reverse and usually affect
strategy, capacity, and infrastructure. Short-term decisions are more tactical or operational and deal
with day-to-day activities. They can be adjusted quickly and tend to affect scheduling, inventory, and
employee allocation. The challenge is balancing both timeframes to achieve sustainable success.
Differences in impact:

o Affects competitiveness and growth.
o High cost and risk

5. Decision-Making Tools in Operations Management
Operations managers rely on decision-making tools to evaluate options, reduce uncertainty, and
choose the best course of action. These tools range from simple checklists to complex mathematical
models. They help managers make better decisions about resource allocation, forecasting demand,
scheduling, quality control, and risk management. Choosing the right tool depends on the nature of the
problem, the data available, and the time constraints. For distance learners, becoming familiar with
these tools will build confidence in analyzing operational situations and proposing solutions.
Common tools include:
 Break-even analysis – Determines when revenue covers costs
 Decision trees – Maps possible outcomes and their probabilities
 Linear programming – Solves optimization problems with multiple constraints.
 Gantt charts – Visualize schedules and project timelines
 Forecasting models – Predicts future demand using past data.

business can deliver faster or produce at lower costs than competitors, it becomes more attractive to
customers. This section explores how OM supports business success and how efficient operations

OM contributes to competitive advantage by:

 Speeding up delivery – with better scheduling and process optimization
 Increasing flexibility – by offering customization or faster changeovers
 Enhancing innovation – by enabling faster introduction of new products/services

2. Aligning OM with Business Strategy
For operations to support the organization effectively, its goals must be aligned with the overall
business strategy. Business strategy refers to how a company plans to achieve its mission and
compete in the market. The operations strategy is the part of this plan that focuses on how resources
will be used to meet business objectives. If OM works in a different direction from marketing or finance,
the business suffers from confusion and inefficiency. Aligning OM with the broader strategy ensures
that all departments are working toward the same goals, whether that means focusing on cost
leadership, product innovation, or customer service.
Key alignment principles:
 OM must support the value proposition the company offers to customers
 OM decisions should reflect strategic goals (e.g., focus on speed, not variety)
 Coordination with marketing, finance, and HR is essential.
 Misalignment leads to wasted resources and poor customer service.
