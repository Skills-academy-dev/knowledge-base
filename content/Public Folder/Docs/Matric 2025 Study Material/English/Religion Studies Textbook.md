Module 1

Diversity of Religions and Religious Studies

Introduction

Religion has been a powerful and enduring feature of human existence since the beginning of

recorded history. Every culture, past and present, has developed ways of expressing its deepest

questions and beliefs about life, death, morality, and the sacred. Religion shapes how people

understand themselves, their communities, and their relationship to the wider universe.

Studying religion is not the same as practising it. Religious Studies is an academic discipline

that investigates religion objectively, critically, and respectfully. It seeks to understand religion as

a human phenomenon rather than to promote or dismiss particular beliefs. By doing so, it allows

students to engage with the world’s diversity of faiths and worldviews in a way that promotes

tolerance, understanding, and critical reflection.

South Africa offers a particularly rich context for studying religion. It is home to a wide range of

religious traditions, including Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, African

Traditional Religion (ATR), and the Bahá’í Faith, as well as non-religious worldviews such as

secular humanism. The Constitution protects the freedom of religion and belief, ensuring that

people of all traditions can coexist.

At the same time, religion has played a complex role in the country’s history. During apartheid,

some churches supported the system of racial segregation, while others resisted it and fought

for justice. After apartheid, religious leaders played a central role in promoting reconciliation and

building a vision of the “Rainbow Nation.” This shows how religion can both divide and unite,

both oppress and liberate.

The study of these themes will help you better understand the role of religion in South Africa

and the wider world — and encourage you to think critically about your own position in a diverse

society.

Unit 1: Key Concepts in Religion

1.1 Defining Religion

● Religion is not easy to define because it is complex and expressed differently in cultures.

Scholars, theologians, and philosophers have all given various definitions.

● Substantive definition – focuses on belief in gods or the divine. For example, Christianity

believes in God, while Hinduism has many gods.

● Functional definition – looks at what religion does in society: it provides meaning, social

order, and moral guidance.

● Experiential definition – highlights personal encounters with the sacred, such as mystical

visions or prayer experiences.

● Symbolic definition – emphasises religious symbols (cross, crescent, star, yin-yang) as

carriers of meaning.

● Sociological perspective – religion is a social institution that unites people through rituals

and beliefs (Émile Durkheim).

● Psychological perspective – religion addresses human fears, desires, and need for

meaning (Sigmund Freud saw it as illusion; Carl Jung saw it as archetype).

● Anthropological view – religion is a universal feature of all cultures, with rituals marking

birth, marriage, death.

9

● Philosophical approach – religion answers “big questions” about existence, morality, and

the afterlife.

● Legal and constitutional view – religion is recognised as a right to belief and practice

(e.g., South Africa’s Constitution).

● Personal perspective – for believers, religion is a lived relationship with God/the divine,

not just a definition.

Fun Fact

The word religion comes from the Latin religare, which means “to bind” — suggesting that

religion “binds” people together through shared beliefs and practices.

1.2 Core Components of Religion

Every religion has certain key features that make it recognisable.

● Beliefs – ideas about God, gods, creation, afterlife, morality.

Example: Christians believe in the Trinity; Hindus believe in karma and reincarnation.

● Rituals – symbolic actions that express beliefs.

Example: Muslim Salah prayers, Christian baptism, Hindu puja.

● Sacred texts (normative sources) – writings that guide life and belief.

Example: Bible, Qur’an, Torah, Vedas, Tripitaka.

● Moral codes – ethical rules to live by.

Example: Ten Commandments, Sharia law, Dharma.

● Community – religion always involves groups of believers.

Example: church, mosque, synagogue, sangha, clan rituals.

● Symbols – visual expressions of belief (cross, crescent, lotus, Star of David).

● Sacred spaces – temples, churches, mosques, shrines, mountains.

● Life-cycle rituals – ceremonies at birth, initiation, marriage, death.

● Festivals – religious holidays celebrated by communities (Ramadan, Easter, Diwali,

Passover).

● Interpretation (hermeneutics) – the process of understanding and applying sacred texts

to daily life.

10

Did You Know?

Most religions include five common elements: belief systems, sacred texts, rituals, ethics,

and a community of believers — but the expression of each varies widely between traditions.

Hermeneutics: Approaches to Interpreting Sacred Texts

● Hermeneutics is the study of interpretation — the way believers understand and apply

their sacred texts and traditions. Different approaches shape how religion responds to

society:

● Literal Approach: Interprets texts word-for-word as direct commands or truths.

Example: Some Christians read the Genesis creation account as literal history.

● Historical-Critical Approach: Reads texts in their original cultural and historical setting.

Example: Many scholars analyze the Quran or Bible by asking what a verse meant to its

first audience.

● Liberation Hermeneutics: Interprets texts through the lens of justice and freedom for

the oppressed. Example: In South Africa, liberation theology used the Bible to challenge

apartheid.

● Feminist Hermeneutics: Highlights how women’s voices and experiences should

reshape the reading of texts. Example: Feminist scholars re-read biblical and Quranic

stories to emphasize equality.

● Contextual Hermeneutics: Applies texts to current cultural realities. Example: ATR

stories about community harmony are read today as affirming Ubuntu values.

● Hermeneutics reminds us that sacred texts are not just ancient writings — they are living

sources of guidance that must be understood and applied responsibly in every era.

Tip

When analysing a religion, ask yourself:

“How does this belief or practice help followers find meaning or connection?”

This question helps you identify the religion’s core function in people’s lives.

1.3 Key Concepts in Religious Studies

Religious Identity

● How individuals identify themselves religiously (Christian, Muslim, atheist, etc.).

● Shapes worldview, ethics, and life choices.

● Provides a sense of belonging to a community.

● Passed down through family, but also chosen personally.

● Can influence political, cultural, and social identity.

● Religious identity may be mixed (dual belonging, interfaith families).

● In South Africa, identity often shaped by both religion and culture.

● Identity can cause unity but also division if misused.

● Interfaith marriages challenge and enrich religious identity.

● Identity changes across one’s life (conversion, secularisation, deeper faith).

Comparability

● Comparing religions to find similarities and differences.

● Builds tolerance and reduces prejudice.

● Shows shared ethical values (e.g., Golden Rule).

● Reveals differences in doctrines (e.g., reincarnation vs resurrection).

● Encourages academic objectivity rather than judgment.

● Helps understand how religions adapt in history and culture.

● Used in exams to evaluate similarities and uniqueness.

● Prevents stereotypes by showing complexity.

● Comparative religion is now a full academic discipline.

● Encourages dialogue in multi-faith societies like South Africa.

Did You Know?

The academic study of religion is not about proving or disproving a faith. It focuses on

understanding and interpreting beliefs, symbols, and rituals as part of human culture.

1.4 Religion and Worldviews

Religion provides a framework (worldview) for understanding reality.

Monotheism

Figure 3: Monotheism

(Source: Dreamstime, Accessed: 2025)

● Belief in one God.

● Found in Judaism, Christianity, Islam.

● God seen as creator and sustainer.

● Strong moral codes based on divine law.

● Ultimate accountability to one God.

● Promotes unity of belief.

● In South Africa, Christianity and Islam are main monotheistic religions.

14

Polytheism

Figure 4:Polytheism

(Source: Britannica, Accessed: 2025)

● Belief in many gods with different roles.

● Found in Hinduism, Ancient Greek religion.

● Deities represent natural forces (rain, fertility, war).

● Allows diverse worship styles.

● Often tied to festivals and seasonal cycles.

● Polytheism provides choice of devotion.

● Promotes diversity within one religion.

15

Pantheism

Figure 5: Pantheism

(Source: Thequran.love, 2025)

● God is identical with the universe.

● Found in Taoism, some Hindu philosophies.

● Stresses unity of all existence.

● Encourages harmony with nature.

● God seen not as separate but as immanent.

● Promotes ecological awareness.

● Influences Eastern philosophy and spirituality.

Atheism

● Denial of existence of God or gods.

● Bases morality on human reason.

● Strong in secular humanism.

● Growing movement in modern societies.

● Emphasises science and rational explanation.

● Some atheists actively oppose religion.

● In South Africa, protected under freedom of belief.

16

Did You Know?

A worldview is the lens through which people interpret reality. Even secular worldviews, like

humanism or atheism, serve a similar role to religion by offering answers to life’s big

questions.

Agnosticism

● Belief that the existence of God is unknown/unknowable.

● Neutral position: “we cannot know.”

● Often sceptical of religious claims.

● Still open to the possibility of divine reality.

● Provides humility in matters of belief.

● Many scientists and philosophers lean agnostic.

● Distinct from atheism: not denial but uncertainty.

Secular Humanism

Figure 6: Agnostic vs. Atheist

(Source: Sinai and Synapses. Mitelman, G. 2013)

17

● Non-religious worldview based on human dignity.

● Morality built on reason and human rights.

● Values democracy, equality, and freedom.

● Opposes discrimination based on religion.

● Popular in modern democratic societies.

● In South Africa, Constitution reflects humanist values.

● Allows peaceful coexistence of multiple faiths and non-believers.

Unit 2: Internal Differentiations within Religions

2.1 Christianity

Figure 7: Christianity

(Source: Christian Webhost, 2019)

Origin of Differentiations

● Great Schism (1054) split Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy over papal

authority and theology.

● Protestant Reformation (1517) under Martin Luther challenged indulgences and papal

power.

● English Reformation (1534) created Anglicanism, blending Catholic tradition and

Protestant theology.

● Catholic Counter-Reformation (Council of Trent, 1545–1563) reaffirmed sacraments and

papal authority.

● Pentecostalism arose in early 20th century (Azusa Street Revival, 1906), emphasising

spiritual gifts.

● Charismatic movement spread Pentecostal practices into mainline churches in mid-20th

century.

● AICs (African Independent Churches) grew in response to colonialism, integrating

African customs.

● Liberation movements in Latin America gave rise to Liberation Theology in the 20th

century.

● Modern evangelical movements focus on missions, media, and revivalism.

21

● Globalisation led to non-denominational megachurches and international Christian

networks.

Faith and Practices

Figure 8: Beliefs and Practices

(Source: Weebly, World Religions, Accessed: 2025)

● Catholics practise seven sacraments as means of grace.

● Orthodox Christians focus on liturgy, fasting, and icons.

● Protestants emphasize Bible study, sermons, and personal prayer.

● Evangelicals stress conversion and missionary work.

● Pentecostals/Charismatics emphasise speaking in tongues, prophecy, and healing.

● Orthodox and Catholic traditions venerate saints, while most Protestants reject this

practice.

● AICs blend Christian scripture with African healing and prophecy.

● Worship styles differ from highly formal liturgies to informal Spirit-led services.

● Some traditions require fasting (e.g., Lent, Orthodox fasts).

● Music and dance play significant roles in Charismatic and African churches.

22

Leadership and Authority

● Catholicism is hierarchical, led by the Pope.

● Orthodox Churches are governed by patriarchs and synods.

● Protestantism decentralises leadership; governance varies by denomination.

● Anglicanism has bishops, with symbolic unity under Archbishop of Canterbury.

● Pentecostal churches are often led by pastors with charismatic authority.

● AICs are usually led by prophets or bishops who combine spiritual and community

authority.

● Monastic orders (Jesuits, Franciscans, Orthodox monks) maintain unique leadership

roles.

● Protestant congregations may have elders or lay leaders.

● Many Protestant and Anglican churches ordain women clergy.

● Global ecumenical councils seek cooperation between leaders of different

denominations.

Philosophies/Teachings

Figure 9: Christianity: Beliefs and Teachings

(Source: Abraham Darby Academy, Accessed: 2025)

● Catholicism teaches salvation by faith and works.

● Protestants emphasise faith alone (sola fide) and scripture alone (sola scriptura).

● Orthodox Christianity stresses mystical theology and theosis.

● Pentecostalism highlights baptism in the Holy Spirit and miracles.

● Evangelicals prioritise spreading the Gospel worldwide.

● Liberation Theology interprets Christian teaching through the lens of social justice.

● Prosperity Gospel (in some circles) links faith with financial blessing.

● Ecumenical theology seeks unity across Christian branches.

● African Christianity integrates Ubuntu values with biblical teaching.

● Doctrinal debates continue on sacraments, gender, sexuality, and authority.

Examples (Global & South Africa)

● Roman Catholic Church: cathedrals in Durban, Johannesburg, Cape Town.

● Eastern Orthodox congregations exist in Johannesburg and Durban.

● Dutch Reformed Church (NG Kerk) historically supported apartheid.

● Methodist and Anglican Churches led resistance against apartheid.

● Zion Christian Church (ZCC) is the largest AIC in South Africa.

● Shembe Church blends Zulu culture with Christian faith.

● Pentecostal megachurches like Rhema Bible Church in Johannesburg.

● Charismatic groups like Hillsong in Cape Town.

● Youth missions and evangelical groups active in schools and universities.

● South African Council of Churches (SACC) unites denominations on social issues.

African-Inspired Christian Churches (AICCs)

These are churches established independently in Africa by Africans. They often combine

Christian teachings with African cultural and spiritual traditions, reflecting both continuity and

innovation in religious practice.

24

Zionist Churches

● Beliefs and Practices: Emphasize faith healing, prophecy, and charismatic worship.

● Significance: Represent the largest group of AICCs in Southern Africa.

Zion Christian Church (ZCC)

● Origin: Founded in 1910 by Engenas Lekganyane in South Africa.

● Beliefs and Practices: Combines Christian teachings with African traditions, including

symbolic rituals and sacred spaces.

● Significance: One of the largest religious movements in Southern Africa.

Shembe Church (Nazareth Baptist Church)

● Origin: Founded in 1910 by Isaiah Shembe in KwaZulu-Natal.

● Beliefs and Practices: Integrates Old Testament teachings with Zulu traditions, including

sacred dance and festivals.

● Significance: Remains a strong expression of African Christianity with a large following

today.

Did You Know?

The major branches of Christianity — Catholicism, Orthodoxy, and Protestantism — all

trace their roots to early debates about authority and interpretation of Scripture, especially

during the Great Schism (1054 CE) and the Reformation (16th century).

2\. 2 Islam

Figure 10: What is Islam?

(Source: Twinkl, Accessed: 2025)

Origin of Differentiations

● Split began after Prophet Muhammad’s death in 632 CE over succession.

● Sunni Muslims believed leadership should be chosen by community consensus.

● Shia Muslims believed leadership should remain in Prophet Muhammad’s family through

Ali.

● Battle of Karbala (680 CE) deepened Sunni–Shia division.

● Sufism developed as a mystical dimension within Islam.

● Islamic law (Sharia) produced four major Sunni schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi’i, Hanbali.

● Shia Islam developed its own legal and theological schools.

● Colonialism and modernity influenced reform movements (e.g., Wahhabism, Salafism).

● Pan-Islamic revival movements sought unity against Western domination.

● Migration and diaspora communities further diversified Islamic practice globally.

26

Faith and Practices

Figure 11: Five Pillars of Islam

(Source: BBC, Bitesize, Accessed: 2025)

● All Muslims follow the Five Pillars: Shahada, Salah, Zakat, Sawm, Hajj.

● Sunnis emphasise congregational prayer on Fridays.

● Shia Muslims commemorate Ashura, mourning Imam Husayn.

● Sufis practise dhikr (remembrance of God) through chanting, music, and meditation.

● Wahhabism rejects innovations (bid‘ah) and promotes strict adherence to Quran and

Sunnah.

● Different jurisprudential schools interpret dietary, marriage, and business laws differently.

● Some Muslims incorporate local cultural practices (e.g., African Islamic healing).

● Sunni mosques often use minbar sermons for guidance; Shia mosques emphasise

imams’ teachings.

● Pilgrimage (Hajj and Umrah) may include different devotional practices across sects.

● Women’s dress codes vary across communities based on interpretation of modesty.

Did You Know?

The main division between Sunni and Shia Islam originated from disagreement over who

should lead the Muslim community after Prophet Muhammad’s death — a political and

spiritual debate that continues to shape Islamic identity today.

27

Leadership and Authority

● Sunnis: leadership decentralised, authority rests with scholars (ulama).

● Shia: authority rests with Imams and, in modern times, Ayatollahs.

● Sufi orders are led by spiritual guides (shaykhs).

● Wahhabi/Salafi movements emphasise authority of conservative scholars.

● Political leadership often overlaps with religious authority (Iran, Saudi Arabia).

● Sunni caliphate historically united leadership but no longer exists.

● In South Africa, imams lead mosques and issue guidance.

● Shia clerics in diaspora provide authority to local communities.

● Sufi leaders maintain spiritual lodges and teaching centres.

● Muslim community organisations (like Jamiatul Ulama SA) coordinate leadership

nationally.

Philosophies/Teachings

● Unity of God (Tawhid) is central across all sects.

● Sunni Islam relies on consensus and analogy for law.

● Shia theology stresses divine justice (adl) and spiritual role of Imams.

● Sufism teaches inner purification and closeness to God.

● Wahhabism/Salafism emphasises strict monotheism and scriptural literalism.

● Islamic schools and madrassas in SA provide education.

● Muslim political organisations historically opposed apartheid.

● Annual Hajj delegations from SA unify Sunni and Shia pilgrims.

● Halal certification bodies reflect religious authority in daily life.

2.3 Hinduism

Origin of Differentiations

Figure 13: Hinduism: Symbols, Beliefs & Origins

(Source: History, Hinduism, 2017)

● Hinduism emerged from ancient Vedic traditions, not a single founder.

● Philosophical schools like Vedanta, Yoga, and Samkhya shaped interpretations.

● Devotion (bhakti) to different deities created Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism,

Smartism.

● Regional and linguistic diversity in India influenced distinct practices.

● Reform movements in 19th century (Arya Samaj, Brahmo Samaj) challenged caste and
