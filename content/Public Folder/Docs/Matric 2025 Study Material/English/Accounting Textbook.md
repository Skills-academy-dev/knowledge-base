Introduction
Accounting is more than just numbers—it’s a powerful tool for managing information and making
smart financial choices. Every business, big or small, depends on accounting to track its
income, expenses, profits, and losses. Even individuals use accounting skills to budget their
income and plan for the future.
Here are just a few reasons why Accounting is a valuable subject to study:
● It builds life skills: You will learn to budget, save, and understand financial documents.
● It opens up career opportunities: Careers in finance, auditing, entrepreneurship, and
management all start with good accounting knowledge.
● It develops logical thinking: Accounting teaches you how to analyse, solve problems,
and make decisions based on evidence.
● It supports other subjects: Skills from accounting can be used in Business Studies,
Economics, and Mathematical Literacy.
Studying accounting can help you succeed in many different fields—even if you don’t become
an accountant!

This textbook is structured to help you study independently at your own pace. Each module
focuses on a key topic in accounting and is divided into units that explore smaller sections of
that topic. Here’s how to use this textbook effectively:
● Start with the Learning Objectives: At the beginning of each module and unit, you’ll
find a list of what you should know or be able to do by the end. Use these objectives as
a checklist for your learning.
● Read the Introduction Carefully: Each module begins with an introduction to explain
the topic and why it matters. This gives you a big-picture view before you dive into the
details.

● Test Yourself: Use the exercises at the end of each module to prepare for exams or

Make sure to keep a notebook, calculator, and your answer book nearby as you work through
the content. With practice and dedication, you will become confident in your accounting skills
and ready to apply them in real-life situations.

Introduction
In today’s world, businesses must follow clear rules and behave responsibly to succeed. This
module introduces you to the structure and legal framework of companies, as well as the roles
and duties of the people who run them. You will also learn why ethical conduct and good
governance are essential in any business.
Understanding how companies are formed and how they operate will help you make sense of
real business situations. You will explore important documents like the Memorandum of
Incorporation, and learn how companies raise money through shares. This module also covers
the responsibilities of directors and shareholders, and the principles of limited liability.
Finally, you will learn how ethics, laws, and internal controls work together to prevent fraud and
support honest business practices. By the end of this module, you will have a strong foundation
for understanding how companies are structured and managed in South Africa.
Unit 1: Company Structures and Legal Framework
Every business that wants to operate legally and responsibly in South Africa must follow a
certain structure and comply with laws. In this unit, we’ll explore the key legal documents,
company types, and organisations that play a role in company formation and operation. You’ll
learn how public and private companies differ, what the Companies Act requires, how to register
a company, and how share capital is managed. We’ll also introduce you to the Johannesburg
Securities Exchange (JSE), where public companies raise money by selling shares.
1.1 Public vs Private companies
There are different types of companies in South Africa, and each type must follow specific rules.
Understanding the difference between public and private companies helps us know how they
operate, raise money, and report financial results.
Definitions
Public Company: A company that sells its shares to the public and is listed on the JSE.
Private Company: A company that does not sell shares to the public and is usually owned by
a few individuals.

How Companies Raise Capital
●  Public companies raise funds by selling shares on the JSE.
●  Private companies raise funds through private investment, loans, or reinvested profits.
Real-Life Example
Shoprite Holdings Ltd is a public company listed on the JSE. Thando Tech (Pty) Ltd is a
private software company owned by three partners.
Did You Know?
In South Africa, public companies must file audited financial statements with CIPC
every year. Private companies only do this if they meet specific criteria.

1.2 The Companies Act
The Companies Act is a law that provides rules for forming and managing companies. It
ensures that companies act fairly and in the best interests of shareholders, employees, and the
public.
Definition
Companies Act: A South African law that governs how companies operate and protects
stakeholders.
Purpose of the Companies Act
● Encourages the development of businesses.
● Promotes accountability and transparency.
● Protects the rights of shareholders and the public.
Main Responsibilities Placed on Companies
● Keep accurate records of financial transactions.
● Hold Annual General Meetings (AGMs).
● Appoint directors responsibly.
● Submit annual financial statements to the CIPC.
Tip
A well-run company always follows the Companies Act to avoid legal trouble and gain
investor trust.
11

How It Protects Stakeholders
● Ensures fair treatment of shareholders.
● Prevents fraud through regular audits and reporting.
● Allows minority shareholders to take legal action if needed.
Did You Know?
The current Companies Act (Act 71 of 2008) replaced the old Act in 2011 to modernise
and simplify company law.

1.3 Memorandum of Incorporation (MoI)
Every company in South Africa must have a legal document called the Memorandum of
Incorporation (MoI). It acts like the rulebook for the company.
Definition
Memorandum of Incorporation (MoI): A legal document that outlines how a company is
structured and operated.
Function of the MoI
● Sets out the rights and duties of directors and shareholders.
● Defines company rules (e.g., how decisions are made).
● Allows some flexibility to add extra rules or remove standard ones.
12

Information Included in an MoI
● Company name and type.
● Number and types of shares.
● Voting rights.
● Procedures for meetings.
● Director powers and limits.
Real-Life Example
An MoI might state that only the CEO and one director can sign a contract worth more
than R1 million.
Tip
You can customise your MoI when registering a company—but it must still comply with
the Companies Act.

1.4 Registrar of Companies and Registration Certificate
Before a company can begin trading, it must be registered with the government. This is handled
by the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC).
Definition
Registrar of Companies: The official in charge of company registrations in South Africa.
14

The Role of the Registrar
● Maintains company records.
● Issues registration certificates.
● Ensures companies comply with the law.
Steps to Register a Company
1. Reserve a company name.
2. Complete registration forms (CoR forms).
3. Submit the MoI and ID documents.
4. Pay a registration fee.
5. Receive your company registration certificate.
Step Action
1. Name Reservation Check if the name is available on CIPC.
2. Form Submission Submit MoI, ID copies, and CoR forms.
3. Certificate Issued Legal proof of registration.
Real-Life Example
Sam wants to start a bakery called “Golden Crust Bakers (Pty) Ltd”. He completes the
registration online through the CIPC portal and receives his registration certificate within
a week.
Did You Know?
You can register a company online in South Africa using the CIPC eServices platform.

1.5 Authorised vs Issued Share Capital
When a company is formed, it decides how many shares it may issue. These are called
authorised shares. But not all shares are sold right away—only some are issued.
Definitions
Authorised Share Capital: The maximum number of shares a company is allowed to issue.
Issued Share Capital: The shares that have been sold or allocated to shareholders.
16

Table: Differences Explained
Term Meaning
Authorised Shares Allowed by the MoI, not necessarily issued.
Issued Shares Shares actually given to shareholders.
Example:
Zanele Traders (Pty) Ltd is authorised to issue 1 000 shares. They’ve only issued 600 so
far. The 400 remaining can be issued later to raise more money.
How Companies Manage Share Capital
● Shares are issued to raise capital for growth or projects.
● Companies may increase authorised capital through a special resolution.
● Issued shares appear in the company’s financial statements.
Tip
Issuing too many shares may reduce each shareholder’s control (called dilution).
Did You Know?
In South Africa, companies are no longer required to have a par value per share.

Johannesburg Securities Exchange (JSE): South Africa’s main stock exchange for public

The Role of the JSE in the Economy
● Encourages investment and savings.
● Helps businesses grow and create jobs.
● Provides information to help investors make decisions.

Unit 2: Company Roles and Responsibilities
A company needs leadership, control, and accountability to function properly. These
responsibilities are shared between shareholders, who own the company, and directors, who
manage it. Understanding how these roles are separated and defined will help you recognise
how companies are structured and how important decisions are made. This unit also explains
key principles like limited liability and the duties of directors, which protect the interests of
everyone involved.
2.1 Shareholders and Directors
Every company needs people to invest in it and others to run it. These roles are held by
shareholders and directors. While shareholders provide funding and have ownership rights,
directors are trusted to manage the company’s daily operations.
Definition
Shareholder: A person or institution that owns at least one share in a company.
Director: A person elected or appointed to oversee and manage the company’s operations.
20

Table: Roles and Responsibilities
Role Main Responsibilities
Shareholders ● Own shares in the company
● Vote at Annual General Meetings (AGMs)
● Receive dividends
● Appoint or remove directors
● Approve major changes in the company
Directors ● Run the company on a day-to-day basis
● Develop strategies and set company policies
● Ensure compliance with laws and ethical standards
● Prepare and approve financial statement
Example
Ayesha invests in a food company by buying shares. She becomes a shareholder. The
company is managed by a team of directors who make decisions about product pricing,
marketing, and staffing.
Tip
Shareholders don’t manage the business, but they do have the power to elect directors
and vote on important matters.
Did You Know?
Even a person who owns just one share can be a shareholder and have voting rights at
a general meeting.

2.2 Executive vs Non-Executive Directors
Not all directors are involved in the same way. Some are part of the company’s operations, while
others serve as outside advisors. Understanding these roles is important for good corporate
governance.
Definitions
Executive Director: A full-time employee who is actively involved in managing the company.
Non-Executive Director: An independent board member who does not manage the company
but helps with oversight and advice.
22

Real-Life Example
Simon is an executive director in charge of operations. Lindiwe, a retired accountant, is

advises the board.
Tip
Non-executive directors help make sure the board is not biased. Their outside view
helps identify risks the executive team might miss.
Did You Know?
South African law does not require non-executive directors, but good governance
practices recommend them—especially in public companies.

2.3 Limited Liability
One of the main benefits of forming a company is limited liability. This legal protection
encourages investment by lowering personal financial risk.
Definition
Limited Liability: A rule that says shareholders are not personally responsible for the debts
of the company beyond the money they invested.
How It Works
● If a company goes bankrupt or owes money, the shareholders don’t lose personal
property (like houses or cars).
● Only the money paid for the shares is at risk.
Example Scenario
Thuli invests R20 000 in a company. Later, the company goes into debt and cannot pay its
creditors. Thuli only loses her R20 000 investment—creditors cannot touch her personal
savings.
24

When Liability May Not Be Limited
● If directors commit fraud or act dishonestly, they can be held personally responsible.
Tip
Limited liability makes it safer to invest in companies and is one reason why companies are
popular for doing business.
Did You Know?
The term “Ltd” in a company’s name (e.g. Sipho Ltd) means it has limited liability.

2.4 Separation of Ownership and Control
In most companies, the people who own the business are not the same as those who manage
it. This system is called separation of ownership and control.
Definition
Separation of Ownership and Control: A company structure where shareholders (owners)
are different from directors (managers).
Table: How It Works
Shareholders Directors
Own the company Run the company
Elect directors Make business decisions
Receive profits (dividends) Develop company strategies
Benefits
● Allows professionals to manage operations efficiently.
● Shareholders can focus on investment returns.
Challenges
● Directors may act in their own interests instead of the shareholders’.
● Poor oversight can lead to bad decisions or fraud.
Example
In a large public company like Pick n Pay, thousands of shareholders own shares, but the
business is run by a team of professional directors.
26

Tip
To manage this separation, companies use internal controls, financial reporting, and
regular board meetings.
Did You Know?
This principle is one reason why strong governance policies, like the King Code, are
important in South African companies.

2.5 Duties of Directors
Directors are legally responsible for managing the company properly. Their actions must follow
both ethical and legal standards.
Table: Key Duties of Directors
Duty Description
Fiduciary Duty Act in the best interests of the company.
Duty of Care and Skill Make decisions using reasonable care and judgment.
Avoid Conflict of Interest Don’t use position for personal gain or allow business conflicts.
Act within Authority Follow the rules set out in the MoI and Companies Act.
Duty to Keep Records Ensure the company keeps accurate financial records and reports
truthfully.
Consequences of Breaching Duties
● Fines, imprisonment, or being banned from acting as a director.
● Damage to the company’s finances and reputation.
Example
A director who signs a false financial report may be fined and banned. The company may
lose investors due to lack of trust.
Tip
Directors can get professional advice to help make responsible decisions and avoid
legal risks.

Did You Know?
Directors must declare any personal interest in contracts or business deals. If they
don't, it's a breach of duty.

Unit 3: Ethics and Corporate Governance
Ethics and corporate governance are the foundation of a successful and trusted company.
Companies need to behave responsibly, follow rules, and ensure that their leadership is
transparent and accountable. In this unit, you’ll learn about professional accounting bodies like
SAICA and SAIPA, the King Code principles, types of unethical behaviour, business rescue, and
how internal control systems are used to manage risks and prevent fraud.
3.1 SAICA, SAIPA and King Code III
In the world of accounting, professionalism is very important. This section introduces two main
professional accounting bodies in South Africa and a code of good business practice called the
King Code III.
Definitions
SAICA: South African Institute of Chartered Accountants – oversees Chartered Accountants
(CA(SA)).
SAIPA: South African Institute of Professional Accountants – oversees Professional
Accountants.
King Code III: A set of corporate governance principles that guide how companies should be
managed.
Roles of SAICA and SAIPA
Body Main Role
SAICA Sets and monitors ethical and professional standards for Chartered Accountants.
SAIPA Regulates Professional Accountants, ensuring integrity and proper conduct.
31

Functions:
● Provide training and continuous development.
● Enforce codes of conduct.
● Offer guidance on ethical dilemmas.
Real-Life Example
A Chartered Accountant registered with SAICA must report any conflict of interest in
financial audits or risk suspension.
Tip
Always check if your accountant is registered with a recognised body—it’s a sign of
trust and quality.
King Code III Principles
The King Code is built on 3 core principles:
● Integrity: Acting with honesty and strong moral values.
● Transparency: Open communication and disclosure.
● Accountability: Taking responsibility for decisions and actions.
Did You Know?
South Africa was the first country in the world to adopt a corporate governance code
that includes sustainability.

3.2 Ethical Conduct, Disciplinary Measures, and Business Rescue
Ethical conduct means doing what is right. Companies must act legally and morally at all times.
When they do not, disciplinary actions and rescue measures may follow.
Definition
Ethical Conduct: Acting in a way that is honest, fair, and respectful of others.
Examples of Ethical Behaviour
● Paying workers fairly.
● Declaring all income for tax.
● Avoiding discrimination and exploitation.
33

Unethical Behaviour
● Lying about profits.
● Favouritism in awarding contracts.
● Misusing company funds.
Disciplinary Measures for Unethical Behaviour
● Verbal or written warnings.
● Suspension or dismissal.
● Reporting to authorities or accounting bodies (SAICA/SAIPA).
● Fines or legal prosecution.
Business Rescue Explained
Business rescue is a legal process designed to help companies in financial trouble. It gives
companies time to:
● Reorganise debts and contracts.
● Protect jobs.
● Return to profitability.
Step Action
1. Apply for business rescue Submit request to court or creditors.
2. Appoint a practitioner A qualified person takes over control.
3. Create recovery plan Negotiate with creditors and restructure.
Real-Life Example
Edcon, a major retail company, entered business rescue in 2020 to try to save jobs and
prevent collapse during the COVID-19 pandemic.
34

Tip
Business rescue is better than liquidation. It offers a second chance to keep the
company alive.
Did You Know?
During business rescue, legal actions against the company are temporarily suspended.
Flow Chart: Steps in the business rescue process
3.3 Corporate Governance, Conflict of Interest, Director Evaluations
Corporate governance refers to how companies are run. It ensures directors act responsibly and
make decisions that protect shareholders and other stakeholders.
Definitions
Corporate Governance: The system by which a company is directed and controlled.
Conflict of Interest: When a person’s private interests interfere with their professional duties.
35

Why Corporate Governance Matters
● Encourages responsible leadership.
● Prevents fraud and abuse of power.
● Builds trust with investors and the public.
Conflict of Interest Examples
● A director awarding a contract to their own company.
● Hiring family members without disclosing the relationship.
How to Avoid It:
● Declare all personal interests.
● Remove yourself from related decisions.
● Follow company rules and the MoI.
Evaluating Directors
Directors must be reviewed regularly to:
● Check performance.
● Ensure they act ethically.
● Assess decision-making and meeting attendance.
Evaluation Criteria Purpose
Attendance Shows commitment.
Strategic input Assesses contribution to planning.
Ethical behaviour Ensures honesty and responsibility.
Compliance with laws Protects the company from legal risks.
36

Example
A board uses an anonymous survey to rate each director’s contribution and identify gaps
in governance.
Tip
Strong governance can increase the company’s value and public image.
Did You Know?
Most public companies publish director evaluation reports in their annual reports.

3.4 Fraud, Embezzlement, Kickbacks
Companies lose millions every year to fraud and corruption. It is essential to understand the
types of unethical financial activities and how to prevent them.
Definitions
Fraud: Intentional deception for personal or business gain.
Embezzlement: Theft or misappropriation of company funds.
Kickbacks: Bribes or secret payments for business favours.
37

Examples
● Creating fake invoices for services never received (fraud).
● A cashier stealing money from sales (embezzlement).
● A supplier paying a manager to win a contract (kickback).
Prevention Methods
● Have clear policies and internal controls.
