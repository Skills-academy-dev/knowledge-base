Introduction

Whether you live on a farm or in the city, the knowledge you will gain here is important for

understanding how food is produced, how it reaches our homes, and how it supports the

economy and environment. This book is carefully designed to help you learn about the

relationship between soils, plants and animals in the production of food, fibre, fuel and other

valuable agricultural products. Agricultural Sciences is not just about farming — it is about life,

science, and your future role as a responsible citizen.

Agricultural Sciences teaches you how natural resources such as soil, water, plants and animals

are used to produce food and other products that we all depend on. It also highlights the need

for careful management and conservation of these resources to ensure they are available for

future generations.

By studying Agricultural Sciences, you will:

● Understand how science and technology are applied to improve food production and

ensure food security.

● Learn how to care for the environment, manage natural resources wisely, and treat

animals humanely.

● Develop problem-solving skills by exploring real-world issues in farming, food production,

and marketing.

● Appreciate the social and economic importance of agriculture to communities and the

country as a whole.

● Discover how agricultural knowledge, including indigenous practices, contributes to our

cultural heritage.

These skills and insights will be useful whether you choose a career in agriculture or any other

field, and they help you become an informed citizen who can make wise decisions about food,

health, and the environment.

Introduction

Have you ever wondered how a cow can turn rough, dry grass into rich, creamy milk? Or how

chickens can peck at grains and grow strong enough to lay eggs almost every day? It all comes

down to the incredible way animals eat, digest, and use their food.

In this module, you will explore how the digestive systems of different farm animals work — from

the four-chambered stomach of a cow to the grinding gizzard of a chicken. You will discover

what happens to feed after it is swallowed, how nutrients are absorbed, and why a balanced diet

is essential for healthy animals.

Understanding animal nutrition is not only interesting, but it also helps farmers raise animals that

grow well, produce more, and stay healthy. By the end of this module, you’ll see how smart

feeding choices lead to thriving farms and plenty of food on our tables.

Unit 1: The Structure of the Alimentary Canal

Farm animals eat different types of feed. Their digestive systems are specially designed

(adapted) to get the best value from what they eat, whether it is grass, grain or insects. This unit

will help you understand the structure of the alimentary canal (the digestive tract) in

ruminants, non-ruminants and fowls, and how each part helps break down feed so that nutrients

can be absorbed.

1.1 Comparison of the External Structure of Alimentary Canals

Different farm animals have alimentary canals designed to suit their diets. Ruminants like cattle

and sheep are adapted to digest tough, fibrous plants. Pigs, as non-ruminants, need

easier-to-digest feeds. Fowls have a completely different system that relies on storing and

grinding food.

Ruminants have complex stomachs with four compartments, allowing them to ferment and

digest roughage that other animals cannot handle.

Definition:

Ruminant: An animal that chews cud and has a stomach with four compartments.

Non-ruminants have a single-chambered stomach. They rely on stomach acids and enzymes

● Pigs: Have sharp incisors and large molars for chewing. Their stomach secretes strong

● Horses: Cannot regurgitate feed. They have a large caecum after the small intestine that

3\. Fowls (birds with crop & gizzard)

Fowls have a completely different digestive system. They use:

● Crop: A soft pouch in the oesophagus that stores and moistens food.

● Gizzard: A muscular stomach that grinds food, often with swallowed small stones.

A chicken pecks at maize kernels, stores them in its crop, then later the gizzard crushes

1.2 Functions and Adaptations of Digestive Structures

Every part of the digestive system has a role, from grabbing feed to mixing it with saliva,

pushing it through the gut, digesting it, and finally absorbing nutrients.

15

Mouth, teeth and tongue in ruminants vs non-ruminants

● Ruminants:

● No upper incisors. Instead, a tough dental pad works with lower incisors to cut

grass.

● Large, muscular tongue wraps around grass.

● Molars grind feed coarsely.

● Non-ruminants (pigs):

● Sharp incisors for biting.

● Canines for tearing (less important in pigs).

● Molars and premolars for grinding.

● Fowls:

● Have a pointed beak but no teeth. They swallow grains whole.

Did You Know?

A cow chews its cud up to 60 times per minute when resting.

Oesophagus and the role of peristalsis

The oesophagus is the muscular tube that moves feed from the mouth to the stomach.

● Peristalsis: Wave-like contractions push food down.

● In ruminants, reverse peristalsis brings cud back up for rechewing.

Definition:

Peristalsis: Wave-like squeezing of muscles that moves food along the digestive tract.

16

Tip:

If you ever see a cow lying down chewing with a slow jaw motion, it is likely rechewing

its cud.

Forestomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum) vs simple stomach

● Ruminants:

● Have three forestomachs before the true stomach (abomasum).

● Use microbes to ferment cellulose.

● Non-ruminants:

● Have a simple stomach with glands that secrete hydrochloric acid and enzymes

like pepsin.

Abomasum (true stomach)

● Functions like the stomach in pigs.

● Secretes:

● Hydrochloric acid (HCl): kills bacteria, activates enzymes.

● Pepsin: begins protein digestion.

● Rennin: clots milk in young calves.

Example:

In calves, rennin helps form a milk curd that digests slowly.

Small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum)

Divided into three sections:

● Duodenum: Receives bile and pancreatic juices.

● Jejunum: Main site for digestion and absorption.

● Ileum: Continues absorption.

Lined with villi that increase surface area.

17

Large intestine (caecum, colon, rectum)

● Absorbs water and minerals, forms faeces.

● In horses, the caecum is very large for fermenting fibre.

● In fowls, two small caeca do a little fermentation.

Accessory organs (salivary glands, liver, pancreas)

● Salivary glands: produce saliva (lubricates food, contains amylase in pigs).

● Liver: makes bile which emulsifies fats.

● Pancreas: makes enzymes for proteins, starches, fats.

Did You Know?

Bile doesn’t digest fat itself — it breaks fat into tiny droplets so enzymes can work

better. This is called emulsification.

1.3 Internal Structure of Digestive Organs

Inside the digestive tract, you’ll find many clever structures that help animals get the most from

their feed.

Papillae of the rumen

● Finger-like projections that increase surface area to absorb VFAs (energy for the

animal).

Tip:

Healthy papillae mean better absorption. Diets too low in fibre can lead to poor papillae

development.

18

Honeycomb structure of the reticulum

● Looks like a honeycomb.

● Traps heavy or sharp objects to protect the stomach.

Real Life Example:

Farmers sometimes give cows magnets that stay in the reticulum to catch nails or wire.

Lamellae of the omasum

● Thin folds that act like filters.

● Squeeze out water and fine feed particles before entering the abomasum.

Glands in the abomasum

● Produce gastric juices (HCl and pepsin).

● Start breaking down proteins into peptides.

Villi and glands of Lieberkühn in the small intestine

● Villi: tiny, finger-like projections that absorb nutrients.

● Glands of Lieberkühn: between the villi, produce digestive juices.

Structure Main function

Glands of Lieberkühn Release enzymes for final digestion

Unit 2: Digestion Processes

Once feed enters the digestive system, it must be broken down into small, usable nutrients. This

happens through mechanical digestion (physically breaking feed into smaller pieces) and

chemical digestion (using enzymes and acids to change large food molecules into simpler

substances).

Different animals have digestion processes suited to their feeding habits. In this unit, you will

learn key terms, explore how digestion takes place in different farm animals, and understand

how nutrients are absorbed.

2.1 Key Definitions

Before studying the steps of digestion, it is important to understand what digestion, absorption,

and assimilation each mean.

Digestion

Digestion is the process where feed is broken down into small, simple substances so the body

can use it. It has two parts:

● Mechanical digestion: Physically breaking food into smaller pieces (like chewing).

● Chemical digestion: Using enzymes and acids to change large molecules into small

ones.

Definition

Digestion: Breaking feed into smaller, usable parts.

Absorption

Absorption happens when the simple nutrients made by digestion move through the walls of

the intestines into the blood or lymph system.

22

Practical Example:

Glucose from maize meal is absorbed through the villi in the small intestine.

Assimilation

Assimilation is when the absorbed nutrients are taken up by cells and used to build muscles,

produce milk, or give energy.

Tip:

Think of it like baking:

● Digestion is breaking the cake down into crumbs,

● Absorption is moving the crumbs into the body,

● Assimilation is using those crumbs to build muscles or produce milk.

2.2 Mechanical Digestion

Mechanical digestion starts in the mouth and continues in different ways in other animals. It

helps by increasing the surface area so enzymes can work faster.

Chewing (mastication)

● In mammals like cattle and pigs, chewing breaks feed into smaller pieces, mixes it

with saliva and makes it easier to swallow.

Definition:

Mastication: The process of chewing.

23

Did You Know?

Cows can make over 100 litres of saliva a day, which helps soften dry grass.

Grinding in the gizzard

● Birds like chickens have no teeth. Instead:

● Crop: Stores feed.

● Gizzard: A strong muscular organ that uses swallowed stones to grind feed into

a fine mash.

Practical Example:

A chicken pecking at maize grains swallows them whole. The gizzard crushes them to release

nutrients.

Rumination: regurgitation, rechewing, reswallowing

● Ruminants like cattle, sheep and goats bring up partially digested feed (cud) from the

rumen back to the mouth to chew again.

● This process is called rumination.

Table: What happens during each stage

Stage What happens

Regurgitation Cud moves from rumen to mouth

Rechewing Chews cud into finer pieces

Reswallowing Goes back to rumen, then reticulum and omasum

● It breaks big fat droplets into small droplets (emulsification) so lipase can work faster.

Without bile, fats would clump together and be much harder to digest.

The rumen is like a fermentation tank. It breaks down plant fibres that non-ruminants cannot

Requirements for microbes

For microbes to work well in the rumen, they need:

● pH: Slightly acidic (about 6–7).

● Temperature: Around 39°C.

● Fibre: To keep the rumen healthy and moving.

● Water: Helps fermentation and keeps microbes alive.

Sudden diet changes, like too much maize too quickly, can kill these microbes, causing

2.6 Absorption Processes

Once feed is fully digested into small molecules, it must be absorbed into the body through the

gut walls.

Absorption by villi in the small intestine

● Villi are tiny finger-like structures lining the small intestine.

● They increase surface area, making absorption faster.

29

Definition:

Villi: Tiny projections that absorb nutrients.

Did You Know?

If you spread out all the villi in a cow’s small intestine, it would cover a tennis court\!

Table: Processes - osmosis, diffusion and active transport

Process How it works

Osmosis Water moves from where there is more water to where there is less.

Diffusion Nutrients move from a high concentration to a low concentration.

Active transport Nutrients move against the concentration gradient, using energy.

Absorption of VFAs through the rumen wall

● In ruminants, VFAs made by microbes are directly absorbed through the rumen wall

into the blood.

● This means cattle get most energy straight from the rumen.

Practical Example:

This is why cattle can live on fibrous veld — microbes make VFAs that give them energy

Unit 3: Components and Digestibility of Feed

Animals rely on feed to supply the nutrients they need for growth, reproduction, milk, meat and

egg production, and staying healthy. However, not all feeds are the same. The value of a feed

depends on what nutrients it contains and how well the animal’s body can digest and use these

nutrients. In this unit, you will explore the main nutrients in feed, what they do, and how to judge

the quality of feed through digestibility concepts.

3.1 Main Nutrients in Animal Feed

Feeds are made up of different nutrients that work together to keep animals alive and

productive. Missing any one of them can lead to health problems or poor production.

1\. Water: importance, functions, sources

Water is often forgotten but it is the most important nutrient. Animals can lose weight quickly

or even die without enough water.

Functions of water:

● Helps keep body temperature steady.

● Aids digestion by moving feed through intestines.

● Carries nutrients in blood.

● Flushes out waste through urine and sweat.

Sources of water:

● Drinking water from dams, troughs or rivers.

● Water inside feed (e.g. fresh pasture, vegetables).

Did You Know?

A dairy cow can drink 50 litres or more per day, especially in hot weather.

Practical Example:

Cows on irrigated green pasture may drink less water because they get moisture from grass.

32

2\. Proteins: amino acids, functions, deficiency effects

Proteins are made up of building blocks called amino acids. They are essential for growth,

making milk, eggs, wool, and repairing body tissues.

Functions:

● Build muscles and body tissues.

● Make enzymes, hormones and blood cells.

● Important for pregnancy and milk.

Deficiency effects:

● Slow growth in young animals.

● Less milk or small eggs.

● Poor healing and more sickness.

Practical Example:

A broiler farmer feeds soybean meal (high protein) to chickens so they grow quickly.

Definition:

Amino acids: The small units that join to form proteins.

Tip:

Young growing animals and dairy cows need more protein than dry cows or old sheep.

33

3\. Carbohydrates: starch, cellulose, glycogen

Carbohydrates provide most of the energy animals need.

Table: Types of carbohydrates:

Type Example & where found Notes

Starch Maize, barley, sorghum Easily digested in stomach.

Cellulose Grass, hay, silage Needs microbes in rumen to break down.

Glycogen Stored in liver & muscles Quick energy reserve.

Practical Example:

Pigs eat maize meal (high in starch) because they can’t digest cellulose well.

4\. Fats and oils: energy content, fatty acids

Fats are rich energy sources — they provide about 2.5 times more energy than

carbohydrates.

Functions:

● Give high energy.

● Help absorb vitamins A, D, E, K.

● Make milk creamy and improve skin health.

●

Definition:

Fatty acids: The simple parts that make up fats.

Practical Example:

Adding sunflower cake to cattle feed increases energy, helping maintain weight in winter.

34

Tip:

Too much fat in feed can cause diarrhoea, especially in pigs.

5\. Minerals

Minerals are needed in small (trace) or large (macro) amounts, but all are essential for life.

Macro-elements (needed in large amounts) Role

Calcium (Ca), Phosphorus (P) Strong bones, milk production

Unit 4: Types of Feed and Feed Flow Planning

Feeding is one of the biggest costs on a farm and directly affects how well animals grow,

produce milk, meat, or eggs, and stay healthy. Farmers must know the different types of feeds,

what they supply to the animal, how to use supplements safely, and how to plan feeding across

all seasons to avoid shortages. In this unit, you will learn how to classify feeds, understand the

use of supplements like urea, and plan a feed flow programme to ensure animals are well fed all

year.
