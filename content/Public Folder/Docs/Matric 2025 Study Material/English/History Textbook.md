Africa and the world — from your local community to global powers. It helps us understand how

societies changed, how conflicts arose, how leaders influenced history, and how ordinary people

fought for justice. In simple terms, History is the study of how the past connects to the present

and influences the future.

By learning History, you will:

● Understand how societies developed – Explore the Cold War, the rise of independent

African states, civil rights struggles, resistance to apartheid, and South Africa’s transition

to democracy.

● Think critically about evidence – Analyse sources such as photographs, speeches,

freedom.

interpretation, and essay writing, which are valued in careers like law, journalism,

teaching, politics, and public service.

● Stay connected to your world – By studying the past, you will better understand

debates about democracy, human rights, globalisation, and social justice in your own

community and around the world.

Introduction

The Cold War was one of the most important periods of the twentieth century. It was not a direct

war between armies, but a long struggle for power and influence between two superpowers: the

United States of America (USA) and the Soviet Union (USSR). After the Second World War

ended in 1945, the world was divided between two competing systems — capitalism in the West

and communism in the East. This rivalry shaped global politics for more than four decades.

In this module, you will explore how the Cold War developed and spread. You will begin with the

breakdown of wartime cooperation, focusing on the Yalta and Potsdam conferences and the

growing mistrust between the USA and USSR. You will examine how each superpower built

spheres of influence, with the USA using policies such as the Truman Doctrine and Marshall

Plan, while the USSR controlled Eastern Europe through satellite states. Key crises such as the

Berlin Blockade, the construction of the Berlin Wall, and the Cuban Missile Crisis highlight how

close the world came to direct conflict.

The module also looks at how the Cold War extended beyond Europe. You will study case

studies of China and Vietnam, which show how communist revolutions and wars for

independence shaped global politics. China’s transformation under Mao Zedong into a world

power and Vietnam’s resistance against the USA demonstrate how the Cold War played out in

Asia.

By the end of this module, you should have a clear understanding of why the Cold War began,

how it spread, and how it influenced global and local struggles. You will also develop skills in

analysing sources, evaluating different historical interpretations, and writing essays that assess

causes, consequences, and debates about responsibility.

Unit 1: Origins of the Cold War

The end of the Second World War in 1945 brought hope for peace, but it also created tensions

between the former allies, the USA and the USSR. Their very different political and economic

systems — capitalism and communism — led to suspicion and mistrust. This unit explores how

these tensions developed into the Cold War, a global conflict that influenced countries across

the world, including South Africa. By studying this unit, you will understand why the Cold War

began, how it shaped international politics, and why it is still important for understanding our

world today.

1.1 End of World War II and the Yalta & Potsdam Conferences

After defeating Nazi Germany, the Allied leaders met to decide the future of Europe. Two

important conferences — Yalta (February 1945) and Potsdam (July 1945) — revealed deep

differences between the USA, Britain, and the USSR.

● Yalta Conference (February 1945):

○ Germany was to be divided into four zones (USA, USSR, Britain, later France).

○ Berlin, the capital, would also be divided.

○ Free elections were promised in Eastern Europe.

○ The USSR agreed to join the war against Japan.

● Potsdam Conference (July 1945):

○ Leadership changes: Truman (USA) replaced Roosevelt; Attlee replaced

Churchill (Britain).

○ Disagreements arose over Germany’s future and reparations.

○ The USA revealed its development of the atomic bomb, increasing Soviet

suspicion.

Definitions

Reparations: Payments demanded from defeated countries after a war.

Conference: A formal meeting between leaders to discuss political decisions.

Tip

The shift from cooperation to mistrust began here. Potsdam especially showed how uneasy

the alliance had become.

1.2 Ideological differences: Capitalism vs Communism

The USA and the USSR had very different political and economic systems. These opposing

ideologies shaped their decisions after the war and made cooperation extremely difficult. Each

side believed its system was superior and wanted to spread it globally.

● Capitalism (USA): Based on private property, free markets, and democratic elections.

● Communism (USSR): Based on state control of resources, collective ownership, and

one-party rule.

● These differences created suspicion and made compromise nearly impossible.

1.3 Soviet satellite states in Eastern Europe

The USSR wanted security from future invasions after suffering heavy losses in WWII. To

achieve this, it took control of countries in Eastern Europe. These countries became known as

satellite states because they followed Moscow’s orders.

● Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany, and others became communist.

● Elections were manipulated to ensure communist victories.

● The USSR argued this was for security; the USA saw it as expansionism.

Definition

Satellite state: A country that is formally independent but controlled by another powerful

nation.

1.4 The USA’s policy of containment: Truman Doctrine & Marshall Plan

The USA responded to Soviet expansion with the policy of containment. This meant stopping

communism from spreading further. Containment shaped US foreign policy throughout the Cold

War.● Truman Doctrine (1947):

○ Provided aid to countries threatened by communism.

○ First applied in Greece and Turkey.

● Marshall Plan (1947):

○ Billions of dollars in aid to rebuild Western Europe.

○ Aimed to reduce poverty (believed to fuel communism).

○ USSR rejected it and banned Eastern bloc states from participating.

Tip

Think of containment as “boxing in communism.”

1.5 Berlin Crises (1949–61): Blockade, Airlift, and Wall

Berlin, divided deep inside Soviet-controlled East Germany, became a key symbol of Cold War

tensions. Both sides wanted control of the city, leading to major crises that nearly sparked war.

● Berlin Blockade (1948–1949): USSR cut off road, rail, and canal access to West Berlin.

● Berlin Airlift: USA and Britain flew in supplies for nearly a year, showing commitment to

defend West Berlin.

● Berlin Wall (1961): Built by East Germany (backed by USSR) to stop East Berliners

escaping to the West.

Definition

Blockade: Preventing supplies from entering an area.

Did You Know?

At the height of the Airlift, a plane landed in West Berlin every 45 seconds\!

1.6 NATO vs Warsaw Pact (military alliances)

The mistrust between East and West led to the formation of rival military alliances. These

alliances meant that any conflict between the USA and USSR risked drawing in many other

countries.

● NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation, 1949): USA, Canada, and Western

European countries. Defence alliance against Soviet attack.

● Warsaw Pact (1955): USSR and Eastern bloc countries. Defence alliance against

NATO.

● Both alliances built large armies and nuclear weapons, escalating tensions.

1.7 The Cuban Missile Crisis as an example of brinkmanship

In 1962, the Cold War reached its most dangerous point when the USSR placed nuclear

missiles in Cuba, just 150 km from the USA. For thirteen days, the world feared nuclear war.

● USA discovered Soviet missile sites in Cuba.

● President Kennedy responded with a naval blockade.

● After tense negotiations, USSR agreed to withdraw missiles in exchange for US

promises not to invade Cuba.

● The crisis showed how close the world could come to nuclear disaster.

Definition

Brinkmanship: Pushing a dangerous situation to the edge of disaster to achieve an

advantage.

South African Link

Cuba later played a major role in Angola’s civil war (1970s–80s), linking Cold War tensions to

1.8 Historical debate: Who was to blame? (different interpretations)

Historians disagree about who caused the Cold War. Different schools of thought have

developed over time, showing how interpretations of history can change depending on evidence

and perspective.

● Traditional view (USA, 1940s–50s): USSR caused the Cold War through aggression.

● Revisionist view (1960s–70s): USA provoked the USSR through policies like the

Marshall Plan and atomic bomb.

● Post-revisionist view (1970s–present): Both sides were to blame due to

misunderstandings and mistrust.

● New evidence after 1991: Soviet archives show USSR leaders were often defensive

rather than aggressive.

Did You Know?

The release of Soviet archives after 1991 changed many historians’ views of the Cold War.

Unit 2: Extension of the Cold War – Case Studies

Although the Cold War began in Europe, it soon spread across the globe. Nowhere was this

more visible than in Asia. Two key case studies — China (1949–1976) and Vietnam

(1954–1975) — show how communist revolutions, ideological struggles, and US attempts to

contain communism shaped global politics. These struggles not only influenced Asia but also

provided lessons and inspiration for independence movements in Africa, including South Africa.

2.1 China (1949 onwards)

In 1949, after years of civil war, Mao Zedong’s Communist Party won control of China. This

marked a turning point in the Cold War, as the world’s most populated country became a

communist power. China’s policies and its relations with both the USSR and USA changed the

balance of global politics.

Establishment of Communist China (1949)

China had been torn apart by decades of civil war between the Nationalists and Communists. In

1949, Mao Zedong and the Communists claimed victory, establishing a new communist state

that transformed the Cold War balance.

● The Chinese Communist Party (CCP), under Mao, defeated the Nationalist Party

(Kuomintang).

● On 1 October 1949, Mao declared the People’s Republic of China (PRC).

● Nationalists retreated to the island of Taiwan, where they set up a rival government

supported by the USA.

● The USA refused to recognise the PRC and instead kept diplomatic ties with Taiwan until

the 1970s.

Definition

People’s Republic of China (PRC): The official name of communist China under Mao.

The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

To strengthen his control and revive communist spirit, Mao launched a radical campaign called

the Cultural Revolution. This caused massive social and political upheaval.

● Mao encouraged young people to form groups known as Red Guards, who attacked

anyone considered “enemies of communism.”

● Schools, universities, and cultural institutions were disrupted.

● Millions of intellectuals and officials were humiliated, imprisoned, or killed.

● Traditional Chinese culture and religion were targeted.

Nearly every Chinese citizen had to carry Mao’s Little Red Book, filled with his political

Relations with the USSR and USA (1949–1973)

At first, China and the USSR were allies, but by the 1960s, they became bitter rivals. This

opened the way for an unexpected relationship between China and the USA.

● 1949–1950s: Strong alliance with the USSR. The USSR gave China economic and

military aid.

● 1960s: The Sino-Soviet Split developed due to disputes over leadership in the

communist world.

● 1972: US President Richard Nixon visited China. This was a turning point in the Cold

War, showing that the USA and China could cooperate against the USSR.

Definition

Sino-Soviet Split: The breakdown of relations between China and the USSR in the 1960s.

22

Flow Chart: Timeline of China’s relations with USSR (alliance → split) and USA (hostility →

Changing Relations with Neighbours

China’s foreign policy also affected its neighbours, often through conflict and tension.

● Tibet (1950): China invaded Tibet, leading to ongoing resistance.

● India (1962): China fought a border war with India.

● Vietnam: China supported communist North Vietnam against the USA.

● Taiwan: With US backing, Taiwan remained a rival to Communist China.

South African Link

Just as China influenced its neighbours through force and diplomacy, apartheid South Africa

sought to dominate southern Africa in the 1970s and 1980s.

China as a World Power by Mao’s Death (1976)

Despite internal challenges, China emerged as a significant world power during Mao’s

leadership.

● Tested nuclear weapons in 1964, joining the nuclear powers.

● Recognised as a global player when it took Taiwan’s seat at the United Nations

Security Council in 1971.

● Its population, size, and growing influence made it impossible to ignore in Cold War

politics.

Economic Liberalisation After Mao (post-1976)

After Mao’s death, China shifted direction under new leadership.

● Deng Xiaoping introduced market reforms while maintaining communist political control.

● China opened its economy to trade and foreign investment.

● These reforms laid the foundation for China’s future role as a global economic power.

2.2 Vietnam (1954–1975)

Vietnam was a small country, but it became one of the Cold War’s fiercest battlegrounds. For

decades, Vietnamese people resisted foreign control — first against the French, then against

the USA. The Vietnam War showed how a determined national liberation struggle could defeat a

global superpower.

Struggles Against Colonialism Before WWII

Vietnam’s history of resistance shaped its role in the Cold War. For decades, Vietnamese

people fought against foreign powers, first France, then Japan, and later the USA.

● Colonised by France in the 1800s.

● Occupied by Japan during WWII.

● After the war, nationalist leader Ho Chi Minh declared independence, but France tried to

regain control.

● The French defeat at Dien Bien Phu (1954) led to the division of Vietnam into North

(communist) and South (anti-communist).

Stages of the Vietnam War

The Vietnam War went through three main phases, involving both local fighters and superpower

intervention.

● 1957–1965: Viet Cong vs South Vietnamese Army

○ Guerrilla warfare by Viet Cong, backed by North Vietnam.

○ South supported by USA

● 1965–1969: US Involvement and Escalation

○ USA sent over 500 000 troops.

○ Operation Rolling Thunder: heavy bombing campaign.

○ Despite military strength, the USA could not defeat guerrilla tactics.

● 1969–1975: Withdrawal and Political Impact

○ USA began withdrawing troops under Nixon’s “Vietnamisation” policy.

○ 1975: North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon, uniting the country under

communism.

24

Definition

Guerrilla warfare: Fighting by small groups using ambushes and surprise attacks.

Flow chart: The three stages of the Vietnam War

Perspectives: Vietnamese vs US Views

The Vietnam War was seen very differently by those involved.

● Vietnamese perspective: A national liberation struggle to reunify the country.

● US perspective: A fight to stop communism spreading in Asia (“Domino Theory”).

Tip

Always compare perspectives — one person’s “liberation” may be another’s “aggression.”

Vietnam War as a Global Issue

The Vietnam War was not just about Vietnam — it became a symbol of Cold War struggles

worldwide.

● Huge anti-war protests in the USA, Europe, and other parts of the world.

● Many countries criticised US involvement as imperialism.

● The war encouraged other liberation movements by showing a small country could resist

a superpower.

South African Link

Anti-apartheid activists in the 1970s saw Vietnam as proof that a determined people could

overcome a powerful enemy.

25

Memory of the War in Vietnam and the USA

The Vietnam War is remembered differently in Vietnam and the USA, reflecting the pain and

pride of each side.

● Vietnam: Celebrated as a heroic victory.

● USA: Remembered as a failure that divided the nation.

● Films, books, and memorials show the lasting impact of the war.

Case Study 1: The Berlin Crisis (1948–1949)

Background

After WWII, Germany and Berlin were divided among the USA, Britain, France, and the

USSR. In 1948, the Western Allies introduced a new currency and began rebuilding West

Germany. Stalin saw this as a threat and blocked all access routes into West Berlin.

The USA and Britain responded with the Berlin Airlift, flying in food, coal, and medical

supplies for nearly a year. The blockade failed, and in 1949 Stalin lifted it. This deepened Cold

War mistrust and confirmed the division of Germany into West and East.

Case Study 2: The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)

Background

In 1959, Fidel Castro took power in Cuba, aligning with the USSR. In 1962, US spy planes

discovered Soviet nuclear missiles on the island. Kennedy responded with a naval blockade.

For 13 tense days, the world feared nuclear war. In the end, Khrushchev agreed to remove

missiles from Cuba if the USA promised not to invade Cuba. Secretly, the USA also removed

missiles from Turkey.

Case Study 3: The Vietnam War (1954–1975)

Background

After defeating French colonial rule at Dien Bien Phu (1954), Vietnam was divided: North

(communist, led by Ho Chi Minh) and South (anti-communist, supported by the USA). The

USA intervened heavily to prevent the spread of communism.

Despite massive bombing campaigns and half a million US troops, the Viet Cong’s guerrilla

tactics and growing public opposition in the USA forced withdrawal. In 1975, Saigon fell, and

Vietnam was united under communism.

Summary

The Cold War was a global conflict of ideas, politics, and influence between the USA and the

USSR after the Second World War. It shaped world events for more than forty years and

influenced Africa and South Africa as well.

Key Points

● The Cold War developed because of ideological differences: capitalism in the USA

and communism in the USSR.

● Early tensions appeared at the Yalta and Potsdam Conferences, where the Allies

disagreed about the future of Europe.

● The USSR controlled Eastern Europe through satellite states, while the USA responded

with a policy of containment through the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan.

● Crises in Berlin (1948–49, 1961) and the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) showed how

close the world came to open conflict.

● Rival military alliances — NATO and the Warsaw Pact — formalised the division of

Europe.

● Different historical interpretations exist about who was responsible for the Cold War.

● In China, Mao’s communist revolution in 1949 and the later Sino-Soviet split changed

the balance of world power.

● In Vietnam, a long war (1954–1975) saw the defeat of the USA by communist forces,

proving the strength of local resistance against a superpower.

● Case Studies:

○ Berlin Crisis – first Cold War confrontation.

○ Cuban Missile Crisis – closest the world came to nuclear war.

○ Vietnam War – showed the limits of US military power.

The Cold War was more than a rivalry between two superpowers. It was a struggle that shaped

the politics of Europe, Asia, Africa, and even South Africa. By studying its origins, crises, and

case studies, we see how global tensions influenced local struggles for freedom and justice.
