Introduction

Geography is all around you. It explains the world we live in — from the weather systems that

bring rain and drought, to the rivers that shape our landscapes, to the cities and economies that

sustain human life. It helps us understand how natural environments function, how people

interact with them, and how global issues affect our daily lives. In simple terms, Geography is

the study of Earth, its people, and the dynamic processes that connect them.

By learning Geography, you will:

● Understand how the Earth works in real situations – You’ll explore how weather

patterns form, how rivers change landscapes, how settlements develop, and how

economies grow and adapt.

● Make informed choices – Whether it’s decisions about water use, farming, urban living,

or environmental conservation, Geography equips you with the knowledge to weigh up

risks and benefits.

● Tackle global challenges – You will think critically about issues like climate change,

urbanisation, food security, and sustainability, and suggest possible solutions.

● Prepare for exciting careers – From urban and regional planning to environmental

management, tourism, teaching, transport, and government, Geography provides skills

and knowledge valued by universities and employers.

● Stay connected to your world – You’ll be able to understand news about weather,

natural disasters, urban development, and economic changes, and see how these affect

your community and the wider world.

Geographical Skills and Techniques Module 1

Introduction

Geographical skills form the foundation of your Geography studies. They help you to read,

interpret, and apply information from maps, photographs, and data sources. These skills are not

just for school – they are useful in everyday life, from finding directions to analysing climate

change, and even in future careers such as town planning, environmental management,

surveying, and disaster risk reduction.

In this module, you will practise essential mapwork techniques, learn to interpret topographic

and orthophoto maps, and explore Geographic Information Systems (GIS). You will also

strengthen your ability to use atlases to compare physical and human geography. These skills

prepare you for both your NSC examinations and for making informed decisions about

real-world issues in South Africa.

Unit 1: Mapwork Techniques

Maps are powerful tools that help us understand the world around us. In this unit, you will learn

how to use scale, contours, directions, and grid references to interpret maps accurately. These

skills are essential for solving real-life problems such as planning routes, measuring distances,

and understanding weather patterns in South Africa.

1.1 Applying map skills: scale, contours and cross-sections

Maps are reduced versions of the Earth’s surface. The scale shows the relationship between

distances on the map and actual distances on the ground.

The three types of scale:

● Word scale: e.g. 1 cm = 1 km.

● Ratio scale: e.g. 1:50 000 (1 cm on the map = 500 m on the ground).

● Line scale: A drawn line divided into distance units.

1.2 Direction: magnetic north, true north and magnetic declination

Maps show three types of north:

● True North: Points to the geographic North Pole.

● Magnetic North: Direction a compass needle points (moves slowly over time).

● Grid North: Direction along map grid lines.

Figure 4: A compass rose used to indicate direction

Via Afrika Grade 12 Geography Study Guide

Figure 5: True north, magnetic north and true bearing

Via Afrika Grade 12 Geography Study Guide

13

Magnetic Declination is the angle between True North and Magnetic North. This must be

taken into account when using a compass.

Worked Example — Magnetic Declination

In 2002, the magnetic declination of an area was 23°22′ west of True North.

The mean annual change = 12′ westwards.

Calculate the declination in 2010.

Step 1: Find the time difference: 2010 – 2002 = 8 years.

Step 2: Multiply by annual change: 8 × 12′ = 96′ = 1°36′.

Step 3: Add (since it is westwards): 23°22′ + 1°36′ = 24°58′ west.

The 2010 declination is 24°58′ west.

1.3 Grid referencing

Grid references help us locate exact positions on maps.

● Four-figure reference gives a grid square (e.g. 3426).

● Six-figure reference gives a precise point inside the square (e.g. 342653).

Worked Example - Step-by-Step Calculation:

1\. Grid Square:

The dam wall is in square 3319, so:

○ The easting (the left-hand side of the square) = 33

○ The northing (the bottom of the square) = 19

2\. Position within the square:

○ “Three-tenths across” means 0.3 across the square from the left-hand easting line.

Add 0.3 to 33 → 33.3 → written as 333

○ “Halfway up” means 0.5 up the square from the bottom northing line.

Add 0.5 to 19 → 19.5 → written as 195

3\. Six-figure grid reference:

Combine the three-digit easting and three-digit northing.

Six-figure grid reference = 333195

Maps and aerial photographs contain both physical features (natural) and constructed

features (human-made).

● Physical features: rivers, hills, valleys, forests.

● Constructed features: roads, buildings, dams, railways.

To analyse them effectively, geographers follow a step-by-step approach, looking at climate,

relief, landforms, drainage, settlements, and economic activities.

Tip for Exams

Always start with natural features (climate, relief, drainage) before moving to human

features (settlements, land use).

On a Durban topographic map, you can identify the Umgeni River (physical) and the N2

Unit 2: Topographic Maps

Topographic maps give us a detailed view of both natural and human features of the land. In this

unit, you will explore symbols, contours, cross-sections, and slopes to interpret landscapes.

These skills are especially useful in South Africa for farming, road construction, water

management, and disaster planning

2.1 1:50 000 maps: conventional signs and symbols

Topographic maps at a scale of 1:50 000 are widely used in South Africa. At this scale, 1 cm on

the map equals 500 m on the ground.

To interpret these maps, you need to understand the conventional signs and symbols. They

are standardised across all South African maps.

● Colours:

○ Blue = water (rivers, dams, lakes, marshes).

○ Brown = contours and spot heights.

○ Black = man-made features (buildings, roads, railways).

○ Green = vegetation.

● Examples of symbols:

○ Black square = building.

○ Triangle with number = trig beacon.

○ Dashed line = footpath.

○ Double red line = national road.

2.2 Contours and landforms

Contours are lines joining points of equal height above sea level. They show the shape of the

land.● Close contours = steep slope (e.g. Drakensberg mountains).

● Far apart contours = gentle slope (e.g. Free State plains).

Landforms visible from contour patterns:

● Hill – concentric circles increasing in height.

● Valley – V-shape pointing upstream.

● Saddle – two hills with a dip between them.

● Cliff – contours close together.

2.3 Cross-sections on 1:50 000 maps

A cross-section is a side profile of the land between two points on a map.

Steps to construct a cross-section:

1\. Draw a straight line between two points (e.g. A–B).

2\. Place a strip of paper along the line and mark all contour heights.

3\. Transfer these marks to graph paper with a vertical scale.

4\. Plot the points and join them smoothly.

2.5 Intervisibility

Intervisibility means whether two points can be seen from each other. A straight line drawn

between them will show if higher ground blocks the view.

Steps:

1\. Draw a cross-section between two points.

2\. Draw a line of sight from one point to the other.

3\. If the line crosses higher ground, the points are not intervisible.

Real-life Example

In the Cape Fold Mountains, intervisibility may be blocked by high ridges.

Unit 3: Aerial Photographs and Orthophoto Maps

Aerial and orthophoto maps show us real images of the Earth’s surface. In this unit, you will

learn how to interpret these photographs and compare them with topographic maps. These

skills are used by geographers, farmers, and town planners to track land use, urban growth, and

environmental changes.

3.1 Interpreting vertical aerial photographs

Aerial photographs are taken from an aircraft or drone looking down at the Earth’s surface.

They are valuable because they show real detail of natural and human features.

● Types of aerial photos:

○ Vertical photographs – camera points directly downwards, producing an almost

map-like view.

○ Oblique photographs – taken at an angle, showing a “perspective view” (often

used in tourism brochures).

For Grade 12 mapwork, the focus is on vertical aerial photographs because their scale is

more uniform.

Advantages of vertical aerial photographs:

● Show both natural features (rivers, valleys, mountains) and constructed features (roads,

buildings).

● Can be used to update maps.

● Help planners in agriculture, forestry, and urban development.

Limitations:

● Scale can still vary slightly from the centre to the edges.

● Landforms may be harder to recognise compared to maps with symbols.

Real-life Example

In KwaZulu-Natal, aerial photographs are used to monitor sugar cane plantations and

settlement expansion around Durban.

26

3.2 Identifying features on orthophoto maps

Orthophoto maps are aerial photographs that have been corrected for distortion and combined

with map information such as grid lines, contour lines, and place names. They combine the

realism of a photograph with the accuracy of a map.

Features you can identify on orthophoto maps:

● Physical features: hills, valleys, rivers, vegetation patterns.

● Constructed features: settlements, roads, railways, bridges, dams.

● Land use: farming fields, industrial zones, recreational areas like sports fields.

Real-life Example

Orthophoto maps of Johannesburg are used by urban planners to track urban sprawl and

identify informal settlements.

Tip

When reading an orthophoto, look for shape, size, texture, and shadow to identify features.

Unit 4: Geographical Information Systems (GIS)

Modern technology allows us to collect and analyse geographical data with great accuracy. In

this unit, you will explore how GIS uses maps, satellite images, and data to solve problems.

From tracking drought in Cape Town to mapping informal settlements in Johannesburg, GIS

plays a vital role in managing South Africa’s resources.

4.1 Examination of satellite images

Satellite images are taken by satellites orbiting the Earth. They provide detailed views of

physical and human features.

● Physical features: rivers, vegetation, land use patterns.

● Human features: cities, roads, farms, dams.

Real-life Examples

During the 2018 Cape Town drought, satellite images helped monitor water levels in major

dams such as Theewaterskloof.

After the KwaZulu-Natal floods of 2022, satellites tracked areas most affected to plan

disaster relief.

4.2 GIS concepts: remote sensing and resolution

Remote sensing is the collection of data about the Earth’s surface from a distance, usually via

satellites or aircraft.

Resolution is the detail in an image:

● High resolution = small features visible (e.g. individual houses).

● Low resolution = only large features visible (e.g. forests, cities).

Tip

High-resolution images are used for urban planning, while low-resolution images are used for

weather and climate studies.

4.3 Spatial and attribute data; vector and raster data

GIS works with two types of data:

● Spatial data – “Where?” (the location of features).

● Attribute data – “What?” (the characteristics of those features).

Data formats in GIS:

● Vector data: points (e.g. schools), lines (e.g. roads), polygons (e.g. farms).

● Raster data: made of pixels, like satellite images or aerial photos.

4.4 Data standardisation, sharing and security

GIS data must be consistent so it can be shared across organisations.

● Standardisation: making sure data uses the same format, scale, and projection.

● Data sharing: allows different departments (e.g. water, agriculture, transport) to use the

same information.

● Security: protecting sensitive data such as land ownership or population details.

Did You Know?

The South African National Space Agency (SANSA) provides GIS data for government

planning, disaster management, and environmental monitoring.

4.5 Data manipulation: integration, buffering, querying, statistical analysis

GIS is powerful because it can manipulate and analyse data.

● Integration – combining maps, images, and statistics.

● Buffering – drawing zones around features (e.g. a 1 km buffer around rivers to identify

flood-risk areas).

● Querying – asking questions (e.g. “Which farms are within 5 km of a water source?”).

● Statistical analysis – measuring, calculating, and comparing data (e.g. calculating

population density in Gauteng).

4.6 Applications of GIS to Grade 12 topics

GIS is applied in almost every field of Geography.

● Climatology: tracking cyclones in the Indian Ocean.

● Geomorphology: mapping catchment areas and soil erosion in Limpopo.

● Settlements: studying urban sprawl in Johannesburg.

● Economic Geography: analysing mining activity in Rustenburg.

● Tourism: mapping tourist routes in Cape Town’s Winelands.

3\. Layer the maps to see patterns and relationships.

Unit 5: Using Atlases

An atlas is more than a collection of maps—it is a tool for understanding patterns and

connections between people and the environment. In this unit, you will learn how to read

thematic maps and compare different maps to draw conclusions. These skills will help you make

sense of real issues such as population growth, rainfall patterns, and economic development in

South Africa.

5.1 Examining thematic maps

An atlas is a collection of maps that provide information about the world, regions, or specific

countries. Beyond political and physical maps, atlases contain thematic maps. These maps

show information about one specific theme or subject.

Types of thematic maps commonly found in atlases:

● Climate maps – rainfall and temperature patterns.

● Vegetation maps – types of plants found in an area.

● Population maps – distribution and density of people.

● Economic maps – agriculture, mining, industry.

● Transport maps – roads, railways, and air routes.

● Political maps – provinces, countries, and borders.

Real-life Examples

● A climate map of South Africa shows that the Western Cape has winter rainfall, while

most of the country receives summer rainfall.

● An economic map shows that Rustenburg in the North West is a major centre for

platinum mining.

Definition

Thematic Map: A map showing a specific type of information, such as rainfall, population, or

industries.

Tip

Always read the map title, legend (key), and scale carefully before interpreting a thematic

5.2 Comparing information from different maps

Geographers often need to compare two or more maps to understand relationships between

physical and human geography.

Steps to compare maps effectively:

1\. Look at the title of each map to see what it shows.

2\. Examine the scale to ensure fair comparison.

3\. Study the legend/key carefully.

4\. Identify patterns and correlations between maps.

Examples of comparisons using South African maps:

● Comparing a rainfall map with an agricultural map:

○ The Highveld (Gauteng, Mpumalanga, Free State) has high summer rainfall and

is a major maize-growing area.

● Comparing a population density map with a relief map:

○ Coastal plains (Durban, Cape Town) and Gauteng have high population

densities, while the arid Karoo has low density.

● Comparing a mining map with a transport map:

○ The Gauteng–Rustenburg area has many mines, supported by dense road and

rail networks.
