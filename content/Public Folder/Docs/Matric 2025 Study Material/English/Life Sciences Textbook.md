INTRODUCTION

Life Sciences is all around you. It explains the living world — from the DNA inside your cells

to the ecosystems that sustain life on Earth. It helps us understand how our bodies function,

how plants grow, how diseases spread, and how humans interact with the environment. In

simple

terms, Life Sciences is the study of life itself and the processes that make it possible.

By learning Life Sciences, you will:

● Understand how life works in real situations – You’ll explore how cells divide, how

the nervous system responds to stimuli, how reproduction ensures survival, and how

humans impact ecosystems.

● Make informed choices – Whether it’s making decisions about health, nutrition,

reproduction, or environmental conservation, Life Sciences equips you with knowledge

that helps you weigh up benefits and risks.

● Tackle global challenges – You will learn how to think critically about issues like

climate change, biodiversity loss, food security, and genetic engineering, and suggest

possible solutions.

● Prepare for exciting careers – From medicine and nursing to biotechnology,

agriculture, genetics, and environmental science, Life Sciences gives you skills and

knowledge that employers and universities value.

● Stay connected to your world – You’ll be able to make sense of news about health,

diseases, conservation, and scientific advances, and understand how they affect your

daily life.

Introduction

All living organisms share one remarkable feature: the ability to store, transmit, and use

information that determines their structure and function. This information is carried in DNA

(deoxyribonucleic acid), the molecule often called the “blueprint of life.” In this module, you will

explore the structure and role of DNA and RNA, and discover how these molecules control

every process in living organisms, from cell division to protein production.

You will learn how DNA was discovered through the work of scientists such as Watson, Crick,

Franklin, and Wilkins, and why their contributions are considered one of the greatest

achievements in biology. You will also study the process of DNA replication, which ensures that

genetic information is passed accurately from one generation of cells to the next, as well as

protein synthesis, which explains how genes are expressed in the form of proteins.

Finally, you will examine how DNA technology is applied in modern society through DNA

profiling, forensic investigations, paternity testing, and medical research. Alongside these

applications, you will also consider the ethical debates around biotechnology and genetic

testing, especially in the South African context.

By the end of this module, you will have a strong foundation in molecular biology, understand

how genetic information shapes life, and be prepared to apply this knowledge to real-world

issues and exam contexts.

Unit 1: Structure and Function of DNA and RNA

DNA and RNA are the two most important molecules in biology because they carry the

instructions for life. Without them, living organisms would not be able to grow, reproduce, or

survive. In this unit, you will learn how DNA is structured, why it is called the “blueprint of life,”

and how RNA helps in turning this blueprint into proteins that keep us alive. Understanding

these molecules also helps explain how scientists in South Africa use DNA in fields like

medicine, agriculture, and forensics.

1.1 Structure of DNA (double helix, nucleotides, base-pairing rules)

The structure of DNA was one of the greatest discoveries in biology. It explained how traits are

inherited and how life’s instructions are stored.

Key Features of DNA

Shape (Double Helix): DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder, called a double helix. The two

“sides” of the ladder are sugar-phosphate backbones, while the “rungs” are made of nitrogenous

base pairs.

Nucleotides: Each building block (nucleotide) is made up of:

● A sugar (deoxyribose)

● A phosphate group

● A nitrogenous base: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), or cytosine (C).

Figure 1: The basic structure of a nucleotide

Base-Pairing Rules:

● Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T) → 2 hydrogen bonds

● Guanine (G) pairs with Cytosine (C) → 3 hydrogen bonds

● This rule is called complementary base pairing.

Definition

Nucleotide: The basic building block of DNA or RNA, consisting of a sugar,

phosphate, and nitrogenous base.

Real-life Example

The South African National Blood Service uses DNA to ensure blood samples are safely

matched to recipients, reducing risks in transfusions.

1.2 Function of DNA as a carrier of genetic information

DNA is more than just a molecule. It acts as the instruction manual for building and maintaining

living organisms.

Functions of DNA

● Hereditary Material: DNA stores all the instructions that make an organism what it is.

These instructions are found in genes, which are small sections of DNA.

● Proteins: Genes carry codes for proteins, which control the body’s structure (muscle,

bone, hair) and function (enzymes, hormones).

● Inheritance: During reproduction, DNA is passed on from parents to offspring, ensuring

genetic continuity.

Definition

Gene: A sequence of DNA that codes for a specific protein.

Real-life Example

In KwaZulu-Natal, DNA is used to study genetic resistance to malaria, helping doctors

understand why some people are naturally protected against the disease.

1.3 RNA structure and types (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA)

While DNA stores information, RNA acts as a “messenger” and a “worker” that helps turn the

instructions into proteins.

Types of RNA and Their Roles

RNA Structure:

● Single-stranded, not double.

● Sugar is ribose, not deoxyribose.

● Contains Uracil (U) instead of Thymine (T).

14

Types of RNA:

● mRNA (messenger RNA): Carries a copy of the DNA code to the ribosome.

● tRNA (transfer RNA): Transports amino acids to the ribosome.

● rRNA (ribosomal RNA): Helps form the ribosome, the “factory” where

proteins are built.

1.4 Roles of RNA in protein synthesis

Proteins are essential for life. They are built using instructions from DNA, but RNA makes this

process possible.

Steps in Protein Synthesis

Protein synthesis happens in two steps:

1\. Transcription (in the nucleus):

● DNA unwinds.

● An mRNA copy is made of the gene.

● This mRNA leaves the nucleus with the genetic code.

2\. Translation (in the cytoplasm):

● Ribosomes read the mRNA codons (sets of 3 bases).

● tRNA brings the correct amino acids.

● The amino acids join to form a protein chain.

Real-life Example

South African researchers use protein synthesis studies to design vaccines, such as those

used for COVID-19.

Unit 2: History of DNA Discovery

Science is built on discoveries, teamwork, and sometimes even disagreements. The story of

DNA is no different. In this unit, you will explore how different scientists contributed to

uncovering the structure of DNA—the molecule that carries the code of life. Understanding this

history not only highlights the importance of collaboration in science but also raises questions

about recognition, fairness, and ethics in research. These lessons remain relevant in South

Africa today, as our scientists work together on genetic research, health care, and biodiversity

conservation.

2.1 Contributions of Watson and Crick

James Watson (an American biologist) and Francis Crick (a British physicist) are widely

recognised for building the first correct model of DNA in 1953.

● They proposed the double-helix structure, showing that DNA is made of two strands

twisted around each other.

● Their model explained how DNA replicates and how it can store information using

base-pairing rules.

● They worked at Cambridge University and used data produced by other scientists to

guide their model building.

Definition

Double Helix: The twisted ladder-like structure of DNA, where sugar-phosphate

backbones form the sides and base pairs form the rungs.

2.2 Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray crystallography

Rosalind Franklin was a chemist whose X-ray diffraction photographs provided crucial

evidence of DNA’s structure.

● She worked at King’s College, London, and produced the famous “Photograph 51”,

which clearly showed a helical pattern.

● Her precise measurements of DNA fibres allowed Watson and Crick to confirm that

DNA had two strands and a consistent width.

18

● Unfortunately, during her lifetime she did not receive the recognition she deserved for

her work.

Did You Know?

Franklin also conducted research on viruses such as the tobacco mosaic virus, showing that

her contributions extended beyond DNA.

2.3 Wilkins and the double-helix model

Maurice Wilkins worked in the same laboratory as Franklin.

● He used X-ray crystallography to study DNA structure and shared some of Franklin’s

findings (without her full consent) with Watson and Crick.

● His role was controversial, but his work still supported the development of the

double-helix model.

● Watson, Crick, and Wilkins were awarded the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or

Medicine. Sadly, Franklin had passed away in 1958 and was not eligible for the prize.

Ethical Note: This case highlights the importance of fairness and acknowledgement

in science—a topic that learners in South Africa should consider as part of

responsible scientific practice.

Unit 3: DNA Replication

DNA replication is the foundation of life. Every cell in your body contains DNA that must be

copied accurately whenever the cell divides. Without replication, living organisms could not

grow, repair damaged tissues, or reproduce. In this unit, you will learn how DNA replicates, the

enzymes involved, and why this process is crucial for the survival of all organisms. Replication

errors are rare, but when they occur, they can result in mutations, some of which may lead to

genetic disorders or diseases. Understanding DNA replication also helps explain biotechnology

applications, such as genetic testing and forensic science, which are important in South Africa

today.

3.1 Semi-conservative replication

Replication follows the semi-conservative model discovered by Meselson and Stahl in 1958.

Each new DNA molecule consists of:

● One original strand (conserved from the parent molecule).

● One newly synthesised strand (built during replication).

This ensures that genetic information remains consistent in every new cell.

Step-by-step process:

1\. The DNA double helix unwinds.

2\. Hydrogen bonds between bases break, separating the two strands.

3\. Each strand acts as a template for a new complementary strand.

4\. Nucleotides pair with exposed bases following base-pairing rules:

● Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T).

● Cytosine (C) pairs with Guanine (G).

5\. The result is two identical DNA molecules, each with one old and one new strand.

Definition

Semi-Conservative Replication: A process of DNA duplication where each daughter

molecule contains one parental and one new strand.

22

3.2 Enzymes involved (helicase, polymerase, ligase)

DNA replication requires specific enzymes that work like a team, each with a unique role:

● Helicase → Unwinds and unzips the double helix by breaking hydrogen bonds.

● DNA Polymerase → Adds complementary nucleotides to the exposed strands

and checks for errors.

● Ligase → Joins short DNA fragments (Okazaki fragments) on the lagging strand,

ensuring a continuous chain.

Enzyme Function Analogy

Tip

Mistakes in replication are very rare – about 1 in every 10 million nucleotides – because

DNA polymerase proofreads. Without this, mutations would be far more common.

3.3 Importance of replication in cell division

DNA replication is essential for:

● Growth: Ensures every new cell has the same DNA as the parent cell.

● Repair: Damaged or old cells are replaced with genetically identical cells.

● Reproduction: Gametes carry the correct genetic code for the next generation.

23

● Continuity: Maintains genetic stability across all cells of an organism.

Did You Know?

If the DNA from all your cells were stretched out, it would be about 2 times the distance from

Earth to the Sun – yet it fits inside your body thanks to DNA coiling and packaging.

Worked Example: Predicting Complementary Strands

A DNA template strand has the sequence:

5’ – ATG CCA TTA GCT – 3’

Using base-pairing rules, write the complementary strand.

Step 1: Match each base with its complementary partner:

● A → T

● T → A

● G → C

● C → G

Step 2: Write the complementary sequence:

3’ – TAC GGT AAT CGA – 5’

The new strand is complementary and antiparallel (runs in the opposite direction).

Application in South Africa: This same process is used in forensic DNA profiling during

crime investigations to match suspects to DNA found at a crime scene.

Unit 4: Protein Synthesis

Proteins are essential molecules that carry out most of the work in cells. From enzymes that

speed up chemical reactions, to haemoglobin that transports oxygen, proteins are vital for life.

The instructions for making proteins are stored in DNA, but the actual building process involves

several steps and molecules working together. In this unit, you will learn how the genetic code in

DNA is converted into functional proteins through the processes of transcription and translation.

You will also see how understanding protein synthesis is important for health, agriculture, and

biotechnology in South Africa.

4.1 Transcription (DNA → mRNA)

Transcription is the first step in protein synthesis. It occurs in the nucleus of the cell, where

DNA serves as a template for producing messenger RNA (mRNA).

Process of Transcription:

1\. The DNA double helix unwinds at the gene that codes for the required protein.

2\. One DNA strand (the template strand) is used to build an RNA copy.

3\. RNA nucleotides (A, U, C, G) pair with complementary DNA bases:

● A pairs with U (Uracil replaces T in RNA).

● T pairs with A.

● C pairs with G.

● G pairs with C.

4\. The enzyme RNA polymerase joins nucleotides to form an mRNA strand.

5\. The completed mRNA strand leaves the nucleus and moves to the ribosome in the

cytoplasm.

Definition

Transcription: The process of making an mRNA copy of DNA instructions for a

protein.

26

Figure 5: Transcription ProcessLife Sciences Grade 12 Textbook

4.2 Translation (mRNA → polypeptide chain)

Translation is the second step of protein synthesis. It happens at the ribosome, where the code

carried by mRNA is translated into a sequence of amino acids, forming a polypeptide (protein).

Process of Translation:

1\. The ribosome attaches to the mRNA strand.

2\. Each group of three bases (a codon) on the mRNA specifies one amino acid.

3\. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring amino acids to the ribosome. Each tRNA has an

anticodon that matches the mRNA codon.

4\. Amino acids are joined together in the correct sequence to form a polypeptide chain.

5\. The chain folds into a functional protein (e.g., an enzyme).

Definition

Translation: The process of assembling amino acids into a protein according to the

sequence of codons on mRNA.

Definition

Codon: A group of three bases on mRNA that codes for a specific amino acid.

4.3 Role of ribosomes and tRNA

Ribosomes are the workbenches of protein synthesis, ensuring codons are read accurately

and amino acids are linked in the correct order. tRNA acts as a courier, matching each codon

with its amino acid via a complementary anticodon. Together, they turn genetic code into a

specific polypeptide.

Ribosomes:

● Made of rRNA and protein; small and large subunits clamp onto mRNA.

● Move along mRNA, forming peptide bonds between amino acids.

tRNA:

● Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid.

● The anticodon base‑pairs with the mRNA codon to ensure correct placement.

Table: Functions of Protein Synthesis Components

Component Function

mRNA Carries the genetic code from DNA to the ribosome.

Ribosome Reads the mRNA codons and assembles amino acids.

tRNA Brings specific amino acids to match mRNA codons.

Amino acids Building blocks of proteins; linked to form polypeptide chains.

29

Definitions

Ribosome: The site of translation.

Anticodon: A three‑base tRNA sequence that pairs with an mRNA codon.

4.4 The genetic code and codons

The genetic code is the set of rules by which the sequence of bases in mRNA is converted into

amino acids. It is universal across all organisms.

Key Points:

● Codons are groups of three mRNA bases.

● Each codon codes for one amino acid (or a start/stop signal).

● There are 64 possible codons, but only 20 amino acids. This means several codons

can code for the same amino acid.

● AUG (Methionine) is the start codon. UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons.
