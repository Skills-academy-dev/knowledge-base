WELCOME

Economics is all around you. It’s in the price of your favourite snack, the job opportunities in

your town, and the decisions government makes about taxes and spending. In simple terms,

Economics is the study of how people, businesses, and governments make choices about using

limited resources to meet unlimited needs and wants.

By learning Economics, you will:

● See how the economy works in real life – You’ll explore how goods and services

move through markets, how money flows, and how trade connects South Africa to the

world.

● Make smart choices – Whether it’s deciding how to spend your allowance, starting a

business, or understanding a new policy, Economics will help you weigh up costs and

benefits.

● Tackle big problems – You will learn how to think critically about challenges like

unemployment, inflation, and inequality, and suggest possible solutions.

● Prepare for exciting careers – From finance and law to government and teaching,

Economics gives you skills that employers value.

● Stay informed – You’ll be able to make sense of economic news and understand how

local and global events affect your life.

Introduction

Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole. Instead of focusing on individual businesses

or consumers, it studies the big picture—how all the parts of the economy work together. In this

module, you will explore the flow of money, goods, and services between households,

businesses, government, and the rest of the world. You will also learn how we measure a

country’s economic performance using tools like GDP and other national accounts.

We will look at how spending and saving behaviour affects the economy through the multiplier,

By the end of this module, you will have a clear understanding of the key forces that drive the

Unit 1: The Circular Flow in an Open Economy

The economy is like a living system, where money, goods, and services flow continuously

between different groups. This movement is called the circular flow of income and spending. In

this unit, you will learn about the main participants in the economy, how goods, services, and

money move between them, and how markets connect these participants. You will also explore

the concepts of injections (money coming into the economy) and leakages (money leaving the

economy), and understand how South Africa interacts with the rest of the world. This knowledge

will help you see how your job, the shops in your town, the taxes you pay, and the goods you

buy from other countries are all linked.

1.1 Participants in the economy: households, firms, government, foreign sector

The economy has different groups of people and organisations that take part in production,

consumption, and trade. Each group plays an important role in keeping the circular flow moving.

The Four Main Participants:

1\. Households

● Owners of resources such as labour, land, and capital.

● Provide these resources to firms in exchange for wages, rent, interest, and

profits.

● Use income to buy goods and services from firms.

2\. Firms (Businesses)

● Use resources from households to produce goods and services.

● Sell goods and services to households, government, and foreign buyers.

● Pay households for resources used (wages, rent, etc.).

3\. Government

● Collects taxes from households and firms.

● Provides public goods and services like roads, hospitals, and education.

● Regulates the economy through laws and policies.

10

4\. Foreign Sector

● Includes trade and financial links with other countries. Exports

(goods sold abroad) bring money into the economy.

● Imports (goods bought from abroad) send money out of the economy.

Definition

Sector: A part of the economy made up of a specific group of participants, such as

households or businesses.

Real-Life Example

A South African citrus farmer (firm) exports oranges to Europe (foreign sector), hires workers

from the community (households), and pays tax to the government.

Did You Know?

South Africa is one of the largest exporters of citrus fruits in the world, sending millions of

tonnes overseas each year.

1.2 Real and money flows between sectors

In the circular flow, there are two types of movements: real flows and money flows. These

flows move in opposite directions but are linked.

Real Flows:

● Movement of goods and services (e.g. food, clothing) and factors of production (e.g.

labour, machinery).

Money Flows:

● Payments for goods, services, and resources (e.g. wages, rent, interest, profits).

Flow Relationship:

● Households provide resources to firms (real flow).

11

● Firms pay households for resources (money flow).

● Firms provide goods and services to households (real flow).

● Households pay firms for goods and services (money flow).

Definition

Factor of Production: A resource used to produce goods and services (land, labour, capital,

When you work in a clothing factory, your labour is part of the real flow, and your salary is part of

1.3 Injections and leakages

Not all income is spent inside the economy. Some money is added from outside sources, and

some is removed before it can be spent again.

12

Injections (Add Money into the Economy):

● Investment (I) – Businesses spend on new equipment, buildings, or technology.

● Government Spending (G) – Public projects like roads, schools, and hospitals.

● Exports (X) – Goods sold to foreign buyers bring money into the country.

Leakages (Remove Money from the Economy):

● Savings (S) – Money put in banks instead of being spent immediately.

● Taxes (T) – Paid to government, reducing household and business spending power.

● Imports (M) – Goods bought from other countries send money overseas.

Why It Matters:

If injections are greater than leakages, the economy grows. If leakages are greater than

injections, the economy slows down.

Example:

A South African construction company wins a contract to build housing (injection). A family buys

imported electronics from Japan (leakage).

1.4 Goods, factor, financial, and foreign exchange markets

Markets connect buyers and sellers. They can be physical places, like shops, or online

platforms. In the circular flow, different types of markets serve different purposes.

Types of Markets:

1\. Goods Market – Where final goods and services are bought and sold (e.g.

supermarkets, online stores).

2\. Factor Market – Where resources like labour, land, and capital are bought and sold

(e.g. job markets, rental agreements).

3\. Financial Market – Where money is borrowed and lent (e.g. banks, stock exchanges).

4\. Foreign Exchange Market (Forex) – Where different currencies are traded for

international transactions.

Example:

● The Johannesburg Stock Exchange (JSE) is part of the financial market.

● Currency exchange counters at airports are part of the foreign exchange market.

Definition:

Foreign Exchange Rate: The value of one currency compared to another.

Did You Know?

The Rand’s value changes daily based on global supply and demand for the currency.

Unit 2: National Accounts

To understand how well an economy is performing, economists collect and organise data into

national accounts. These accounts measure the total value of goods and services produced, the

income earned, and the spending that takes place in a country over a specific period. In this

unit, you will learn the meaning of GDP, GNI, and GDP per capita, how to calculate GDP using

different methods, and how to adjust figures to account for inflation. This knowledge will help

you read and interpret official statistics and understand economic reports in the news.

2.1 GDP, GNI, GDP per capita – definitions and differences

These three indicators are key measures of economic performance. Although they are related,

each tells a different story about the economy.

Key Definitions:

● Gross Domestic Product (GDP) – The total market value of all final goods and services

produced within a country’s borders in a specific period, usually a year.

● Gross National Income (GNI) – The total income earned by a country’s residents,

regardless of where the income is generated, including money received from abroad.

● GDP per Capita – GDP divided by the total population, showing the average economic

output or income per person.

Differences:

● GDP focuses on where the production takes place.

● GNI focuses on who earns the income, no matter where.

● GDP per capita adjusts GDP for population size, giving a measure of average living

standards.

Real-Life Example

A South African mining company operating in Botswana will have its profits counted in

Botswana’s GDP but in South Africa’s GNI, because the profits are sent back to South

Africa.

16

Worked Example – GDP per Capita:

If South Africa’s GDP = R6 trillion and the population = 60 million:

GDP per capita = R6 000 000 000 000 ÷ 60 000 000 = R100 000 per person.

Definition

Final goods: Goods that are ready for use by the consumer and will not be resold or used to

make another product.

Did You Know?

Some countries like Qatar have a high GDP per capita because of small populations and

high-value exports, while larger countries may have high GDP but lower GDP per capita.

2.2 Methods of calculation (production, income, expenditure)

GDP can be calculated in three main ways. Although each method looks at the economy from a

different perspective, all three should produce the same result because they measure the same

total activity.

1\. Production Method (Value Added Method)

● Adds up the value of all goods and services produced, subtracting the value of goods

used in production (intermediate goods).

● Focuses on what is produced.

Example: If a bakery buys flour for R1 000 and sells bread for R3 000, the value added is R2

000 (R3 000 – R1 000).

17

2\. Income Method

Adds up all incomes earned in the economy:

● Wages and salaries.

● Rent.

● Interest.

● Profits.

● Focuses on who earns the income.

Example: In a year, households earn R500 billion in wages, R50 billion in rent, R30 billion in

interest, and R70 billion in profits. GDP = R650 billion.

3\. Expenditure Method

Adds up all spending on final goods and services:

● Consumption (C) – Spending by households.

● Investment (I) – Spending by firms on capital goods.

● Government spending (G).

● Net exports (X – M) – Exports minus imports.

2.3 Price adjustments: nominal vs real values

To compare GDP over time, we must separate the effect of price changes from actual changes

in production. This is why economists use nominal and real GDP.

Key Definitions:

● Nominal GDP – GDP measured at current market prices, without adjusting for inflation.

● Real GDP – GDP adjusted for changes in prices, showing the true growth in output.

Why This Matters:

● If prices rise but production stays the same, nominal GDP increases while real GDP

remains unchanged.

● Real GDP is a better measure of whether the economy is actually producing more.

Worked Example:

If nominal GDP = R5 trillion in 2023 and R5.5 trillion in 2024, but inflation was 10%, real GDP

growth may be close to 0%.

19

Definition

Inflation: The general increase in prices over time.

Did You Know?

The South African Reserve Bank uses the Consumer Price Index (CPI) to adjust GDP from

nominal to real terms.

Unit 3: The Multiplier

When spending changes in an economy, the final effect on total income and output is often

much larger than the original change. This is called the multiplier effect. In this unit, you will

learn how the multiplier works, how to calculate it using different models, and how governments

use it to boost growth or control inflation. Understanding the multiplier helps explain why even

small injections of spending — such as building a new school or starting a new factory — can

create much bigger changes in jobs, production, and incomes across the economy.

3.1 Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) and save (MPS)

The size of the multiplier depends on what people do with additional income. If they spend

more, the multiplier is bigger. If they save more, the multiplier is smaller.

Key Definitions:

● Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) – The fraction of additional income that

households spend on goods and services.

● Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS) – The fraction of additional income that

households save instead of spending.

Leakage: Income that leaves the economy’s spending cycle, reducing the multiplier effect.

During economic uncertainty, South Africans often increase their savings, which can reduce

the impact of government spending programmes.

3.3 Policy applications

The multiplier effect is a powerful tool for governments when designing policies to stimulate

growth or control inflation.

How Governments Use the Multiplier:

1\. Boosting Growth (Expansionary Policy)

● Increase spending on infrastructure (roads, schools, hospitals).

● Provide tax cuts to households to increase disposable income.

● Offer subsidies to encourage business investment.

25

2\. Controlling Inflation (Contractionary Policy)

● Reduce government spending.

● Increase taxes to reduce household spending.

3\. Export Promotion

● Encourage industries to sell more goods abroad, increasing injections into the

economy.

Worked Example:

If the multiplier is 2.5 and the government spends an additional R800 million on public works:

Increase in GDP = 2.5 × R800 million = R2 billion.

Real-Life Example

During the COVID-19 recovery period, the South African government invested in public

infrastructure projects to create jobs, expecting the multiplier effect to spread benefits through

the economy.

Did You Know?

The multiplier effect works in reverse too — if spending is cut, the reduction in GDP can be

larger than the initial cut.

Unit 4: Business Cycles

Economic activity does not move in a straight line. Instead, it goes through periods of faster

growth and slower growth or decline. These ups and downs are called business cycles. In this

unit, you will explore the different phases of the business cycle, the causes of these fluctuations,

the various types of cycles identified by economists, and how the government responds to them.

By understanding business cycles, you will be able to interpret economic trends, predict

possible changes in employment and production, and understand why certain government

policies are introduced at specific times.

4.1 Phases of the business cycle

The business cycle shows the changes in economic activity over time. Each cycle goes through

four main phases, which are usually shown in a wave-like diagram.

Phases:

1\. Expansion (Recovery)

● Economic activity increases.

● Businesses produce more goods and services.

● Employment and consumer spending rise.

● Confidence among businesses and consumers is high.

2\. Peak● The economy reaches its highest point in the cycle.

● Production is at its maximum sustainable level.

● Inflationary pressures may start building.

3\. Contraction (Recession)

● Economic activity slows down.

● Production decreases, unemployment rises, and spending drops.

● Businesses may delay investments.

28

4\. Trough

● The economy reaches its lowest point before starting to recover.

● Low production and low spending levels.

● Often followed by policy measures to stimulate growth.

Features of Business Cycles:

● Recurrent but irregular – They repeat over time but not in fixed intervals.

● Linked to key indicators – GDP, employment, inflation, and investment

change with the cycle.

● Global influence – Events in other countries can affect South Africa’s cycle.

South Africa entered a recession in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by an

Definition

Recession: A period of at least two consecutive quarters (six months) of negative GDP

growth.

4.2 Causes: endogenous and exogenous factors

Business cycles are influenced by both internal and external factors. Economists refer to these

as endogenous (inside the economy) and exogenous (outside the economy) causes.

Endogenous (Internal) Causes:

● Changes in consumer and business confidence.

● Fluctuations in investment levels.

● Changes in interest rates and access to credit.

● Variations in stock levels in businesses.

Exogenous (External) Causes:

● Natural disasters such as droughts or floods.

● Global commodity price changes (e.g. oil, gold, platinum).

● Pandemics or wars affecting international trade.

● Political instability or changes in global economic conditions.

Example:

When the global price of platinum drops, South African mining companies earn less, production

slows, and job losses may occur, leading to a contraction phase.

Did You Know?

Because South Africa exports a lot of minerals, its economy is highly sensitive to changes in

global demand and prices.

4.3 Types of cycles (Kitchin, Juglar, Kuznets, Kondratieff)

Economists have identified business cycles of different lengths, each linked to specific causes

Table: Types of Cycles

Cycle Length Cause

Kitchin 3–5 years Inventory and stock adjustments in businesses

Juglar 7–11 years Investment in machinery and capital goods

Kuznets 15–25 years Changes in infrastructure and construction

Kondratieff 45–60 years Technological innovations and major changes

Details:

● Kitchin Cycle – Short-term, often linked to how quickly businesses adjust their

inventories to demand changes.

● Juglar Cycle – Medium-term, driven by investment booms and declines.

● Kuznets Cycle – Linked to large-scale projects like building transport systems and

housing.

● Kondratieff Cycle – Very long-term, often tied to new technologies such as the

industrial revolution or the digital age.

Example:

South Africa’s infrastructure boom before the 2010 FIFA World Cup is an example of a

Kuznets-type cycle, as it involved major construction and investment over several years.

Tip

In exams, you don’t need to memorise exact lengths but should know the general time frames

and causes.

4.4 Policy responses and new economic paradigm

Governments try to smooth out the extremes of business cycles to avoid deep recessions or

overheated expansions.

Policy Responses:

1\. Expansionary Policies (used during recessions)

● Increase government spending on projects.

● Lower taxes to boost disposable income.

● Reduce interest rates to encourage borrowing.

2\. Contractionary Policies (used during booms)

● Reduce government spending.

● Increase taxes to slow consumer spending.

● Raise interest rates to discourage excessive borrowing.

The New Economic Paradigm:

● Focuses on smoothing cycles to avoid extreme highs and lows.

● Balances demand-side policies (stimulating spending) with supply-side policies

(improving skills, technology, and infrastructure).

● Considers globalisation, technological change, and environmental sustainability as

key factors in long-term stability.

Example:

After the 2008 global financial crisis, the South African government used public infrastructure

investment to stimulate the economy, a classic example of expansionary policy.

Did You Know?

The Reserve Bank’s main tool for controlling the business cycle is adjusting the repo rate —

the interest rate at which it lends to commercial banks.

4.5 Forecasting Business Cycles

Forecasting predicts future economic trends using past and present data, helping governments,

businesses, and households make better decisions.

Key Features of Forecasting:

1\. Economic Indicators:

● Leading indicators – Predict changes before they happen (e.g. building plans

passed, share prices).

● Coincident indicators – Move with the economy (e.g. GDP, employment levels).

● Lagging indicators – Change after the economy shifts (e.g. unemployment after

recovery).
