INTRODUCTION

English is all around you. It is the language of learning in schools, the workplace, government,

media, and global communication. By studying English First Additional Language (FAL), you will

strengthen your ability to listen, speak, read, and write in English with confidence. In simple

terms, English FAL is about learning how to use the language clearly and effectively in different

contexts — whether for exams, further studies, or everyday life.

By learning English FAL, you will:

● Develop strong communication skills – Practise speaking and writing for different

purposes, from informal conversations and letters to formal essays, reports, and

speeches.

● Improve reading and comprehension – Learn strategies such as skimming, scanning,

and close reading to understand texts ranging from short stories and poetry to

advertisements and newspaper articles.

● Appreciate literature – Explore novels, plays, short stories, and poems that reflect

South African experiences and universal themes, and learn how writers use language

creatively.

● Master language structures – Build your knowledge of grammar, spelling, punctuation,

and sentence construction to write accurately and clearly.

● Prepare for your future – Gain skills such as critical thinking, summarising,

interpretation, and editing, which are valued in careers like teaching, journalism,

business, public service, and many more.

● Stay connected to your world – By developing your English, you will better understand

news, debates, and global discussions, and be able to express your own views

effectively.

Introduction

Language is made up of rules and patterns that help us communicate clearly and effectively. To

use English confidently, you need to understand how words, sentences, and punctuation work

together to create meaning. This module focuses on the building blocks of the language — parts

of speech, spelling, sentence construction, and punctuation. Mastering these skills will not only

help you in your examinations but also in your writing, speaking, and everyday communication.

In this module, you will start with the parts of speech, such as nouns, verbs, pronouns, and

conjunctions. These are the basic categories of words, each with its own role in a sentence. By

recognising and using them correctly, you will improve both your grammar and your ability to

express ideas with precision.

You will then move on to word formation and spelling. Here you will learn how to form plurals,

use abbreviations, and apply general spelling rules. Correct spelling is essential for clear

communication and shows attention to detail in your work.

The next unit deals with sentence construction. You will learn how to arrange words in the

correct order, how to use main and subordinate clauses, and how to apply different sentence

functions and moods. This will help you to write sentences that are clear, varied, and

grammatically accurate.

Finally, you will study punctuation. Punctuation marks such as full stops, commas, quotation

marks, colons, and brackets guide the reader and give meaning to your writing. Using

punctuation correctly can prevent misunderstandings and improve the flow of your sentences.

By the end of this module, you will have strengthened your understanding of the rules that form

the foundation of English. With practice, you will be able to apply these skills to your own writing

and communication, making your work more effective, polished, and professional.

Unit 1: Parts of Speech

In English, every word has a role to play. These roles are known as parts of speech. By

understanding them, you will be able to build correct sentences, avoid common errors, and

express yourself more clearly. This knowledge is also essential for exams because many

questions test your ability to identify and use the correct part of speech. In real life, these skills

help you when writing CVs, formal emails, social media posts, or reports.

Nouns are naming words. They give names to people, places, animals, things, and ideas.

Types of Nouns:

● Common nouns – general names (teacher, table, car).

● Proper nouns – specific names, always starting with a capital letter (Durban, Nelson

● Abstract nouns – names of feelings, ideas, or qualities (justice, hope, fear).

Tip

If you can place “the” or “a” before the word, it is usually a noun.

Pronouns replace nouns to prevent repetition and make sentences flow smoothly.

Types of Pronouns:

● Personal pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, we, they

● Possessive pronouns: mine, yours, theirs

● Relative pronouns: who, which, that

● Demonstrative pronouns: this, these, those

1.3 Verbs

Verbs are doing or being words. They tell us what someone does or the state of something.

Types of Verbs:

● Action verbs: run, eat, write

● Being verbs: is, are, was

● Auxiliary verbs (helping verbs): can, must, will, should

Examples:

● The learners write essays every week. (action)

● She is a nurse. (being)

● You must complete your homework. (auxiliary)

Did You Know?

Without verbs, a sentence cannot exist. Every complete sentence needs at least one verb.

1.4 Adverbs

Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. They often answer the questions How?

When? Where?

Examples:

● He spoke softly. (How?)

● We will meet tomorrow. (When?)

● She looked everywhere. (Where?)

TipMany adverbs end in -ly (quickly, slowly), but not all (fast, well).

1.5 Conjunctions

Conjunctions are joining words. They link words, phrases, or clauses.

Types:

● Coordinating conjunctions: and, but, or, yet, so

● Subordinating conjunctions: because, although, while, since, if

Examples:

● I wanted to watch TV, but I had to study.

● She stayed home because it was raining.

1.6 Prepositions

Prepositions show the relationship between words, often about place or time.

Examples:

● The bus stopped at the station.

● We are meeting after school.

● The books are on the desk.

1.7 Interjections

Interjections express sudden emotions or feelings.

Examples:

● Wow\! That was an amazing speech.

● Oh no\! I lost my phone.

1.8 Countable and Uncountable Words

Some nouns can be counted, and some cannot.

● Countable nouns can be counted (chairs, books, pens).

● Uncountable nouns cannot be counted (sugar, water, advice).

Examples:

● I bought three oranges at the market. (countable)

● There is too much traffic in Pretoria. (uncountable)

1.9 Modal Verbs

Modal verbs are helping verbs that show possibility, ability, permission, or necessity.

Examples:

● can, may, must, should, might, will

Examples in context

● You must wear your uniform. (necessity)

● She can speak three languages. (ability)

● May I sit here? (permission)

1.10 Question Words

Question words are used to ask for information.

Examples:

● What is your name?

● Where are you going?

● Why are you late?

14

1.11 Relative pronouns

Relative pronouns link a clause to a noun.

Examples:

● The learner who answered correctly won a prize.

● This is the book that I borrowed from the library.

Pronouns can be used in different roles:

● Subject pronoun: She is reading.

● Object pronoun: I saw her at the taxi rank.

● Possessive pronoun: That bag is mine.

1.13 Intensive Words

Intensive pronouns add emphasis.

Examples:

● I myself spoke to the teacher.

● The President himself attended the event.

1.14 Onomatopoeia

Onomatopoeia refers to words that imitate natural sounds.

Examples:

● The bee buzzed in the garden.

● The thunder boomed during the storm.

1.15 Proper Nouns

Proper nouns are specific names of people, places, or organisations. They always begin with a

capital letter.

Examples:

● South Africa, Nelson Mandela, Durban, Vodacom

2.3 General Spelling Rules

English spelling may seem tricky, but there are clear patterns that can help you.

Rule 1: Doubling Consonants

If a one-syllable word ends in a vowel + consonant, double the consonant before adding –ing or

–ed.

21

● run → running

● sit → sitting

But: Do not double if the word ends in two consonants.

● jump → jumping

Rule 2: Drop the Silent –e

When adding a suffix beginning with a vowel (–ing, –ed, –er), drop the final –e.

● make → making

● hope → hoping

Keep the –e if the suffix begins with a consonant.

● hope → hopeful

Rule 3: Change y to i

If a word ends in consonant + y, change y to i before adding suffixes.

● baby → babies

● happy → happier → happiness

But: Keep the y when adding –ing.

● study → studying

Rule 4: “i before e except after c”

● Correct: believe, chief, field

● After c: receive, deceive

● Exceptions: science, neighbour, weird

Rule 5: Prefixes and Suffixes

Adding prefixes (un–, re–, pre–, dis–) does not change the root spelling.

● happy → unhappy

● do → redo

Suffixes may change spelling:

● care → careful

● happy → happiness

Unit 3: Sentence Construction

A sentence is the basic building block of communication. Without correct sentence construction,

writing and speaking can be confusing or unclear. This unit will help you understand how to

place words in the right order, how to use clauses, and how to recognise different types and

moods of sentences. By the end of this unit, you will be able to build accurate sentences that

improve your essays, transactional writing, and exam answers. These skills are also valuable in

daily life — whether you are writing an SMS, an email, or a job application.

Figure 5: Sentence construction process(AI-Generated)

3.1 Word Order in Sentences

In English, meaning often depends on word order. Unlike some African languages that allow

flexible structures, English requires a fixed sequence: Subject → Verb → Object.

Examples:

● Thandi (subject) reads (verb) the newspaper (object).

● The learners (subject) wrote (verb) their essays (object).

Questions

In questions, the auxiliary verb often comes before the subject.

● Are you ready for class?

● Do they understand the lesson?

25

Negatives

Negative sentences place not or do not after the verb or auxiliary verb.

● She is not tired.

● They do not enjoy the film.

Definition

Word order: The sequence in which words appear in a sentence to make meaning clear.

Tip

In exams, if a sentence sounds wrong when you say it aloud, the word order is usually

incorrect.

3.2 Main Clauses

A main clause contains a subject and a verb and expresses a complete thought. It can stand

alone as a sentence.

Examples:

● The children are playing.

● We wrote our homework.

Combining Main Clauses

Two main clauses can be joined by coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, so).

● The learners wrote the test, and they passed.

● She wanted to stay, but it was late.

3.3 Subordinate Clauses

A subordinate clause also has a subject and verb but cannot stand alone. It adds extra

information to the main clause.

26

Examples:

● Because it was raining, the soccer match was cancelled.

● I will call you when I arrive home.

How to Identify

Look for connecting words such as because, if, when, since, although, while.

Table: Main vs Subordinate Clauses

Main Clause (complete thought) Subordinate Clause (incomplete thought)

The learners were excited. because they won the debate.

We stayed indoors. since it was cold outside.

Tip

If the sentence feels incomplete, it is a subordinate clause.

3.5 Sentence Mood

Sentence mood shows the attitude of the speaker or writer. It tells us how the message is

delivered.

Types of Mood

● Indicative mood: States a fact.

○ The learners are writing an exam.

● Interrogative mood: Asks a question.

○ Are they ready for the exam?

● Imperative mood: Gives an instruction or command.

○ Study hard for your exam.

● Conditional mood: Shows something that depends on a condition.

○ If you work hard, you will pass.

● Subjunctive mood: Expresses a wish or something not real.

○ I wish I were at the beach today.

Worked Example

Sentence:

"If the minibus taxi arrives late, the learners will miss assembly."

● If the minibus taxi arrives late = Subordinate clause.

● The learners will miss assembly = Main clause.

● The word order is Subject → Verb → Object.

● The sentence is in a conditional mood.

Unit 4: Punctuation

Punctuation gives structure to writing. Without it, words would run together and cause

confusion. Just as traffic signs control the flow of cars on the road, punctuation marks guide the

reader through your sentences. In this unit, you will learn how to use punctuation marks

effectively: from basic marks like the full stop and comma to more advanced ones like the colon,

semi-colon, and dash. Correct punctuation will not only help you perform well in exams but will

also make your everyday writing, such as emails, notices, and WhatsApp messages, clearer

and more professional.

4.1 Full Stop, Comma, Question Mark, Exclamation Mark

These are the most common punctuation marks. They signal the end of thoughts, pauses,

questions, and strong emotions. Mastering them ensures your sentences are easy to follow.

● Full Stop (.)

Used to end a statement.

○ South Africa has 11 official languages.

32

● Comma (,)

Shows a pause, separates items in a list, or sets off extra information.

○ I bought apples, bananas, and oranges.

○ After the meeting, the learners went home.

● Question Mark (?)

Used at the end of a question.

○ When is the exam?

● Exclamation Mark (\!)

Expresses surprise, excitement, or urgency.

○ Wow\! That was amazing.

○ Stop\!

Tip

Do not use a question mark and an exclamation mark together in formal writing (e.g. What?\!).

Stick to one.

4.2 Apostrophe and Possessives

The apostrophe is often misused, but it plays two important roles: showing missing letters

(contractions) and showing ownership (possessives).

● Contractions (letters left out)

○ don’t = do not

○ it’s = it is

● Possession (ownership)

○ The girl’s book (one girl owns it)

○ The girls’ books (many girls own them)

Common Mistake:

● its = belonging to it (The dog wagged its tail.)

● it’s = it is (It’s raining today.)

4.3 Quotation Marks and Dialogue Punctuation

Quotation marks are used to show the exact words someone says. They are essential in

dialogue, stories, and direct speech.

● Basic Rule: Place the spoken words inside quotation marks.

○ She said, “I am going home.”

● Punctuation Inside Quotes:

○ “Where are you going?” asked Sipho.

○ “I will call you later,” said Lerato.

● South African Example:

4.4 Colon, Semi-colon, Dash and Brackets

These punctuation marks are more advanced. They help you connect ideas, explain, or add

extra information.

● Colon (:)

Introduces a list, explanation, or quotation.

○ You must bring the following: ID, exam slip, and stationery.

● Semi-colon (;)

Joins two closely related sentences without using and.

○ Some learners enjoy maths; others prefer languages.

● Dash (—)

Creates emphasis or sets off information.

○ The learners—tired but excited—finished the exam.

● Brackets ( )

Add extra information.

○ Nelson Mandela (1918–2013) was the first democratic president.

Did You Know?

The dash (—) is longer than a hyphen (-). They are not the same\!

4.5 Punctuation in Complex Sentences

Complex sentences often need punctuation to separate clauses and avoid confusion. The

placement of a comma depends on the order of clauses.

● Subordinate Clause First: Use a comma.

○ Although it was raining, the learners played soccer.

● Main Clause First: No comma needed.

○ The learners played soccer although it was raining.
